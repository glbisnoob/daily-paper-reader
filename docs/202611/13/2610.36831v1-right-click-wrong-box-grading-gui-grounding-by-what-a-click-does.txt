# Abstract 

Leading GUI grounders score 93 to 96% on ScreenSpot-v2, and reported gaps between new and prior models are about a point. At this level the useful question is what the remaining errors are. Benchmarks count a click as correct only inside one annotated box, but interfaces often expose one action through several elements: a duplicated link, the label of a checkbox, the title next to a logo. The box is not wrong. The rule is incomplete. We call a click correct when executing it produces the same outcome as clicking the annotated target. A probe that executes and fingerprints about a thousand clicks per page turns this definition into outcome maps and an execution judge for live web applications, with no new annotation. We execute the predictions of three strong 7–8B grounders on a code forge and an online shop. About one in five and one in three of the clicks that box scoring rejects reach the intended outcome, and the shares remain one in seven and one in three when the instructions are rewritten as natural tasks. A blind audit of 11 models on ScreenSpot-v2 (three 7–8B grounders and eight 3B variants) finds the same effect on web screenshots, with two independent reviewers on a 60-unit subset. The share is highest for the strongest grounders. Training seeds and test-set sampling add noise that more runs and paired intervals control. Box scoring instead under-counts correct clicks, which repetition does not remove, and the audit-based correction of 0.2 to 1.8 points on ScreenSpot-v2 is as large as the gaps the field reports. The correction is needed in evaluation. In our 3B GRPO study, crediting equivalent clicks in the reward gives no stable gain over box rewards across three seeds and four test sets. We release the probe, the outcome maps, the audit and every prediction. 

# 1. Introduction 

GUI agents act through clicks, so grounding an instruction to a screen location is the perception step every agent depends on. The strongest open grounders we evaluate score 93 to 96% on ScreenSpot-v2 [28] (Sec. 4), and across 29 recent grounding papers the median absolute gap between a new model and the strongest prior model of its size is 1.15 points. At this level the useful question is no longer how many clicks miss the box. It is what the remaining misses are. When the remaining mistakes of top ImageNet models were reviewed 1arXiv:2610.36831v1 [cs.CV] 27 Oct 2026 one by one, nearly half turned out not to be mistakes [25]. Grounding benchmarks score a predicted point with one rule. It is correct when it falls inside the annotated box of the target element [7, 9, 17, 28, 30], and reinforcement learning for grounding uses the same rule as its reward [24, 33]. The rule assumes that each instruction has one correct region. Interfaces are built the other way. A commit list links every author name to the same profile, a form label focuses its control, and a shop shows a product’s name in the breadcrumb and in the title, both linking to the product (Fig. 1). Public screenshots show the same pattern (Fig. 3). None of this is an annotation mistake. The box is not wrong. The rule is incomplete, because a click outside the box can do exactly what the instruction asks. The converse also happens, and parts of an annotated box can be inert. We define correctness by what a click does. Given an interface state and an annotated target, a click is outcome-correct when executing it produces the same outcome as clicking the target. A probe executes a click in a browser, intercepts every request that would change server state, and records a fingerprint of the result. Running the probe densely over a page yields an outcome map , the set of pixels that achieve each element’s outcome. Running it on model pre-dictions yields an execution judge . Neither needs annotation beyond the existing box. For public screenshot benchmarks, where nothing can be executed, we run a blind audit with the same definition. 

What the remaining errors are. On a self-hosted Gitea code forge and a Django Oscar shop, 17.7% and 31.2% of live targets have pixels outside the box that reach the same outcome or pixels inside it that do nothing. Executing the predictions of UI-Venus-1.5-8B, GTA1-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B shows that about one in five clicks rejected by box scoring is outcome-correct on Gitea, and about one in three on Oscar. Rewriting the instructions as natural tasks keeps these shares at 14.3% and 34.8%. On ScreenSpot-v2, a blind audit of 11 models, cross-checked by two independent reviewers, finds 6.4% of rejected clicks outcome-correct overall and 14.4% on web screenshots. Among the audited models the share is highest for the strongest grounders, so box scoring misreads more of the errors that still separate strong grounders than of the errors of weaker models. 

Bias, not noise. Another training seed moves ScreenSpot-v2 accuracy by up to 2.6 points, and test-set sampling limits paired comparisons of 7–8B models to about 1.4 points. Both are noise that more seeds and a paired interval control, yet none of the 29 papers reports run-to-run variation on ScreenSpot-v2. Box scoring adds a bias, a one-sided under-count of the correct clicks of every model. It is estimated at 0.2 to 1.8 points on ScreenSpot-v2, and no number of runs or test items removes it. That is the size of the gaps the field reports, and the part that differs between models changes the point-estimate order of models within a point of each other. 

Where the fix belongs. In our 3B study, a GRPO reward that credits the equivalent clicks found by the probe brings no stable gain over box rewards across three seeds and four test sets. Trained models already reach the target outcome on every held-out item whose target has an exact duplicate, so the correction is needed in evaluation, while its value as a training reward remains unshown at this scale. Our contributions are: • A definition of outcome-defined correctness for GUI grounding, and a probe that turns it into outcome maps and an execution judge for live web applications without new annotation. • An audit of what box scoring rejects on two live applica-tions and two public benchmarks, with execution judging of strong grounders and a reviewed screenshot audit of 11 models. • An error budget for one-point comparisons on ScreenSpot-v2 that separates the scoring bias from seed and sampling noise, with reporting recommendations. • A controlled training study that places the fix in evaluation rather than in the reward. All maps, judgments, predictions and protocols are released. 

# 2. Related Work 

GUI grounding benchmarks and models. ScreenSpot [7] established the screenshot grounding task across mobile, desktop and web, and ScreenSpot-v2 [28] re-annotated 11.3% of its samples to fix wrong boxes and ambiguous in-structions. ScreenSpot-Pro [17] raises resolution and domain difficulty, OSWorld-G [30] adds fine-grained annotations, and Mind2Web [9] provides real web pages with element-level ground truth. All of them score a prediction by its position relative to one annotated region. Grounding mod-els have grown from supervised pretraining on large GUI corpora [7, 12, 18, 21, 28, 31] to coordinate-free heads [27], data-centric recipes [34] and reinforcement learning, as in GTA1 [33], GUI-G 2 [24] and UI-Venus [26]. They and gen-eral models such as Qwen-VL [2, 3] report results under this protocol. 

Rewards for grounding. GRPO [23] with a point-in-box reward is the standard recipe for RL-trained grounders [33], and GUI-G 2 replaces the binary hit with a Gaussian reward around the box centre [24]. GUI-Libra [32] observes that offline GUI rewards are only partially verifiable, since a zero reward may hide an alternative valid action, and proposes KL regularisation and success-adaptive scaling of negative advantages, studied mainly for multi-step navigation. We measure how often such alternative actions exist in single-step grounding, give the reward access to those the probe finds, and train GUI-Libra alongside. 

Multiple valid actions. MobiBench [15] annotates sev-eral valid actions per step for mobile agents and shows that single-path evaluation underestimates task success. Concur-2rent work, ConClick [35], studies grounding errors that land on non-target elements and uses a vision-language judge to estimate that at most 6% of them are valid alternatives. Its sample and categories differ from ours, so the comparison is only broad. Our ScreenSpot-v2 audit adds three things: hu-man judgments with measured agreement, a breakdown by platform that reveals a much larger share on the web, and ex-ecution judging on two live applications where equivalence is observed directly. 

Execution-based evaluation. AutoGUI [16] executes interactions and asks an LLM to describe each element’s function from the change on screen, to create training data. We compare outcomes of clicks to grade predictions. We-bArena [36] and OSWorld [29] judge agents by the state they reach at the end of a task. We bring the same principle down to a single click, which makes it usable for grounding benchmarks and grounding rewards. 

Auditing benchmarks. Re-examining test sets has re-peatedly changed how the vision community reads its leader-boards, from ImageNet re-labelling and replication [4, 22] to systematic label-error audits [20]. A review of every remain-ing mistake of top ImageNet models found that nearly half were not mistakes [25], and Boundary IoU showed that the standard mask measure overlooks improvements in bound-ary quality [6]. Work on benchmark variance [1, 5, 10, 13] shows that seeds and test-set sampling can rival the effects being claimed. In GUI grounding, the re-annotation behind ScreenSpot-v2 corrected individual labels. We keep the la-bels and audit the grading rule, with ground truth obtained by execution wherever the interface can run, and we measure seed and sampling variance alongside it. 

# 3. Outcome-Defined Correctness 

3.1. Definition 

Let s be an interface state, p a screen point and o(s, p ) the outcome of clicking p in state s. For an annotated target with box b, the target outcome ob is the most frequent non-null outcome over points inside b, with the box centre breaking ties. A click is outcome-correct when o(s, p ) = ob, and the outcome-equivalent region is E(s, b ) = {p : o(s, p ) = ob}.Box scoring replaces E(s, b ) with b. The two disagree in two ways. Points in E(s, b ) \ b are correct clicks that box scoring rejects, and points in b\E(s, b ) are inert clicks that it accepts. Outcomes are defined relative to the state s, which is fixed by the screenshot or page being evaluated. On live pages, o(s, p ) is the operational fingerprint of Sec. 3.2. 

3.2. Executing and fingerprinting a click 

The probe (Fig. 2) runs Chromium at 1280 × 720 . For each point it hovers for 120 ms, records the page state, clicks, waits until requests issued by the click have settled (at least 250 ms and at most 1.5 s beyond that, ignoring long-lived

streaming connections), and records the state again. The outcome is the first matching signature in a fixed order: main-frame navigation (method, normalised URL and form fields), a new window, a non-GET request, a dialog, a change in the visible page, or no effect. Navigations and non-GET requests are intercepted and aborted, so the server state never changes and every click starts from the same page. Hover effects cancel out because both snapshots include them, and nodes that change on their own, such as clocks, are found from idle snapshots and excluded. A text field gaining focus counts as an outcome, and other elements gaining focus do not. After any click with an effect the page is reloaded. 

3.3. Outcome maps 

For every visible interactive element the probe clicks a 3 × 3

grid inside the box and one point 3 px outside each edge, and it adds a 48 px grid over the whole viewport (at most 1,500 points per page). Elements whose box contains no effective point are dead and set aside. For each live target we record whether a different element reaches its outcome (a distinct equivalent , such as a duplicated link or a label), whether at least 10% of its box is inert, and whether clicks just outside the box still reach its outcome. A target disagrees with box scoring if any of the three holds. A random 10% of points on every page is re-probed from a fresh load to measure repeatability. We probe two self-hosted applications with different in-teraction structure, both seeded deterministically. Gitea 1.25 is a code forge similar to the GitLab site of WebArena [36], with users, organisations, repositories, issues, pull requests and wikis (94 pages). Django Oscar 4.2 is an open-source e-commerce framework, close to the shopping site of We-bArena, with a storefront of product grids, categories, re-views, basket and account pages and a staff dashboard of tables and forms (60 pages). On Oscar an application-side middleware rolls back every database transaction, so that page views leave no trace. Both databases are identical before and after probing. 33.4. Execution judging 

Each live target with visible text becomes a grounding item with the instruction template Click the <role> "<text>" , up to 40 items per page. We execute each model’s predicted point with the same probe and compare its outcome with the target outcome. As a reference we also execute the centre of every annotated box. The centre reproduces the mapped outcome on 98.1% of items, which bounds environment drift between mapping and judging. Confidence intervals resample pages [11]. 

3.5. Screenshot audit 

Public benchmarks ship screenshots, so outcomes cannot be executed. For ScreenSpot-v2 we use human judgment with the same definition. We take the existing predictions of 11 models and keep the parsed clicks that miss the box. Clicks on the same item that lie within 0.8% of the long image side are merged, giving 1,036 audit units. A fixed random order selects a 40-unit pilot and a 360-unit main sample. Each unit is shown as a panel with the full screenshot and a zoomed crop, the target box in red and the click in blue, together with the instruction. Model names are hidden. A unit is E-same 

when the click hits the target control outside its box (the box covers only an icon of a larger button), E-dup when it hits another control with the same function (a duplicated link, the label of a checkbox or field), N when the function differs, and U when the screenshot does not show enough to decide. Clear conventions are required to accept a whole row or card as clickable, and uncertain cases go to U. Two independent reviewers judge a random 60-unit subset of the main sample without seeing our labels. All decision rules, thresholds and sampling seeds were recorded with timestamps and checksums before the data they govern was produced. The supplementary material lists the rule files behind every analysis in this paper and each later amendment, together with what had been seen when it was written.

4.3. Public benchmarks 

Mind2Web. The released Mind2Web pages strip almost all element attributes, link targets included, so equivalence can only be recovered from label relations. Such static equiva-lents are visible for 1.3% of the 4,838 click steps we evaluate. Box and outcome accuracy of the three grounders therefore differ by at most 0.26 points. Where an equivalent is visible the effect is large. With task-level instructions, outcome accuracy on these steps exceeds box accuracy by 12.6 to 20.3 points. Duplicated links, the largest group on Gitea, are invisible in such snapshots, which is why live execution is needed to measure them. 

ScreenSpot-v2. The screenshot audit covers 11 models: the three grounders above, Qwen2.5-VL-3B [3] and seven GRPO-trained 3B variants from Sec. 5. A 40-unit pilot decided that the full audit was worthwhile, and the 360-unit main sample gives the estimates in Tab. 4. Two independent reviewers agree closely with each other and with our labels (Tab. 1). Where both reviewers disagreed with us, on 2 of 60 units, we adopted their label. Outcome-correct clicks make up 6.4% of the rejected clicks. On web screenshots the share is 14.4%, and mobile and desktop screenshots show about 1%. # 5. Where the Fix Belongs 

Outcome maps also give a reward that credits the equivalent clicks they find. GUI-Libra [32] argues that box rewards are only partially verifiable and that the missing credit hurts training. If that held at our scale, the correction would belong in the reward as well as in evaluation. GUI-Libra studies multi-step navigation; we test whether the same ambiguity costs anything when training single-step grounding. 

Setup. We split the Gitea items by page layout, giving 1,745 training and 1,841 held-out items from layouts never seen in training. Items that the base model answers inside the box in all 8 samples carry no GRPO gradient under any re-ward and are removed for all arms. This leaves 1,589 training items, and 313 of them have at least one outcome-equivalent element. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct [3] with LoRA [14] using the GRPO implementation of GTA1 [33], with 8 samples per item for 2 epochs (396 updates). Three arms differ only in the reward. Box rewards clicks inside the target box. Outcome rewards clicks inside the box of the target or of any outcome-equivalent element found by the out-come maps. This stand-in for executing every rollout click agrees with the execution judge on 98.6% of 14,727 held-out clicks of the eight 3B models, and only 5 outcome-correct clicks fall outside every rewarded box. GUI-Libra uses the box reward with KL regularisation ( β = 0 .001 ) and success-adaptive scaling of negative advantages ( λ0 = κ = 0 .5). All arms share data, order and hyperparameters. Results. Every arm improves on the base model by 6–7 points on held-out Gitea layouts, by 4–6 points on ScreenSpot-v2 and by about 3 points on Mind2Web (Tab. 5). On the unseen shop every arm ends 0.8 to 1.4 points below a strong base model, so this training does not transfer there. The reward definition makes no stable difference. Averaged over the three seeds, outcome and box rewards lie within a quarter of a point of each other on every test set, and indi-vidual seeds point in both directions (Fig. 5a). Taking each training run as one observation, the three seeds bound the dif-ference to about one point on held-out Gitea and Mind2Web. On ScreenSpot-v2 the seed spread is too large to bound it this tightly (Sec. 6). GUI-Libra lands in the same range, within half a point of the box reward on average on every test set. 

Why the extra credit adds little. The two rewards differ only on items with an equivalent element, about a fifth of the training items. On the 96 held-out items whose instruction matches several elements with the same outcome, the base model already reaches the target outcome on 92%. Every trained model of every arm and seed reaches 100%, while picking the annotated copy only 27–29% of the time. The box reward on these items acts as label noise that averages out. At this scale the richer reward has almost nothing left to improve, which is a ceiling of the test rather than proof that such a reward can never help. On the shop, every model, the base model included, reaches the target outcome on all 55 items with same-outcome copies. 

# 6. Bias and Noise at the One-Point Scale 

Three things move a close comparison on ScreenSpot-v2: the training seed, the test sample and the scoring rule (Fig. 6). The first two are noise. More seeds average them out, and a paired interval bounds what remains. The third is a bias, a one-sided under-count of correct clicks. It belongs to the model and the benchmark, so repeated runs and more items from the same benchmark leave it in place. 

Seeds. Retraining the same recipe with three seeds changes ScreenSpot-v2 accuracy by up to 1.3 points for box rewards and up to 2.6 points for outcome rewards. The same models vary by less than a point on Mind2Web and on held-out Gitea layouts. With seed 42 the outcome reward wins on ScreenSpot-v2 with a paired McNemar test [19] at 

p = 0 .03 , and with seed 44 the box reward wins at p = 0 .02 

(Fig. 5a). A single-seed significance test is a statement about one training run and says little about the method. 

Test-set sampling. Even a fixed model is measured on a finite sample of 1,272 items. We resampled ScreenSpot-v2 items 10,000 times and computed paired intervals for the three strong grounders, the size class most papers compete in. Reported margins. Two independent literature searches, each coded with fixed rules and checked against the origi-nal tables, together cover 29 recent grounding papers that report ScreenSpot-v2, 28 of which contain a head-to-head comparison. Gains over the untrained base model are larger, with a median of 4.0 points, so the field’s progress is real and visible at that scale. No paper reports run-to-run variation on ScreenSpot-v2. All three sources act at the scale of the reported margins. Several seeds and a paired interval handle the noise. Only a rule that credits equivalent clicks removes the bias. 

# 7. Recommendations and Outlook 

Recommendations. Our measurements translate into two practices for evaluation that are cheap to adopt. 1. Credit equivalent clicks on the web, and report both scores. On web interfaces, between one in seven and one in three of the clicks that box scoring rejects do the right thing, and for the strongest grounders this is about a fifth of their remaining ScreenSpot-v2 errors. Benchmarks can ship outcome maps or lists of equivalent elements with each web item. Reporting outcome-corrected accu-racy next to box accuracy shows how much of a margin depends on the choice between equivalent targets. 2. Report seeds and paired intervals. Three seeds and a paired bootstrap interval cost little compared with train-ing, and they turn a 1-point margin from an anecdote into evidence. In our 3B study, box labels remained an adequate training target (Sec. 5). 

Where equivalence comes from. The difference be-tween web and native screenshots follows from how the two are built. Web pages routinely expose one action through several elements: the image and title of a card, a label bound to its control, a link repeated in a header and a page body. Native mobile and desktop widgets usually have a single hit target, so a click outside the box is almost always a real error, and the audit finds about 1% there against 14.4% on web screenshots. Lists of equivalent elements therefore matter most for web items. 

Scope. Execution judging covers two live web applica-tions, a code forge and an online shop that stand in for the GitLab and shopping sites of WebArena, and the screen-shot audit extends the picture to public mobile, desktop and web data. The training study uses a 3B model with LoRA and about 400 GRPO updates, and larger models or longer schedules may use the extra credit differently. Equivalence is defined for one interface state, which matches how ground-ing benchmarks are built. The released probe is designed to extend to more applications, mobile platforms and multi-step tasks. 

Conclusion. A click is right when it does the right thing. Graded this way, many of the errors that still separate strong grounders on the web are correct clicks. This under-count does not average out, and it is as large as the gaps the field now reports. Outcome maps and execution judging remove it from evaluation. 8References

# E. Training Details 

Recipe. GTA1 GRPO (commit 81f0c4c ) with gradient checkpointing, a shared coordinate parser for reward and evaluation, and a chunked vision attention patch to fit 24 GB GPUs. LoRA has rank 16 and α = 32 on the attention and MLP projections, with the vision modules frozen. Learning rate 2 × 10 −5 with 5% warm-up and linear decay, 8 items per update on 8 GPUs, 8 samples per item at temperature 1, at most 64 new tokens, images up to 1280 × 720 . Each arm takes about one hour on eight RTX 4090 GPUs. GUI-Libra does not give values for λ0 and κ, and we use 0.5 for both. 

Decision rules. The first study (seed 42) asked whether the outcome reward raises outcome accuracy on held-out Gitea layouts, with a guard against losing more than one point on ScreenSpot-v2. The difference was −0.60 points [−1.09 , −0.06 ], so the outcome reward gave no gain. Seed 42 did show a +1 .42 point difference on ScreenSpot-v2, which motivated a confirmation with seeds 43 and 44 on ScreenSpot-v2 and Mind2Web. # G. Literature Coding 

We collected papers that report ScreenSpot-v2 grounding accuracy and coded three kinds of comparison: the proposed model against the strongest prior model of the same size reported in the same table (head-to-head), against its own untrained base model, and ablations.
