Zhejiang University 4Ant Group With the rise of agentic reasoning, visual perception is evolving from passive recognition toward active, task-oriented observation. Active visual perception requires an agent to decide not only what to recognize, but also where to look, how much context to inspect, and when further observation is worthwhile. We explore GUI grounding as a scalable source of spatially grounded supervision for learning such observation behaviors, with the goal of transferring them beyond interface-centric tasks. To this end, we introduce LBS ( Look Beyond 

Screens), a vision-language model for transferable active visual perception. Our framework first bootstraps observation capabilities from teacher-synthesized agentic GUI grounding trajectories and then optimizes multi-turn crop-and-inspect decisions with reinforcement learning. Spatial target annotations are transformed into outcome-gated rewards with selective credit assignment, allowing the model to learn not only how to inspect, but also which inspections are useful for task completion. Under fixed inference settings, models based on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.8-27B obtain consistent improvements over their corresponding baselines. Crucially, after agentic reinforcement learning, these gains transfer to out-of-domain non-GUI benchmarks such as PerceptionBench, HR-Bench and ChartQAPro, exceeding those obtained from cold-start supervised fine-tuning alone. This result suggests that the learned capability is not merely GUI-specific imitation, but a more general observation strategy. Further analysis reveals a complementary role of the two training stages: SFT acquires the primitives of visual inspection, whereas RL learns to allocate observation effort according to downstream utility. We therefore establish GUI grounding as a scalable supervision paradigm for active visual perception and provide evidence that interface-centric interaction data can induce transferable observation behaviors for broader multimodal reasoning. Our code and model are publicly available at https://github.com/inclusionAI/UI-Venus/tree/LBS and https://huggingface. co/inclusionAI/LBS-27B , respectively. 

## 1. Introduction

Active visual perception requires a vision-language model (VLM) to acquire evidence as well as interpret it. On a high-resolution image, the model must locate relevant regions, select an appropriate view, and relate local detail to the original scene (Khayatkhoei et al., 2025, Bai et al., 2025). Crop-and-inspect and think-with-image methods demonstrate the value of additional visual observations (Qi et al., 2025, Zheng et al., 2026, Wu and Xie, 2024, Wang et al., 2026a). A central training challenge is to connect these intermediate observation decisions to task success, particularly when a correct final answer can follow either informative or redundant inspection.

GUI grounding provides a concrete setting for learning this connection. Each example pairs an instruction with a spatial target, making both the final localization and intermediate crop geometry measurable (Cheng et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025, Li et al., 2025). These annotations can therefore supervise intermediate observation decisions without specifying an ideal crop sequence for reinforcement learning. We hypothesize that such supervision can help elicit latent visual knowledge in pretrained VLMs by training them to put this knowledge into practice through sequential observation: deciding where to inspect, how much context to retain, and when further evidence is useful. Although the source images are interfaces, these decisions are also relevant to natural images and charts. This motivates our central question: can spatial supervision from GUI grounding elicit observation behavior that transfers to non-GUI perception? Such transfer would suggest a practical way to make pretrained visual knowledge more accessible through interaction, potentially reducing the need to construct specialized observation trajectories for each downstream domain. We address this question with a two-stage framework. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on teacher-generated grounding trajectories initializes the crop-and-inspect protocol. Multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) then refines the policy using grounding correctness and training-only target annotations (Guo et al., 2025). Outcome-gated spatial rewards connect intermediate inspections to task success, a small-target bonus encourages target coverage, and selective response masking controls which intermediate responses receive policy updates. In this way, spatial annotations provide supervision for the process of acquiring visual evidence as well as for the final grounding result. The same crop interface is used at inference across domains, with task-specific final answers. This design allows us to examine whether observation behavior learned under GUI supervision benefits tasks with different visual content and objectives. Our results support transfer beyond supervised initialization. RL improves ScreenSpot-Pro (Li et al., 2025) accuracy over SFT by 1.83 and 6.13 percentage points for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.8-27B, and im-proves PerceptionBench (Lin et al., 2026) by approximately 3.0 and 7.10 points. The Qwen3.5-based policy also improves on HR-Bench 4K/8K (Wang et al., 2025) and ChartQAPro (Masry et al., 2025). The reward comparison reveals a useful distinction: outcome-only RL improves source-domain grounding but lowers PerceptionBench accuracy, whereas the spatially supervised configuration improves both. These compar-isons show that higher GUI grounding accuracy does not necessarily imply stronger non-GUI transfer; the results highlight the value of supervising intermediate observation decisions. Comparisons of reconstructed interaction histories further examine how crop images and their presentation scale affect answer generation. Together, these findings support GUI grounding as a practical source of supervision for visual observation whose benefits extend beyond the source domain, consistent with the hypothesis that spatially grounded interaction training can help elicit and apply pretrained visual knowledge across tasks. Our contributions are threefold: • We establish empirical evidence that GUI grounding can supervise visual observation policies that transfer beyond interfaces, with gains over SFT across two backbones on PerceptionBench and across additional high-resolution and chart benchmarks for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. • We develop a two-stage framework that converts target annotations into outcome-gated spatial rewards and selective response updates for multi-turn crop-and-inspect training. • We show that improved grounding under outcome-only RL need not translate into improved non-GUI perception, and complement the training comparison with controlled edits to historical crop observations.

## 2. Related Work 

Agentic visual reasoning. Agentic visual reasoning treats observation as sequential evidence acquisition. ReAct interleaves reasoning with environment interaction (Yao et al., 2022), and Toolformer learns API use from self-supervised data (Schick et al., 2023). Visual agents similarly use image manipulation and code-based tools during reasoning (Team et al., 2026a, Anthropic, 2026, Google, 2026). CogCoM integrates image manipulations into reasoning (Qi et al., 2025); V ∗ uses guided visual search to select informative regions at inference time (Wu and Xie, 2024). DeepEyes and DeepEyesV2 study reinforcement learning for visual and multimodal tool use (Zheng et al., 2026, Hong et al., 2026). These methods establish the utility of additional observations and tool interaction. Our focus is the supervision that teaches inspection behavior and whether it transfers from GUI grounding to non-GUI perception. 

Visually grounded reasoning. Visually grounded reasoning connects linguistic predictions to image regions (Kazemzadeh et al., 2014, Plummer et al., 2015, Zhu et al., 2016). Recent methods can be organized along two axes: by paradigm, into training-based approaches (Sarch et al., 2026, Zheng et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b) and test-time approaches (Khayatkhoei et al., 2025, Shen et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2026b); and by motivation, into suppressing irrelevant background (Wei et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2025) and enhancing task-relevant foreground through zoom-in operations (Fan et al., 2026, Luo et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026a, Zheng et al., 2026). ViGoRL anchors reasoning to visual coordinates, while TreeVGR jointly supervises localization and reasoning (Sarch et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026a). GUI grounding methods instead emphasize instruction-conditioned target localization and downstream action execution (Cheng et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025, Team et al., 2026b). Our study connects these directions by using GUI target annotations to supervise intermediate observation actions and evaluating the resulting policy on non-GUI perception. The central distinction is the source of supervision and its transfer: one spatially annotated interaction domain supplies feedback for observation policies evaluated across different visual content and answer formats. 

## 3. Method 

Our framework turns GUI target annotations into supervision for sequential visual observation (Figure 1). SFT first teaches the interaction protocol; RL then scores crop geometry and filters intermediate response updates using grounding outcomes. The policy receives images and instructions, while target annotations remain training-only information. At inference, the crop interface is shared across domains and the final answer format is adapted to each task. 

3.1. Grounding Supervision and Policy Initialization Synthetic grounding tasks. We construct interfaces through code-guided generation, following prior work on synthetic multimodal and GUI data (Yang et al., 2025, Team et al., 2026b). An LLM produces executable HTML/CSS/JavaScript from a scenario, optionally conditioned on a persona or reference screenshot. Ren-dering associates each screenshot with DOM structure, element states, and geometry. Candidate targets are extracted from element semantics and accessibility attributes, filtered for visibility, clipping, occlusion, and correspondence to rendered content, and refined using text-node geometry where appropriate. Accepted targets are paired with natural-language interaction instructions; infeasible instructions provide negative examples. Both SFT and RL use only GUI grounding instructions, with no general-domain VQA training tasks. Reference screenshots used for synthetic image generation exclude images from the evaluation benchmarks.

3.2. Multi-Turn Visual Observation 

At turn t, the policy samples at ∼ πθ (· | ht), where ht contains the task, original image, and interaction history. Each response contains reasoning followed by either a final answer or one or two textual crop_image actions. A crop action specifies an available image and a rectangle in its local pixel coordinates; previously returned crops may be inspected recursively. Final grounding predictions always use original-image normalized coordinates. The tool returns the crop image, its dimensions, and a coordinate mapping to the original image. Results from the same turn are added to the history in action order; failed executions return textual feedback. Earlier images and messages remain available, so each crop adds a local view while preserving access to the original context. Interaction ends with a final answer or a configured stopping limit. Every admitted crop attempt consumes a tool-call slot, including failed executions. Appendix B details crop construction and coordinate tracking.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup Training configuration. We study Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Team, 2026) and Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026). For both models, we freeze the vision encoder and vision–language projector throughout SFT and RL, updating only the language-model parameters. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B initially exhibits limited adherence to our prompting format and agentic observation protocol. To establish reliable protocol following, we use approximately 35,000 

SFT trajectories for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and 10,000 for Qwen3.8-27B. Each SFT checkpoint then initializes the corresponding RL policy. The main configuration combines grounding correctness, gated spatial feedback, format compliance, small-target inspection bonus and the auxiliary critic. Optimization settings and prompts appear in Appendices B and F. We also keep the training recipe simple and do not extensively tune the remaining optimization hyperparameters, focusing our study on the proposed supervision design. 

Interaction protocol. The crop tool uses a scale factor of α = 4.0 and a pixel cap of Pmax = 4 × 10 6 (Equa-tion (18)). Original images are preprocessed with a pixel count between 65,536 and 16,777,216 . We generate rollouts using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). Training allows up to three crop calls per example. Qwen3.8-27B eval-uation also uses at most three crop calls, with reasoning_effort=xhigh and thinking_budget=4096 

(4,096 reasoning tokens per assistant turn), passed through chat_template_kwargs . Within each bench-mark, SFT and RL models use identical prompts, decoding settings, and inference limits. 

Benchmarks and metrics. ScreenSpot-Pro (Li et al., 2025) evaluates instruction-conditioned GUI localiza-tion using point-in-box accuracy. PerceptionBench (Lin et al., 2026) evaluates non-GUI perception across ten capabilities using reference-based answer judgment, with a locally deployed DeepSeek-V4-Flash (Xu et al., 2026) serving as the judge model. The Qwen3.8-27B evaluation uses Deepseekv4-flash-0731, with no failed or skipped judgments across all 3,000 records. We interpret stage-wise gains within each backbone rather than treating cross-backbone scores as a controlled ranking. We report overall accuracy and category-level scores. Additional evaluations cover OSWorld-G-R (Refine) (Xie et al., 2026), HR-Bench (Wang et al., 2025) at 4K and 8K resolutions, and ChartQAPro (Masry et al., 2025). Further details on selected additional benchmarks are provided in Appendix A. 

4.2. GUI Grounding Across Backbones 

We compared our method with several mainstream GUI grounding methods (Company, 2025, Zhang et al., 2026c, Kim and Chelikavada, 2026, Pei et al., 2026, Zhou et al., 2025), and the results are presented in Table 1. Table 1 shows that RL improves ScreenSpot-Pro accuracy over SFT for both backbones. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B increases from 78.56% to 80.39% , and Qwen3.8-27B increases from 75.02% to 81.15% . Since both SFT baselines already follow the crop-and-inspect protocol, these gains support the value of optimizing observation trajectories beyond teacher imitation. Qwen3.8-27B gains 11.89 points over base and improves all six application groups over SFT, led by CAD and engineering ( +7.28 ), creative applications ( +6.45 ), and scientific applications ( +6.30 ). Qwen3.5-35B-A3B improves in five groups, led by development ( +6.35 ), but declines in office applications ( 90.00% to 87.83% ). These results are consistent with the utility of zoom-in inspection for high-resolution GUI grounding, where enlarged local views can make small interface elements and fine-grained visual details easier to distinguish and localize.

4.4. Transfer Across Visual Domains 

Figure 2(a) extends the stage-wise evaluation of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B to another GUI setting, high-resolution perception, and chart understanding. Scores increase from Base to SFT to RL on all four benchmarks. On OSWorld-G-R, SFT raises accuracy from 63.6% to 66.9% , and RL further improves it to 72.7% , yielding a 5.8 -point gain over SFT. Figure 2(b) compares the incremental gains from the two training stages. RL yields larger gains than SFT on every benchmark. On the non-GUI tasks, RL improves over SFT by 2.2 , 3.1 ,and 3.2 percentage points on HR-Bench 4K, HR-Bench 8K, and ChartQAPro, respectively, exceeding the corresponding SFT gains over Base of 1.1 , 1.7 , and 0.1 points. ChartQAPro illustrates this difference: SFT changes the score only from 63.4% to 63.5% , whereas RL raises it to 66.7% . Together with PerceptionBench, these results show that improvements beyond supervised initialization extend across general perception, high-resolution imagery, and charts under the reported evaluation settings. 

4.5. Reward Design Ablation 

Outcome-only RL reveals a distinction between source-domain improvement and cross-domain transfer. It reaches 79.19% on ScreenSpot-Pro and 40.87% on PerceptionBench, compared with 78.56% and 42.10% for SFT. Thus, a 0.63 -point grounding gain accompanies a 1.23 -point transfer decline. Our full configuration, combining the gated spatial process reward, small-target inspection bonus, and text-based critic, improves both benchmarks over SFT and exceeds outcome-only RL by 1.20 and 4.20 points, respectively. Taken together, the results favor the full configuration for cross-domain transfer, while showing that gating alone does not consistently improve performance across the evaluated configurations.

## 5. Conclusion 

GUI grounding provides a practical source of transferable supervision for active visual perception. Building on recent, strong open-weight VLMs and newly established baselines, we demonstrate improvements in both GUI grounding and non-GUI perception. Our results suggest that supervising intermediate observation decisions helps models inspect question-relevant regions, while crop upsampling makes fine-grained details more accessible for visual understanding. We hope this approach can be a step toward models that actively seek relevant evidence and develop a deeper understanding of the visual world. 

## 6. Discussion 

Our results suggest that agentic visual perception is becoming increasingly accessible to modern VLMs. With suitable supervision and a simple crop-and-inspect interface, these models can improve image-level perception without lots of additional general-domain VQA training. This progress motivates extending observation policies from individual images to video. Recent work such as Video-Index (Song et al., 2026) highlights both the potential of agent tools and the remaining challenges in video understanding, while emphasizing evaluation that genuinely requires temporal evidence. A promising near-term direction is therefore to learn when and where to inspect across both space and time, including selecting informative frames and maintaining useful visual memory. Extending these capabilities to long videos and streaming inputs will further require balancing evidence acquisition with context, latency, and computation budgets.

B.1. Training Configuration 

Both Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.8-27B use the same RL optimization and rollout configuration. GRPO samples G = 8 trajectories with temperature 0.8 and top-p 0.95 . We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) with learning rate 10 −6, a cosine schedule without warmup, weight decay 0.1 , (β1, β2) = ( 0.9, 0.95 ),and gradient clipping at norm 1.0 . The global batch contains 512 trajectories ( 64 tasks with 8 rollouts each) across 64 NPUs, with a per-device micro-batch size of 1 and 8 gradient-accumulation steps. Training uses bfloat16 precision and DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 with CPU optimizer offloading, as in the released training launcher. Token-level importance ratios use asymmetric clipping bounds ϵ− = 0.2 and ϵ+ = 0.28 . The training launcher configures one epoch, with maximum sequence length 32,768 , up to 12,288 completion tokens per trajectory, and 2,560 per assistant turn.

## F. Training and Critic Prompts 

F.1. System Prompt for Agentic RL 

We train with ms-swift GRPO and a custom multi-turn scheduler. At rollout initialization, the scheduler constructs the system message below from the protocol template, expanding the crop description. Braced fields are filled with the task, image metadata, and tool budget; line wrapping is for presentation only. The original screenshot is supplied in the initial user message. The {max_tool_calls} field is set to the configured tool-call budget, capped at 3 (default: 3). The scheduler executes textual crop actions and returns images and metadata in a user message, without a native function-calling schema. GRPO rewards and loss masks are computed by the framework and plugin, not specified through an additional training prompt.

The final coordinate MUST be relative to the ORIGINAL image and normalized to [0.00,1000.00], where [0.00,0.00] is the original image's top-left and [1000.00,1000.00] is its bottom-right.
