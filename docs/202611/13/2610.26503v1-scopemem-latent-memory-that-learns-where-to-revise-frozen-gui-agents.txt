Abstract 

Latent memory is gaining ground as alightweight history supplement for GUI agents. It supplies history to a frozen vision-language model via prefix vectors trained with step-wise cross-entropy (CE). While resulting gains are naturally attributed to encoded history, our con-trolled experiments show they primarily stem from action revision. The prefix learns to alter the model’s default actions. However, this revi-sion is unscoped because step-wise CE pushes outputs toward reference actions at every step without explicitly preserving correct decisions. Consequently, it repairs errors but also breaks 8–13% of initially correct answers. Overall ac-curacy metrics hide these losses. We therefore propose S COPE MEM , a latent-memory method for scoped revision. Its encoder conditions pre-fix generation on the model’s initial answer and is trained to correct errors while preserv-ing correct decisions. On the controlled ab-lation set, S COPE MEM reduces the share of broken correct decisions to 5.6% while repair-ing more errors than any evaluated CE-trained prefix. On AndroidControl, GUI-Odyssey, and zero-shot AITW, S COPE MEM outperforms all evaluated prompting and latent-memory baselines, enabling a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B to match or surpass GUI-specialized agents and Qwen3-VL-32B.

Our controlled experiments (§3) show that much of the gain persists even when the encoder does not read history. Holding the actor, its context and training fixed, a task-only encoder improves ac-curacy by 12.0 percentage points, compared with 11.8 points for an encoder that also reads recent history. The actor already sees the same recent his-tory in both conditions, so this comparison isolates the benefit of encoding that history again into the prefix. The small difference suggests that, in this setting, the prefix mainly revises actions using the actor’s existing context. At every step, CE rewards changes that move the actor’s output towards the reference action, with-out requiring its output distribution to remain un-changed when its decision is already correct. This leaves the revision unscoped . A change can repair 1arXiv:2610.26503v1 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 an incorrect decision, but it can also turn a cor-rect decision into an incorrect one, which we call a harm . Across our CE-trained prefixes, 8–13% of the actor’s originally correct decisions become incorrect. Encoders that read different inputs ex-hibit similar harm rates, pointing to their shared objective. Figure 1 illustrates how overall accuracy gains can conceal many harms because accuracy reports only the net effect of repairs and harms. We therefore propose SCOPE MEM , alatent-memory method for scoped revision .It aims to repair the actor’s errors while preserving the decisions it already gets right. To make this judgement, the encoder needs to see the particular answer it may revise, which a context-only encoder never directly observes. SCOPE MEM therefore conditions prefix generation on the actor’s first opinion . Figure 2 shows the resulting two-pass process. The frozen actor first responds without a prefix. The encoder then reads this answer and its confidence signals together with the task and recent history, and generates a prefix for a second pass over the same context. The resulting second opinion is the action S COPE MEM takes. However, seeing the first opinion does not teach the encoder which decisions to preserve. We there-fore train only the encoder with a scoped preserva-tion objective. We call the steps where the actor’s original answer is wrong the scope of revision. Dur-ing training, reference actions identify these steps. CE remains active at every step to encourage the reference action. Outside the scope, we addition-ally penalise changes to the actor’s original output distribution. At deployment, the encoder uses the first opinion and context to decide which answers to preserve, without access to reference actions. On the controlled ablation set, scoped preser-vation reduces harms for every encoder, most strongly for the first-opinion encoder. S COPE MEM 

lowers the harm rate to 5.6% while repairing more errors than any CE-trained prefix. Across AndroidControl, GUI-Odyssey and zero-shot AITW, it outperforms all evaluated prompting and latent-memory baselines. With S COPE MEM , the frozen Qwen3-VL-8B actor matches or surpasses GUI-specialised agents and Qwen3-VL-32B under the same actor context. Vision–language GUI agents. 3 Does Latent Memory Remember or Revise? 

A prefix may supply information the actor lacks or change how it uses information already in its context. To distinguish these explanations, we withhold history from the encoder while keeping the actor’s history available. If the prefix helps mainly by encoding history, its gain should shrink. If it mainly revises how the actor uses existing con-text, much of the gain should remain. Revision also predicts a side effect. Since cross-entropy pulls every step towards the refer-ence action whatever the prefix reads, a revising prefix should change correct decisions as well as wrong ones. Prefixes trained in the usual way, with cross-entropy alone, behave as revision predicts on both counts.

3.2 The gain comes from revision 

Figure 3a shows that the task-only encoder im-proves the actor as much as the history encoder (12.0 vs. 11.8 pp), and both improve it about twice as much as the fixed-query prefix. For history mi-nus task-only accuracy, the paired 95% confidence interval has an upper bound of 1.6 pp, a small frac-tion of either encoder’s gain. The prefix thus mainly revises how the actor uses its existing context and should be judged by the decisions it changes. 

3.3 The revision repairs and harms 

Accuracy measures only the net effect of a revision, so we count its two kinds of change separately. A repair is a step that the plain actor gets wrong and the prefixed actor gets right, and a harm is the reverse. With R repairs and H harms over N steps, the gain over the plain actor is 

∆Acc = R − H

N . (2) We also report two rates. The repair rate divides R by the plain actor’s errors, and the harm rate 

divides H by its correct steps. Every tested prefix changes most of the plain actor’s correct actions in at least one field. Changes need not cause errors, since coordinate shifts within the benchmark’s tolerance still count as changes. Most modified actions remain correct, but some become incorrect. These harms cancel more than a quarter of what each encoder repairs, and more than half for the fixed-query prefix. The task-only and history encoders harm at nearly the same rate although they read different inputs, which points to the objective they share. A gate could fall back to the plain actor’s answer to avoid harms, but it would need to distinguish harmful from helpful revisions. The actor’s confi-dence detects its own errors moderately well (AUC 0.72). However, Figure 3b shows that this confi-dence barely distinguishes S COPE MEM ’s repairs from harms (AUC 0.53). This motivates learning the scope rather than gating revisions by this confi-dence alone.

4.1 A second opinion on the actor’s answer 

Under Eq. (3), revision is needed when the actor’s initial answer is wrong. An encoder without screen-shots can use textual context to estimate the actor’s chance of being correct. However, that context does not show the answer the actor actually produced. We therefore first query the frozen actor without a prefix to obtain the first opinion a0. The encoder receives this answer before the second actor pass. Both passes use greedy decoding. The confidence inputs are the actor’s proba-bilities over ten action types ( p0) and their en-tropy ( H0). The first-opinion encoder Eϕ is a 10.5M-parameter cross-attention network. It gen-erates zt from a0, these inputs, the task, rolling summary and recent actions. With this prefix, the frozen actor produces the second opinion at,SCOPE MEM ’s selected action. 

4.2 Learning the scope 

Reference actions are unavailable at deployment, so correctness labels guide the encoder only during training. The keep indicator kt is 1 when the initial answer is correct under the benchmark’s matching rules, and 0 when the answer is wrong. The training objective is 

L = CE + kt wt Pt, (4) where CE is the cross-entropy loss of the reference action and Pt is a preservation term based on KL divergence. For incorrect initial answers ( kt = 0 ), CE encourages the reference action. For correct initial answers, weighted preservation penalises differences between the actor’s token distributions with and without the prefix, evaluated along the reference action. CE still encourages the reference tokens, while preservation also constrains changes to other token probabilities. We call this objective 

scoped preservation . The encoder receives no correctness label kt. Instead, the encoder learns to generate prefixes from the first opinion, context and confidence inputs. Three choices shape the objective. First, bench-marks require exact action-type matches but al-low tolerance for arguments such as coordinates. 5We therefore give decision tokens , including those specifying action type, ten times the preservation weight of argument tokens . Second, the confi-dence weight wt increases with confidence, so con-fident correct answers receive stronger preservation. Third, the first epoch uses CE alone to teach the prefix to revise. Adding preservation immediately would reward a prefix that changes nothing. The second epoch adds preservation to teach the en-coder which decisions to keep.

5.3 Where S COPE MEM gains over latent memory 

Mem-W is the strongest memory method. It writes a prefix for the same frozen actor, using the same data and schedule. The prefix holds retrieved past trajectories and a working snapshot of the current one, and Mem-W trains it with cross-entropy and self-distillation on every step. Table 3 shows the most consistent difference in harms: Mem-W breaks a third to nearly two thirds more correct decisions than S COPE MEM . On AndroidControl and AITW, the methods repair sim-ilar numbers of errors, so S COPE MEM ’s accuracy advantage comes mainly from preserving more correct decisions. The AndroidControl accuracy gap remains within sampling error. The advantage is largest on GUI-Odyssey, where S COPE MEM 

also repairs 46 more errors. GUI-Odyssey has the longest trajectories, where memory of ear-lier steps could be especially useful. However, SCOPE MEM ’s advantage over Mem-W is similar on short and long trajectories, consistent with re-vision as its main source. Much of this advan-tage comes from completion decisions, where our Mem-W reproduction more often ends tasks too early, and from clicks, which account for most er-rors that only S COPE MEM repairs. 

6 Analysis: Testing the Scope 

We test three predictions of scoped revision with matched variants. (i) Preservation outside the scope lowers harms regardless of encoder input. (ii) Pre-serving every step also holds the actor to its er-rors. (iii) Scoping helps the first-opinion encoder most because it observes the decision being re-vised. Each band of Table 4 fixes the prefix’s in-puts and varies only the second epoch. This epoch is skipped ( first epoch only ) or uses cross-entropy alone , scoped preservation or unscoped preserva-tion . The last applies preservation at every step (kt = 1 ). 

6.1 Does preservation remove harms, and at what cost? 

Switching the second epoch from cross-entropy alone to scoped preservation removes 17–27% of each encoder’s harms in every training run, as claim (i) predicts and Figure 4 shows for the main run. Part of the harms in §3.3 therefore comes from the objective, whatever the prefix reads. Preserving every step costs repairs (claim ii). For the first-opinion encoder, unscoped preservation reduces harms about as much as scoped preser-vation. Relative to cross-entropy alone, however, it loses one in eight repairs and lowers accuracy. Preserving outputs at every step also discourages correction of the actor’s mistakes, for instance on completion steps. Under scoped preservation, the task-only and history encoders retain almost all their CE repairs. The first-opinion encoder is the only variant that both lowers harms and raises re-pairs relative to CE alone. Neither more training nor the second actor pass explains the gain. Starting from the first-epoch checkpoint, a second epoch of cross-entropy alone barely changes the first-opinion encoder’s accuracy. Scoped preservation in the second epoch raises accuracy by 2.1 pp over that same checkpoint, be-yond sampling error. With cross-entropy alone, the 7two-pass first-opinion encoder and the one-pass task-only encoder gain similar amounts over the plain actor (12.03 vs. 11.96 pp), with the difference within sampling error. Most of the harms the scope removes are action-type harms, including premature comple-tions, as Appendix A.3 shows. The action type is chosen by the decision tokens, which the preserva-tion term weights most. 

6.2 Why does the scope need the first opinion? 

Appendix Table 5 shows that scoped preservation adds less than one percentage point of accuracy over CE alone for the task-only and history en-coders, but about three times as much for the first-opinion encoder. Appendix Table 6 shows similar reductions in the shares of correct actions and errors changed by the task-only and history en-coders under scoped preservation. Reducing revi-sion overall can avoid harms, but it can also prevent useful corrections. For the first-opinion encoder, scoping reduces changes to correct actions by a similar amount as for the other two encoders. However, the scoped encoder changes as many of the actor’s errors as its CE version and repairs more of them. This selec-tivity supports the motivation in §4.1: the encoder observes the actual answer being revised. Across the three training runs in Appendix C, the accuracy gain from scoped preservation over CE is larger for the first-opinion encoder than for the history encoder. This interaction averages 1.18 pp (95% interval across runs [0.38, 1.99]). For the main run alone, the bootstrap interval for the interaction includes zero. 

What the prefix uses. The interventions on the encoder’s inputs in Appendix Table 7 point the same way. Corrupting the first opinion lowers ac-curacy beyond sampling error, whereas removing history changes it only within sampling error.

7 Conclusion 

We study latent memory as a history supplement for frozen GUI agents and show that, when re-cent history is already available, much of its ben-efit comes from revising decisions. Our con-trolled experiments reveal that CE-trained pre-fixes repair errors but also break correct decisions, losses that aggregate accuracy hides. We propose SCOPE MEM to scope this revision. Its encoder reads the actor’s first answer and learns to gen-erate prefixes that revise errors while preserving correct answers. S COPE MEM achieves more re-pairs and fewer harms than CE-trained prefixes in controlled ablations. Across three benchmarks, it outperforms prompting and latent-memory base-lines and enables a frozen 8B actor to match or surpass GUI-specialised models. 8Limitations 

Our experiments are offline and step-level, so online task success, for example on Android-World (Rawles et al., 2025), remains to be tested. Step-level matching may also penalise valid alterna-tives to the reference actions, and noise in these ref-erences mislabels some first opinions during train-ing. We study one actor, Qwen3-VL-8B, on mobile benchmarks, and our analysis of memory concerns an actor that already sees its recent steps. Other actors, platforms and history budgets may behave differently. The memory baselines follow their orig-inal papers under our shared training budget, and our fine-grained analyses come from one training run. S COPE MEM also queries the actor twice per step, which roughly doubles its latency. We leave online evaluation and memories that reach beyond the recent steps to future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

We use existing public GUI benchmarks, without new participant recruitment or online action exe-cution. Screenshots may contain names, account identifiers or other incidental personal information, and we have not completed a de-identification au-dit. Frozen-actor interventions could in principle be used to steer an agent towards unintended ac-tions. Our method changes only a small module and is evaluated offline. 

Assets and intended use. The data and models serve offline research, and our system is a research prototype.
