# Beyond Next-Screen Prediction: The Decision Value of GUI World Models 

Sizhe Tang 1,2,∗, Hanhan Zhou 2, Jeffrey Svajlenko 2, Rashmi Gangadharaiah 21George Washington University, 2Amazon Web Services 

> ∗

Work done during an internship at AWS AI Labs. 

GUI world models are increasingly used to improve action selection by predicting the consequences of candidate actions, but it is unclear how much of the resulting gain comes from prediction itself rather than from the evaluator that interprets those predictions. We study this attribution problem through controlled interventions on GUI action selection. With candidates and predictions held fixed, lookahead gains can change with how the prediction is presented to the evaluator, so end-to-end gains cannot be attributed to prediction alone. We then introduce Direct Action Ranking (DAR), which learns from reference actions to score candidates without generating future screens, and compare it with a prediction-augmented variant under matched supervision. Predicted successors add no detectable ranking signal once evaluation is learned directly. Without retraining, DAR improves AndroidWorld task success by 5.8–16.4 points across three proposers, outperforms world-model lookahead, and transfers zero-shot to unseen apps, where every prediction-conditioned configuration falls below proposer confidence. These results suggest that GUI world models should be evaluated by their decision value, the incremental action-selection value of their predictions beyond a strong direct evaluator, rather than by prediction fidelity or end-to-end lookahead gain alone.

1 Introduction 

GUI agents increasingly rely on learned models of interface dynamics to reason before acting. Rather than committing immediately to the proposer’s highest-ranked action, a lookahead agent can predict how the interface will change under several candidate actions and use those predicted outcomes to guide its choice. This idea has become a prominent direction in GUI agents, spanning textual outcome prediction, graphical generation, and renderable next-state simulation (Gu et al., 2025; Chae et al., 2025a; Zheng et al., 2026). The appeal is straightforward: anticipating action consequences can prevent mistakes before execution. A lookahead pipeline has two components. A world model generates possible future observations; an evaluator turns them into a ranking over candidate actions. End-to-end success depends on both the information in the prediction and the evaluator’s ability to use it. The same prediction can support a better choice under one evaluator and a worse choice under another. A gain from “world-model lookahead” is therefore a gain of the prediction–evaluator system, and it does not reveal how much came from the predicted observation. This distinction changes what should be measured. Prediction fidelity measures whether a predicted screen resembles the true future interface; end-to-end lookahead gain measures the whole pipeline. Neither measures what the prediction contributes to the choice. We call this decision value of GUI world models: the improvement in action selection attributable to predicted observations beyond direct evaluation of the same candidates. Decision value is defined by intervention, not by fidelity. A visually accurate prediction can be decision-irrelevant when the current screen and action already determine the right choice, while a coarse prediction can still be useful if it exposes a consequence that changes which candidate is best. Measuring it therefore requires comparing direct and prediction-conditioned evaluation on the same candidates under matched supervision (Figure 1). We study this question for one-step prediction used in inference-time candidate selection. We investigate this quantity by progressively separating prediction from evaluation. > GUI agents and grounding.

SeeClick and UGround learn visual grounding (Cheng et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2025); SeeAct coordinates planning and grounding (Zheng et al., 2024). WebVoyager and UI-TARS pursue end-to-end multimodal interaction (He et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025). These agents must both propose useful actions and identify their targets. We study the subsequent selection problem, keeping the proposer fixed while comparing ways to choose among its candidates.  

> World models for GUI agents.

GUI lookahead uses textual predictions in WMA and WebDreamer (Chae et al., 2025a; Gu et al., 2025), separate graphical and textual generation in ViMo (Luo et al., 2025), and renderable code in Code2World and gWorld (Zheng et al., 2026; Koh et al., 2026). WebEvolver and WebWorld also use simulation for policy training and inference-time search (Fang et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026). Xu et al. (2026) examine feedback representation and confidence in mobile guidance. End-to-end gains evaluate the complete system; our fixed-prediction and matched-training comparisons separate the effects of changing an evaluator from those of adding predicted observations.  

> Learned action evaluation.

Solution verifiers (Cobbe et al., 2021) and process supervision (Lightman et al., 2024) learn to assess reasoning. Web-Shepherd learns step-level web rewards (Chae et al., 2025b); WebArbiter generates reasoning-based preference verdicts (Zhang et al., 2026). DAR learns from reference-action matches to score GUI candidates directly. Training a prediction-augmented DAR model on the same labels then tests whether predicted successors improve this learned comparison.

4 Separating Prediction from Action Evaluation 

4.1 Fixed Predictions, Different Decisions 

We first hold candidates and predictions fixed and vary how they are evaluated. On 3,000 AndroidControl evaluation steps, we use Qwen2.5-VL-7B candidates (Bai et al., 2025b) ( K ≤ 3) and cached Code2World-8B predictions (Zheng et al., 2026). These candidate sets leave room to improve selection without changing the proposer: 56.1% contain a reference match, while Policy Top-1 matches on 43.8% (Table 2). With these fixed predictions, lookahead raises Reference-Match Accuracy over Policy Top-1 by 1.7 points with Claude Sonnet 4.6 and 2.5 points with Claude Opus 4.8; both 95% episode-clustered intervals exclude zero (Appendix A.1). The gain also depends on how the prediction reaches the evaluator: with Opus 4.8 and the same predicted successors, re-encoding them as an accessibility tree or plain text lowers the point estimate from +2.5 to +1.2 points (Appendix D). End-to-end lookahead performance therefore characterizes a prediction–evaluator pair, not the prediction alone. This comparison does not measure decision value, which 4requires matched evaluators (Section 3.3). Figure 2 shows one decision in which Top-1 and lookahead choose different clicks, while the direct evaluator of Section 5 selects the reference scroll; Appendix E gives further examples.

5 Learning to Select Actions Directly 

5.1 DAR: Reference-Supervised Action Ranking 

DAR instantiates the direct evaluator Eθ in Eq. 1: it is both the control for measuring decision value and a practical selector. It scores each candidate from the goal, current interface, and action description, using reference actions only to construct training labels. The mobile input includes the screenshot and available accessibility elements with bounding boxes. We adapt Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025a) with frozen base weights, LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022), and a linear scoring head on the final-token representation. Each candidate is scored independently; candidates from the same observation are grouped when computing the ranking loss. The listwise term (Cao et al., 2007) trains the within-set ordering needed for selection, while the pointwise term supervises whether each candidate matches the reference. 5.4 Comparison With Proposer SFT 

A natural alternative is to spend the same reference actions on the proposer. Full-budget Qwen3-VL-32B SFT raises greedy single-step matching from 61.4% to 65.4% but shrinks the candidate set (mean deduplicated size from 2.73 to 1.34). DAR still adds 2.7 points on the SFT candidates, so training the proposer and learning to evaluate its candidates are complementary. With 2,000 labels, SFT falls below its zero-shot baseline, whereas DAR trained on 2,000 labels improves its own candidate pool by 3.8 points; the two settings differ in proposer and candidate pool, so this contrast is only qualitative. Appendix B.2 gives the full protocol and additional results.

6.4 WHEN OFFLINE RANKING AND TASK SUCCESS DIVERGE 

Offline ranking does not always predict online success. Removing the pointwise loss lowers AUROC from 0.957 to 0.934 , but reduces conditional match accuracy from 0.911 to 0.890 and task success by 6.0 points, with worse calibration (Table 8(b); Appendix C.4). The backbone variants point the same way on a smaller scale: GLM has slightly higher AUROC than MiMo but lower mean task success, and both improve on Top-1 (Table 8(a)). Pooled AUROC measures positive–negative orderings across candidates, whereas execution depends on the winner within each candidate set. Similar AUROC can therefore hide different action choices, and task success depends on which within-set decisions change. This is why the matched comparison in Section 5.3 bounds only the offline ranking signal of predictions, while AndroidWorld measures the downstream value of a selector’s choices. 7 Conclusion 

GUI lookahead couples prediction with evaluation, so end-to-end gains cannot be attributed to prediction alone. We propose measuring a GUI world model by its decision value: what its predictions add beyond a direct evaluator of the same candidates. Under matched supervision, predictions add no detectable ranking signal once evaluation is learned, and the direct evaluator, DAR, outperforms lookahead online without generating future screens. Our study covers one-step prediction for inference-time selection; future GUI world models should report decision value alongside prediction fidelity and end-to-end gain. 11 References
