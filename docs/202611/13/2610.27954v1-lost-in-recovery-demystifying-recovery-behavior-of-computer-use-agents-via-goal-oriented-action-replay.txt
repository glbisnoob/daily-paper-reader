Abstract 

Recent advances in large (vision) language models have enabled computer-use agents that automate digital workflows via fine-grained keyboard and mouse operations. However, such agents remain fragile in long-horizon set-tings, where errors accumulate and executed actions leave unintended side effects, mak-ing the ability to recover from failures critical. Yet, existing remedies remain inadequate: en-vironment resets are infeasible in proprietary settings, action replay is prohibitively expen-sive over long horizons, and API-based roll-back is limited to web environments, leaving model-based recovery largely unexplored. To address this gap, we present R ETRACE , a scal-able evaluation framework for systematically studying recovery behavior in computer-use agents. We formalize Transition-Consistent Recovery : identical downstream actions from the recovered and target states must yield iden-tical outcomes. We operationalize this crite-rion through goal-oriented action replay fol-lowing controlled error injection. Using R E-TRACE , we present the first systematic study of autonomous, model-based recovery, revealing that (i) ideal recovery can unlock model’s la-tent task completion capabilities, (ii) current computer-use agents fail to exploit their la-tent recovery capabilities, and (iii) accurate model-based recovery yields measurable end-to-end gains. Together, these findings estab-lish recovery as a fundamental capability for building reliable computer-use agents. Our code and dataset is available at https:// github.com/BangProx/ReTRACE .

advances in large (vision) language models (Bai et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026) have made it possible to auto-mate such workflows through computer-use agents ,which act in digital environments via fine-grained keyboard and mouse inputs (Gur et al., 2023; Fu-ruta et al., 2023; Sager et al., 2025). However, computer-use agents remain fragile in long-horizon and sophisticated tasks, where early mistakes accumulate and propagate across subse-quent steps (Wang et al., 2026a), and where exe-cuted actions may leave unintended side effects that should not have occurred (e.g., an agent organiz-ing a directory may complete the task but silently overwrite files that the user wanted to keep). As a result, the ability to revert the environment to a pre-viously intact state is critical for reliable operation. To address this, prior work explores two directions: 1arXiv:2610.27954v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 inducing self-reflective behaviors through textual feedback or parameter updates from reward signals that refine forward actions (Chae et al., 2025a,b; Wu et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026a; Xue et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c), and explicit test-time rollback through full environment resets followed by re-executing all prior actions (i.e., action replay) (Koh et al., 2024b; He et al., 2025) or API-based undo operations in web en-vironments (e.g., browser back) (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b), utilized as a heuristic compo-nent within an inference pipeline rather than sub-jected to systematic study. Yet, these heuristic remedies fall short of provid-ing general-purpose recovery. Environment resets are often infeasible in proprietary settings such as production websites and operating systems (Regatti et al., 2021; Nguyen and Cheng, 2023; Wang et al., 2026b), action replay (Koh et al., 2024b; He et al., 2025) inherits this requirement and becomes costly over long horizons, and API-based rollback (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b) is largely con-fined to web environments, leaving broader desk-top settings uncovered. Consequently, autonomous model-level recovery , in which the agent itself re-stores a prior state without relying on such ad hoc mechanisms, emerges as an essential capability. However, whether current agents possess recovery capabilities, and how it affects overall agent perfor-mance remain largely unexplored. Therefore, we investigate several fundamental research questions: 

RQ1. Is recovery necessary to unlock models’ la-tent capabilities? 

RQ2. Do current computer-use agents possess recovery capabilities under erroneous conditions, and are such capabilities latent or entirely absent? 

RQ3. Does accurate model-level recovery translate into measurable improvements in task completion? 

To address this, we introduce R ETRACE , a pipeline that conducts the first systematic study of autonomous, model-based recovery behavior in computer-use agents. Motivated by predictive state representation theory (Littman and Sutton, 2001), which represents states by the formaliza-tion of predicted futures, we formalize successful recovery as Transition-Consistent Recovery : a re-covered state matches its target if executing iden-tical downstream actions from both states yields identical outcomes. This formulation captures la-tent changes beyond visual similarity by leveraging behavioral equivalence, operationalized via goal-oriented action replay . Specifically, we replay each on-policy trajectory multiple times and retain only those with consistent successful outcomes, ensur-ing deterministic environment transitions. From these trajectories, we sample intermediate states as recovery targets, perturb them into erroneous states, and evaluate whether the original task outcome can be reproduced by replaying the remaining actions. We then instantiate R ETRACE on OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) and WindowsAgentArena (Bonatti et al., 2024) across closed- and open-source computer-use agents, and find that recovery plays a critical role in reliable agent operation. We first show that ideal recovery, in which agents are re-turned to an oracle prior state, boosts performance over naive environment resets (Section 4), identify-ing recovery as the key lever for unlocking agents’ latent task-solving capacity ( RQ1 ). Yet current agents leave this lever unused, lacking both the tendency and capability to recover autonomously (Section 5) ( RQ2 ). When equipped with explicit model-based recovery, however, recovery success climbs by more than an order of magnitude, reveal-ing that the underlying capability is latent rather than absent (Section 5) (RQ2) . Moreover, recovery quality in turn translates into downstream task suc-cess: accurate model-level recovery delivers mea-surable end-to-end gains by unlocking otherwise latent task-solving capacity, while weak recovery can actively derail the trajectory ( RQ3 ) (Section 6) .These findings position accurate model-based re-covery, together with reliable continuation from the recovered state, as a key step toward stronger computer-use agents.

3 Preliminary 

Computer-Use Task We formulate a computer-use agent (CUA) task as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Kaelbling et al., 1998), 

M = ( S, A, O, E, T , Ω) , where a policy πθ inter-acts with a digital environment E (e.g., Linux termi-nals, PowerPoint) to complete a user query q. Each state st = ( ht, o t) ∈ S decomposes into a hidden component ht and an observation ot = Ω( st) ∈ O ,satisfying the Markov property. At each step, πθ

selects an action at ∈ A, spanning high-level (CLICK ) to low-level ( pyautogui.click ) op-erations, yielding st+1 ∼ T (· | st, a t).

Forward Policy and Trajectory Since E is only partially observable, πθ conditions on the observa-tion–action history, πθ(at | o0, a 0, . . . , o t), yield-ing a trajectory τ = {(ot, a t)}Tt=0 . However, this forward formulation leaves unspecified how the agent should behave once early actions drive τ into an erroneous state from which q can no longer be completed, the setting we study next.

5.1 Transition-Consistent Recovery 

Given a trajectory τ = {(st, a t)}Tt=0 produced by a policy π, the recovery task is defined as a func-tion f that maps an arbitrary state st back to a past target state, f (st) = starget = st−d, where 

d is the recovery distance. Whether this mapping succeeds is judged by a predefined recovery ora-cle R, which returns a binary indicator of recov-ery success or failure, R(f (st), s target ) ∈ { 0, 1}.Recalling from Section 3 that each state decom-poses as st = ( ht, o t) into a hidden component 

ht and an observation ot, R should verify that 

f (st) matches starget in both components. A nat-ural instantiation of R is to compare the observa-tions of f (st) and starget directly, e.g., via pixel-or feature-level matching. However, this surface criterion is unreliable, since latent components of the environment may be implicitly altered with-out any visible change (Shani and Brafman, 2004) (e.g., a hidden configuration file modified while the screenshot remains identical), causing observation-only oracles to falsely declare success on states that nonetheless diverge in their downstream ef-fects. 5.4 Experiments 5.4.1 Setup 

We now describe the experimental setup, including datasets, models, and inference configurations. 

Dataset We evaluate recovery on two computer-use benchmarks, OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) and WindowsAgentArena (Bonatti et al., 2024), each deployed in an isolated environment and providing a user query together with an oracle evaluator that determines whether its execution succeeds. 

Models We evaluate a range of state-of-the-art computer-use agents. As closed-source models, we use Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026) and GPT-5.4-Computer-Use (OpenAI, 2026), which achieve top performance on OSWorld. For open-source models, we consider two post-trained agentic mod-els, EvoCUA-32B (Xue et al., 2026) and UI-TARS-1.5-7B (Qin et al., 2025), together with Qwen3.5-9B-Thinking (Qwen Team, 2026a), a strong open-source model without task-specific post-training. 

Inference Configurations We run experiments under short horizon and long horizon settings, with recovery distance d set to 1 and 5, respectively. We further vary the maximum recovery step budget 

r ∈ { 1, 3, 10 }.

Recovery Context We consider several recovery policy configurations with different contexts C. For the implicit recovery policy, we provide the obser-vation–action history from the first step up to the error step, denoted 0-E . For the explicit recovery policy, we evaluate four variants: 0-E ; 0-R , which instead provides the observation–action history up to the target recovery step starget ; w/o History ,which excludes the history from the context; and 

w/ Recovery Target Contextualization , which contextualizes the transition history leading up to the target recovery with an LLM summarizer to reduce context (Zhou et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025b).

Nevertheless, even the most capable models combined with ba-sic explicit recovery strategies still fail to achieve complete recovery under our evaluation settings, suggesting that substantial research remains in de-veloping more robust recovery mechanisms for computer-use agents. 

Efficacy of R ETRACE To assess how reliably RETRACE judges recovery success, we conduct a human evaluation study against three baselines: two ablations of our oracle R with either the image-wise matching component RO or the transition-consistent component RTC removed, and GPT-5.4, a state-of-the-art model with extensive computer-use knowledge. We recruit 12 annotators to judge whether each recovered state aligns with the target state, with 50 samples per variant and each anno-tator rating 25 of them. As shown in Table 1, R E-TRACE agrees with human judgments more often than all baselines, including GPT-5.4, confirming the reliability of its recovery oracle R. We fur-ther report an average inter-annotator agreement of 82% and a Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960) of 0.45, indicating reliable agreement.

6.2 Results and Analysis Potential Gains from Model-Based Recovery 

To validate the potential benefits of model-based recovery, we analyze its performance gains across diverse recovery settings. As shown in Table 2, model-based recovery improves Qwen3.5-9B over the no-recovery baseline under every recovery set-ting, with an average gain of 2.12% in the end-to-end setting. Recovery Performance Effects on Task Com-pletion The results in Table 3 show that models with weaker recovery capabilities (UI-TARS-1.5-7B, EvoCUA-32B) suffer performance degradation during end-to-end inference, underscoring the im-portance of accurate recovery behavior. EvoCUA-32B, in con-trast, exhibits severely limited explicit recovery capability: while even a single successful recovery can substantially improve downstream task perfor-mance, its consistently low recovery success rate means that additional attempts more often derail the trajectory into increasingly erroneous states. This suggests that accurate model-level recovery delivers performance gains, while weak recovery can actively derail the trajectory (Finding 6) .

6.3 Qualitative Analysis 

In Section 6.2, we showed that poor recovery per-formance can severely degrade downstream task completion, in contrast to the idealized settings of Section 4. To understand why, we examine the rationales that forward agents generate after recov-ering to incorrect states, and categorize the result-ing behaviors into two major patterns (Table 4). Additional examples are provided in Appendix D. 

Spurious Perception Our first observation is that agents fixate on elements distinct from the target state and rely on them when choosing their next action. For example, in Table 4, the agent imme-diately clicks the right-alignment button because the cursor happens to hover over it in the restored state, skipping the drag operation on the text that should have come first and thereby deviating from the trajectory needed for correct task completion. 

State Tracking Failure Meanwhile, agents also hallucinate actions by assuming the restored state matches the target, failing to register the discrep-ancy when the two diverge. For example, as shown in Table 4, although the restored state is masked by the terminal interface and no longer shows the font-selection UI, the agent acts as if that interface were still open, revealing the side effects of imperfect state restoration. 

7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we conducted the first systematic study of autonomous, model-level recovery behav-ior in computer-use agents through R ETRACE , a scalable pipeline that formalizes successful restora-tion as Transition-Consistent Recovery , where a recovered state matches its target if identical down-stream actions yield identical outcomes, and opera-tionalizes this via goal-oriented action replay . Em-pirical evaluations across closed and open-source 8agents reveal that current agents lack both the tendency and capability to recover autonomously, while accurate model-based recovery substantially closes this gap and translates into measurable end-to-end gains, despite characteristic failure modes such as spurious perception and state-tracking er-rors. We believe these findings position accurate model-based recovery as a key step toward reliable computer-use agents. 

Limitations 

RETRACE is designed to measure and analyze re-covery capabilities and behaviors rather than to directly improve them. Our explicit recovery set-tings can only elicit capabilities already present in the underlying model and cannot compensate for fundamental deficiencies in GUI understand-ing, state tracking, or action planning. Conse-quently, inaccurate recovery by specialist agents with weaker recovery capabilities may induce ad-ditional trajectory drift and reduce task success. Potential directions for addressing this gap include recovery-aware post-training on erroneous states, target states, and corresponding corrective action trajectories, as well as auxiliary objectives for track-ing latent environment states and predicting action-induced transitions. RETRACE can provide the evaluation protocol and controlled recovery scenar-ios needed to develop and assess such methods, but designing and validating them is beyond the scope of the present work. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our work aims to advance the reliability of computer-use agents by systematically studying their recovery behavior, contributing positively to the safe and dependable deployment of digital au-tomation. However, we acknowledge the dual-use nature of such research, as more capable agents could in principle be repurposed for harmful au-tomation. To mitigate such risks, we conduct all experiments in sandboxed virtual environments us-ing publicly available benchmarks in accordance with their licenses, without interaction with real user accounts or personal data, and recommend pairing deployments with appropriate safeguards.

A.1 Evaluation Set Construction Benchmarks We primarily leverage two publicly available benchmarks consisting of both web tasks and non-web tasks: OSWorld 1 (Xie et al., 2024), released under the Apache License 2.0, and Win-dowsAgentArena (Bonatti et al., 2024), released under the MIT License. On-Policy Rollout For each model, we construct trajectories for the corresponding tasks in each benchmark. We then create an annotated dataset by selecting potential starting steps within each trajectory, while filtering out trajectories contain-ing multiple actions within a single step or erro-neous trajectories in which actions are missing from the agent output, as such cases can complicate the annotation process. This process empirically ensures deter-ministic transition dynamics, resulting in a filtered evaluation set in which the starting step of each trajectory is guaranteed to exhibit consistent transi-tions while retaining 64.4% of recovery trajectories (Table 6). For OsWorld short-horizon evaluation settings using GPT-5.4-CUA, we randomly sam-ple 600 samples for perturbation after transition-consistent filtering to keep the API computational cost manageable prior to subsequent filtering stage (Trajectory Perturbation).

B.1 Ideal Gains from Recovery 

This experiment estimates how much end-to-end performance can improve when an agent is allowed to resume from an appropriate past state after a fail-15 ure. It measures the potential gain from recovery under a controlled replay setting, where the target past state is reconstructed by action replay before the forward agent resumes execution. 

Benchmark and model We run the analysis on OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) using EvoCUA-32B (Xue et al., 2026) as the forward model, fol-lowing the setup from Section A.1. We set the temperature to 0.7, max_tokens to 32768 , and 

max_steps to 50 . We first collect standard for-ward rollouts and keep only failed instances whose 

result.txt score is 0. For each failed instance, we identify an error step and select a correspond-ing recovery step using the recovery-step selection procedure described in Section 4. The environment at the selected recovery step is reconstructed by re-playing the saved action prefix up to that step. We measure Success@5 on 83 samples after excluding instances where action replay fails. 

Fixed annotations For each failed instance, we annotate an error step E once before running any recovery-step experiment. The annotation stage reads the saved trajectory, reconstructs the observation-action history, and calls the LLM error-step detector. When evaluating the LLM-selected recovery step, we also annotate a recovery step 

RLLM once in the same preparation stage. These annotations are then materialized into a fixed run plan, so different recovery-step conditions are eval-uated against the same underlying failed trajectory rather than re-detecting E or RLLM at runtime. 

Recovery Steps We compare four starting steps. 

R = 0 restarts from the initial environment state. 

R = E − 1 resumes from the step immediately be-fore the detected error. R = E − random samples a previous step with a fixed seed and reuses the same sampled step across runs for that instance. 

R = R LLM uses the recovery step predicted by the recovery-step detector. For R > 0, the environ-ment is reset and the saved action prefix up to R

is replayed before the forward agent resumes. For 

R = 0 , no prefix is replayed. 

Metric For each instance and each condition, we draw five independent continuations from the se-lected recovery state, and report Success@ K for 

K ∈ { 1, . . . , 5}. Each continuation begins with action-prefix replay to reconstruct the recovery state; because replay can occasionally desynchro-nize from the saved trajectory (e.g., due to UI tim-ing), we retry replay up to three times per con-tinuation. Success@ K measures whether at least one of the K successfully reconstructed continua-tions from the same recovery state completes the task, and reflects how much of the agent’s latent task-solving ability is exposed by recovery. 

B.2 End-to-End Evaluation Benchmark We evaluate on the OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) benchmark, where we follow a consis-tent setting as A.1. 

Forward agents We use three open-source mod-els as the forward agent: Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026a) with thinking mode, UI-TARS-1.5-7B (Qin et al., 2025), and EvoCUA-32B (Xue et al., 2026), following the setup in Section A.1, where default system prompts and instructions in Section A.1 are provided. Within each end-to-end experiment, the recovery agent shares the same backbone as the forward agent; the two differ only in their prompts and step budgets. For all forward agents we set 

max_steps to 50 . Temperatures are chosen from each model’s official inference recipe except for EvoCUA-32B: its default temperature of 0.0 pro-duces deterministic outputs, which would cause the forward agent to repeat the same action when it resumes from a past state, so we set it to 0.7 to pre-serve action diversity after recovery. The resulting values are 0.7 for EvoCUA-32B, 0.7 for Qwen3.5-9B-Thinking, and 1.0 for UI-TARS-1.5-7B. The 

max_tokens budget is 32768 for EvoCUA-32B (to accommodate its long reasoning trace), 3000 

for Qwen3.5-9B-Thinking, and 500 for UI-TARS-1.5-7B. 

Detectors Both the error-step detector and the recovery-step detector are LLM-based and share Qwen3.6-Plus (Qwen Team, 2026b) as their back-bone, accessed through the OpenRouter API 3 with a temperature of 0.7 and custom system prompts (Figure 36, 37). The error detector is queried once per forward step. Once a recovery is triggered, the error detector is frozen for the next 5 steps; this pre-vents the detector from re-interrupting the forward agent before any recovery-induced changes have a chance to take effect. 

Recovery agent The recovery agent is given a recovery step budget of 10 steps per attempt and is triggered at most 

max_recovery_iteration times per episode, with max_recovery_iteration

B.3 Human Evaluation 

When constructing the human evaluation sets, we sample recovery results from three representative models, including Claude-Sonnet-4.6, Qwen3.5-9B, and EvoCUA-32B, across both short- and long-horizon evaluation settings in OSWorld, as well as the short-horizon setting in WindowsAgentArena. We compare R ETRACE against three baselines: variants of our oracle evaluator with either RO

or RTC removed, and GPT-5.4, a state-of-the-art model with extensive computer-use knowledge. We collect cases where the evaluation decisions pro-duced by the oracle implemented in R ETRACE 

differ from those of the alternative evaluators, and randomly sample 50 instances for each variant, re-sulting in a total of 150 evaluation samples in Ta-ble 1. For the image-wise matching evaluator, we provide only the recovery target screenshot and the screenshot after the final recovery step. For the Transition-Consistent evaluator, success is de-termined solely based on whether replaying the remaining actions from the recovered state success-fully reaches the original task goal. For GPT-5.4, we provide the history of observation-action pairs leading up to the error step, the recovery trajec-tory generated by the recovery agent, the recovery target screenshot, the error-step screenshot, and the final recovered-state screenshot. GPT-5.4 is then prompted to determine whether the seman-tic meaning of the recovered state aligns with the target recovery state, following the same context and inputs provided to human annotators for a fair comparison (See Figure 38 for prompt details). After constructing the evaluation set, we split the 50 samples for each variant into two subsets and assigned two human annotators to each subset. In total, we recruited 12 annotators (4 annotators for each variant) who were researchers based in South Korea and highly proficient in computer-use tasks and voluntarily consented to participate in the study, with each annotator compensated approximately 20 USD for their participation, provided with de-tailed guideline for objective evaluation (Figure 11). Each pair of annotators independently eval-uated whether the recovery target state had been successfully restored, labeling each sample in a binary success-or-failure manner consistent with our evaluators, while optionally indicating whether their decision was made despite stochastic envi-ronment dynamics (e.g., different timestamps or pop-ups) when the recovery process was believed to be successful, allowing us to analyze the effect of such environment dynamics on oracle judgments in R ETRACE (Table 11). Using these annotations as reference labels, we compared the decisions from our oracle evaluator against those from the alternative evaluators and measured their alignment with human judgments. Specifically, we independently computed the win rate of our oracle evaluator against each baseline for every annotator pair, and report the average win rate across all four subsets per variant in Table 1. Across annotator pairs, we additionally observed an average agreement rate of 82% and a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.45 (Cohen, 1960).

Evaluation Objective - Evaluate whether the Final Recovery Screenshot is functionally equivalent to the Goal Screenshot. - Success (1): the environment is correctly restored - Failure (0): the environment is not properly restored Evaluation Principles 1. Prioritize Visual Similarity The primary signal is the visual similarity between the Final Recovery Screenshot and the Goal Screenshot. Please carefully compare the screenshots at afine-grained, segment-level detail, including interaction-dependent UI elements. Pay close attention to whether: - active windows or pages differ, - selected content or tabs change, - focused text boxes differ, - dropdowns or menus are opened differently, - cursor positions or hover states change, - highlighted or selected UI elements differ, - drag-and-drop states change, or other interaction-sensitive regions are inconsistent. Mark as Failure (0) if important visual or interaction-level differences remain. **Important** Minor differences caused by dynamic environments (e.g., notifications, pop-ups, timestamps, loading indicators, rendering variations) can be ignored if the underlying functional state remains equivalent. 2. Verify Functional Recovery The recovered state should allow the original task to continue correctly. Even if screenshots appear visually similar, mark as Failure (0) if:-- important interaction context is missing, - unintended changes remain, - incorrect files/pages/windows are active, or the operational state is not properly restored. Use the recovery trajectory and original trajectory to understand: - what actions changed the environment, - whether erroneous states were actually reverted, - and whether the recovery process is consistent with the intended goal state. Trajectory information is especially important when screenshots appear visually similar but the actual operational state may still differ.
