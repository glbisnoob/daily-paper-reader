Abstract 

Despite recent advances in CUAs, their development remains constrained by the limited di-versity, controllability, and reliability of interaction data. Moreover, conventional optimization pipelines lack iterative feedback, while existing recursive improvement approaches often rely on self-generated experiences, limiting ability expansion and yielding diminishing returns. To ad-dress these challenges, we introduce CUA-Master , a nested double-loop recursive improvement framework. At the system level, an outer world-data-model flywheel continuously incorporates open-world corpus and leverages evolving model weaknesses to guide subsequent data construc-tion and optimization iterations. At the data level, an inner flywheel couples open-world seeding and autocatalytic propagation. The former mines authentic workflows as seed tasks from in-structional videos, and employs visual-semantic dual-path trajectory reconstruction; The latter transforms these seeds into reusable primitives for controllable synthesis of diverse, complex, and verifiable tasks. To enable efficient and reliable trajectory collection, we further introduce a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry mechanism in various real environments. Extensive ex-periments demonstrate that CUA-Master-27B achieves competitive performance across GUI-only and GUI-CLI hybrid benchmarks, scoring 80.2 on OSWorld Verified, 36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 

on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents’ Last Exam. 1arXiv:2611.03126v1 [cs.AI] 2 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Computer-use agents (CUAs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for automating real-world workflows across diverse software applications, and unlocking new levels of digital productivity. Recent advances in both foundation models [3, 30, 32, 37, 40, 47] and specialized agents [9, 17, 28, 49, 51] have substantially improved their capabilities not only grounding of computer scenarios, but also in interactive reasoning and long-horizon execution. Besides, early CUAs operate predominantly via graphical user interfaces (GUIs), while subsequent works extend their capability scope by incorporating command-line interfaces (CLIs) [8, 24, 42, 45] and extensible tools [16, 19]. Nevertheless, developing powerful CUAs capable of reliably executing diverse, long-horizon, and cross-application workflows remains challenging. A fundamental bottleneck lies in how data construction and model optimization are coupled and recur-sively improved. Many existing pipelines follow a unidirectional paradigm of data generation followed by model training [18, 28, 49, 51], where data construction is insufficiently informed by the evolving weak-nesses. Consequently, additional data may provide lopsided benefits without addressing persistent capability gaps. Recent recursive improvement approaches partially address this issue by incorporating iteratively self-generated data [33, 50, 75]. However, their exploration remains closed-world optimization, where such cycles still predominantly rely on the own knowledge and capabilities of the model itself. It is bounded by existing capabilities, potentially reinforcing distributional biases, and yielding diminishing marginal returns. These observations fundamentally motivate us to build a recursive system that continuously acquires new open-world knowledge, and systematically expands the performance boundary of the whole system. Beyond system-level limitations, a more concrete concern is the availability of large-scale, high-quality data that adequately captures the diversity and complexity of real-world trajectories. While human-annotated tra-jectories provide high-quality supervision, their substantial annotation costs and limited application coverage make large-scale collection impractical [13, 20, 25, 50, 51]. Therefore, synthetic data generation emerges as a promising alternative [21, 39, 41, 44, 60]. However, existing approaches face three intertwined challenges. First, diversity : tasks derived from restricted and similar sources often exhibit substantial homogeneity, leading to insufficient ability coverage and fast improvement saturation as data volume increases. Second, 

> controllability

: direct instruction rewriting and template-based generation offer limited control over op-erational abilities, complexity, and task dependencies, particularly for long-horizon and cross-application workflows. Third, reliability : scaling the collection of verified trajectories across diverse real-world environ-ments becomes increasingly challenging as task complexity grows, leading to lower rollout success rates and frequent execution failures. Collectively, these limitations constrain not only the quantity of synthetic data, but also its effective coverage of the capabilities required by general-purpose CUAs. To address these limitations, we introduce CUA-Master, a nested double-loop recursive framework that integrates an outer world-data-model flywheel with an inner data flywheel. At the system level, the outer flywheel closes the loop among open-world exploration, data construction, and model optimization. By leveraging the evolving model’s capability gaps to guide subsequent exploration and data generation, while continuously incorporating external knowledge, it addresses both the unidirectionality of conventional training pipelines and the limited knowledge expansion of closed-world self-improvement. At the data level, the inner flywheel couples open-world seeding and autocatalytic propagation as two complementary driving forces. Open-world seeding mines diverse seed tasks from real-world instructional videos through hierarchical retrieval and filtering, and reconstructs their trajectories via a visual-semantic dual-path mechanism. The latter abstracts existing seeds into reusable primitives and composes them into verifiable tasks, with explicit control over task complexity and application scope. To improve rollout reliability and efficiency, we introduce a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry mechanism that preserves valid execution prefixes and recovers from localized failures without restarting entire rollouts. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of CUA-Master. With only 27B parameters, it achieves scores of 80.2 on OSWorld Verified, 36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents’ Last Exam, demonstrating competitive performance approaching markedly larger or closed-source models and strong execution efficiency. Our double-loop flywheel also substantially improves the diversity and coverage of data distribution, reliability and controllability of synthesis, and progressive improvements across iterations. 22 Methodology

2.1.2 Hybrid Action Space 

We adopt a unified atomic action space that supports both direct GUI, e.g. , computer.click /computer.scroll ,and structured CLI operations shell.exec . Each atomic action is represented as a by its action type and a dictionary containing its arguments. As listed in Table 7, the action space is partitioned into GUI and CLI actions, where the former consists of atomic mouse and keyboard operations, while the latter executes structured shell commands. This pattern allows a unified execution interface and can be interleaved within a trajectory, allowing the agent to select the action most appropriate for the current environment state. The environment returns observations o after execution to capture the action outcome, thus, GUI interactions are grounded through screenshots, while CLI interactions are grounded through standardized shell outputs containing the command output, error stream, and execution status. All pointer coordinates are represented in a resolution-independent normalized coordinate system: x, y ∈ [ 0, 1000 ]. In addition, our hybrid action space is also compatible with additional tool.call , affording flexible tool invocation capabilities.

We then programmatically derive additional variants by perturbing these images, e.g. , by removing randomly selected applications and reorganizing desktop layouts, thereby expanding environment coverage at low marginal cost. Each generated image is registered in a catalog and accompanied by a descriptor recording its operating-system type and version, and application inventory, including version and package metadata. 2.2 Recursive Flywheel 

2.2.1 Data Flywheel 

As illustrated by the black dashed box in Figure 2a, we formulate our methodology as a recursive flywheel driven by two complementary forces. Open-world seeding (Section 2.3) continuously injects real-world di-versity and operational knowledge by discovering underrepresented applications, operations, and workflows, mitigating the risks of closed-loop degeneration associated with purely self-generated data [11, 43, 73]. Au-tocatalytic propagation (Section 2.4) transforms these real-world seeds into reusable primitives, enabling composable task synthesis and reliable trajectory collection in executable environments. Together, these two forces establish a self-reinforcing cycle in which open-world seeding provides external knowledge and diversity and forms the necessary foundation, while autocatalytic propagation systematically 

4amplifies its coverage, complexity, and scale. 

2.2.2 World-Data-Model Flywheel 

Beyond the inner data flywheel, we establish a broader world-data-model flywheel. Starting from the open world, diverse applications, operations, and workflows are distilled and expanded into executable tasks and trajectories, providing grounded supervision for model training. As the model evolves, its emerging capa-bilities and remaining limitations provide feedback to guide subsequent open-world exploration, directing data acquisition and scaling toward underrepresented workflows and increasingly challenging tasks. Through this iterative cycle of world exploration, data construction, model improvement, and feedback-driven re-exploration, the agent progressively expands its capability frontier. 

2.3 Open-world Seeding 

As discussed above, one of the most prominent limitations lies in the insufficient richness of tasks. Open-world video resources, e.g. , YouTube and TikTok, contain a wealth of software demonstration videos, which can be leveraged to enrich task diversity. However, their supervision is mostly implicit, temporally diffuse, and often contaminated by non-operational content. We therefore introduce an open-world seeding pipeline that distills instructional video into both real-world seed task T seed and corresponding trajectory X seed .Compared to previous works [27, 57, 71], our key insight is to regard this process not merely as direct trajectory generation, but also as seed task collection and a foundation for further propagation .Besides, we introduce a visual-semantic dual-path reconstruction mechanism to substantially improve the accuracy of trajectory reconstruction. 

2.3.1 Corpus Harvesting 

We organize the open-world search space using a hierarchical application taxonomy as listed in Figure 2b, 2c, and Table 9, including 8 main categories, 36 subdomains, and over 650 applications; and a set of operation-oriented keywords, e.g. , “tutorial”, “how to”, “guide”, etc . For every application, we instantiate compositional queries to retrieve videos from the open-world internet video databases, namely YouTube, TikTok, and Bilibili. To remove low-quality and unpromising candidates, all retrieved videos are first deduplicated by source identity and normalized title, followed by metadata and noise filtering. Specifically, metadata gates enforce admissible video duration, upload recency, source availability, and taxonomy-consistent application attribution; a lightweight noise filter further suppresses promotional, entertainment, language-learning, and other incidental mentions of software that do not demonstrate CUA interactions. To further filter unsuitable candidates, the audio of uniformly sampled frames {f1, . . . , f n} are labeled by the Whisper-Large-v3 model through Faster-Whisper [38] with timestamped transcript and taxonomy metadata as { ˆf1, . . . , ˆfn}. Then, we use an annotator A, normally a more powerful model, as the score evaluator (details in Figure 11) to assign an overall CUA suitability score σ ∈ { 0, . . . , 5} by assessing whether the video presents a legible computer-use interaction and a reconstructible step-by-step interaction trajectory. It additionally predicts CUA presence, trajectory availability, and visual clarity, providing structured signals for subsequent corpus curation. 

2.3.2 Seed Task Mining 

A tutorial video may contain multiple workflows interleaved with non-CUA fragments that are not suitable for task collection. We therefore formulate seed task mining as multimodal temporal episode discovery. Given metadata, the timestamped transcript, and uniformly sampled overview frames {f1, . . . , f n}, we use an annotator A as task generator (details in Figure 12) to decompose and segment independent tasks from a complete video:

Each episode is assigned a concise goal inferred jointly from its visual context, narration, application identity, and demonstrated state transition. This transforms a coarse video-level corpus into task-level supervision.

2.4 Autocatalytic Propagation 

Open-world seeding introduces diverse real-world workflows, but the resulting seed tasks provide limited coverage of the combinatorial space of computer-use operations. Moreover, although annotation-verified, trajectories reconstructed from instructional videos may contain annotation noise and do not necessarily correspond to real executions in the target environments. Moreover, existing CUA task and trajectory synthesis methods often rely on rigid generation or rewriting strategies, potentially leading to homogeneous outputs [7, 28, 49, 61]. Other lines might suffer from unverifiable synthesis [21, 44, 55], and poorly reliable rollout pipeline [23, 25, 61], limiting the potential to systematically expand diverse and increasingly flexible workflows across applications. Therefore, we introduce autocatalytic propagation, which successively performs primitivization, composable task synthesis, and resilient trajectory collection. Our key insight is to treat the real-world rich application operations as composable primitives, which can be flexibly combined to generate tasks under fine-grained control , e.g. , single- or cross-application, and task complexity. Besides, the resilient trajectory collection framework with feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry mechanism notably enhances the success rate and efficiency of rollouts. 

2.4.1 Primitive-Based Composable Task Synthesis 

Directly generating or rewriting seed instructions provides limited control and quality over task semantics and potentially results in homogeneous tasks when scaling up. We instead primitivize each seed task T and its associated trajectory X into reusable, application-level ability primitive sequence, using the annotator A

as primitive extractor (details in Figure 16), which is represented as: 

(T seed , X seed ) A

Ð→ { p1, . . . , p n} , where pi = ( ui, a i, Pi, Ei, Vi) , (6) 6where n denotes the number of generated primitives, each of which is a five-tuple comprising the associated application u, the reusable ability a, execution preconditions P, environment requirements E, and verifier specification V, to formulate the operational dependencies required for execution and verification. Here, we follow and further improve the strategies from [28, 52] to automatically build corresponding E and V for each primitive. We aggregate the extracted primitives into an application-indexed primitive library B. Seman-tically equivalent primitives are canonicalized and deduplicated, while variants with distinct preconditions, environment requirements, or verification logic are retained. This representation aims to abstract away task-specific entities while preserving the operational dependencies necessary for reliable execution, enabling a primitive to be instantiated with different files, content, interface states, and application configurations. Task synthesis is formulated as constrained primitive composition. Given a target complexity budget K

and an application set {u1, . . . , u N }, task complexity can be explicitly controlled along two dimensions: the number of composed primitives K and the number of involved applications N , rather than through unconstrained linguistic rewriting. For each compatible composition, we use the annotator A to instantiate a synthetic task T syn (details in Figure 17) with a coherent instruction Isyn . It simultaneously composes their environment requirements {E 1, . . . , EK } into an executable environment specification Esyn and consolidates the primitive-level verifier specifications {V 1, . . . , VK } into a task-level verifier Vsyn :

Primitives with conflicting preconditions or incompatible environment states cannot be composed together. The resulting synthetic precondition is the conjunction of compatible primitive preconditions. When N = 1, a single-application task is generated, whereas N > 1 yields a cross-application task. 2.4.2 Resilient Trajectory Collection 

Simply discarding a failed rollout and restarting from scratch is inefficient, since a failure is often caused by a localized misoperation while most of the preceding trajectory remains valid. Besides, even successful tra-jectories may contain redundant and unnecessary actions, repeat observations, and verbose reasoning, intro-ducing unnecessary execution overhead and inefficient supervision. We therefore introduce a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry mechanism. For each arbitrary task T , the environment infrastructure (Section 2.1.3) first materializes an environment by selecting a compatible image from the VM pool and initializes necessary configurations to rollout trajectory. When verification fails or inefficiency is detected, the annotator A as detector (details in Figure 18) jointly examines the verifier residuals V, collected trajectory X , and executable environment E, thus the feedback is generated as: 

(T , X , E) A

Ð→ ( ˆs, F) , (8) where ˆs estimates the earliest state-divergence step, and F produces execution-grounded feedback. The feed-back describes the observed failures or inefficiency, without exposing a privileged action solution, alleviating potential distribution mismatch [29, 67]. Subsequently, we restore the environment to the latest valid snapshot preceding ˆs. Inspired and further modified by [6], rather than replaying whole history from the initial snapshot, we create incremental snapshots at stable boundaries. Each snapshot stores the VM state together with a lightweight state signature, including active applications, window layout, relevant files, and controller metadata. The agent policy Π preserves the stable interaction prefix, discards the undesirable suffix, and resumes execution conditioned on the diagnostic feedback as hints. This process continues until V reports pass or a predefined retry budget is exhausted. Only the successful and the most efficient branch is retained as the final trajectory X , while failed or inefficient suffixes are excluded to avoid introducing state-inconsistent supervision. Thus, retries form a lightweight branching search that reuses successful prefixes and significantly enhances the efficiency of large-scale rollouts. 72.5 Model Training 

2.5.1 Recipe 

As discussed in Section 2.2, we integrate model training into the recursive world-data-model flywheel, where each iteration comprises a complete model optimization. Specifically, preceding iterations employ SFT to progressively acquire diverse knowledge and capabilities. In each iteration, based on the in-house assessment of model, including domain- and application-level hierarchical failure cases and rollout success rates analyses, we identify the gaps and limitations of the current policy, guiding the next iteration of adaptive data scaling and the CUA ability frontier. As capability gains from SFT gradually saturate, we transition to RL in the final iteration to further enhance the task-level capability upper limit. As discussed, the synthesized tasks are built upon controllable capa-bility primitives, which renders native support for difficulty-level categorization. Combining this with task generation quality and rollout success rates, we curate a high-quality task collection for RL training. 

2.5.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) 

At the ith SFT iteration turn, we fine-tune the policy on verified trajectories ⋃it=1 (X seed t ∪ X syn t ) collected through both open-world seeding and autocatalytic propagation. Training follows an autoregressive objective over executable action sequences, conditioned on task instructions and interaction histories. Following previ-ous CUA works [26, 46, 61], to avoid potential optimization drift, each iteration of SFT is trained starting from the same base checkpoint, with the updated data from each iteration added to the original training set. 

2.5.3 Reinforcement Learning (RL) 

To break through the capability plateau, we further optimize the SFT-trained policy on the high-quality synthetic tasks (Section 2.4.1) through RL in real executable computer environments. Here, we mainly employ and optimize the efficient online RL framework for CUAs [28]. Leveraging the task-specific verifiers established during task synthesis, we derive outcome-based rewards directly from execution results, without additional manual reward annotation. This final stage builds upon broad operational competence and focuses on improving long-horizon decision-making, execution robustness, and task completion. 

3 Experiments 

3.1 Settings 

3.1.1 Baselines 

We compare CUA-Master against a diverse set of generalist and specialist models. The generalist baselines include open-source models: Qwen-3.5-27B/-397B-A17B [35], Qwen-3.6-27B [36], Qwen-3.8-27B/Max [37], Minimax-M3-428B-A23B [30], Kimi-K2.6-1T-A32B [31], and Kimi-K3-2.8T-A104B [47], as well as proprietary models: Seed-2.1-Pro [40], GPT-5.6-Luna/Sol [32], and Claude-Sonnet/Opus-5 [3, 4]. For specialist baselines, we consider OpenCUA-72B [51], GUI-Owl-1.5-32B [58], EvoCUA-1.5-32B [17], ScaleCUA-9B [28], Qwen-CUA-397B-A17B [26], and UI-Mate-27B [9], which are specifically developed for computer-use tasks. This comparison enables us to evaluate CUA-Master against both general foundation and dedicated CUA models. For fair comparison, we re-evaluate all baseline models under a consistent benchmark protocol, using the same environment configurations, action interfaces, and evaluation criteria, unless otherwise specified.

Besides, we additionally evaluate three cross-platform benchmarks: Win-dowsAgentArena [5], MacOSWorld [64], and MacAgentBench [14] , to assess cross-platform generalization. 3.1.3 Implementations 

For training, all experiments are conducted on 16 nodes, each with 8 NVIDIA-H200-141GB GPUs. Initialized from Qwen-3.5-27B [35], we first perform SFT (Section 2.5.2), adopting AdamW optimization with a peak learning rate of 5 × 10 −6, a cosine schedule, a warmup ratio of 0.03, and a batch size of 128. For RL training, we follow the efficient online RL framework of ScaleCUA [28], a modified GRPO-style algorithm for CUAs, with frontier sampling and visual context segmentation. For each task, we sample 8 trajectories in real VMs (Section 2.1.3) to estimate advantages from verifier rewards. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −6

and a batch size of 64, corresponding to up to 512 sampled trajectories per batch. For each SFT iteration, which trained on a cumulatively expanded dataset from the same initial checkpoint, we include 63K, 38K, 14K tasks and corresponding trajectories respectively, thus the total training data at the last SFT iteration is about 115K; while we use about 12K high-quality tasks for RL stage. More details of training data is reported in Appendix B. For inference, to allow sufficient exploration in CUA tasks, we keep the temperature of most models to 1, except the models with other recommended settings. We deploy all open-weight models with 1-node (for models under 100B parameters) or 2-node (for models between 100B and 1T parameters) schemes; each node is equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200-141GB GPUs, and the inference is optimized based on vLLM [22]. And we use 6-node optimized deployment scheme for K3. For closed-source models, we evaluate them by directly calling their official APIs.

3.2.1 CUA Ability Evaluation 

As shown in Table 1, CUA-Master-27B achieves scores of 80.2 on OSWorld Verified, 36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents’ Last Exam, respectively. Compared with UI-Mate-27B, a powerful similarly sized specialist, it consistently improves performance by 2.6, 12.9, 25.6, and 11.8 across the four benchmarks. As reported in Figure 4, against all the models derived from Qwen-3.5-27B, our model achieves comparable performance of Qwen-3.8-27B on GUI-only tasks, while delivering substantial gains on hybrid CUA scenarios. Notably, on the most challenging benchmark, it only lags behind GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude-Opus-5, and surpasses all other counterparts (when full-pass rate is equal, compare the average score). Despite its substantially smaller parameters, CUA-Master-27B achieves competitive performance against Qwen-CUA-397B-A17B on GUI-only benchmarks, while even outperforming it on hybrid benchmarks, respectively. 

3.2.2 Performance-efficiency Comparison 

As illustrated in Figure 3, CUA-Master-27B demonstrates a favorable performance-efficiency trade-off, illus-trating the faster model decision-making time and also superior interactive execution efficiency. On OSWorld Verified, it achieves a score of 80.2 in only 8 minutes, and on OSWorld 2.0, it slightly surpasses Qwen-3.8-27B (36.9 vs. 36.5) while reducing runtime by 24%. The efficiency advantage becomes particularly pronounced on challenging hybrid benchmarks. On WeaveBench, CUA-Master achieves a competitive score in 34 min-utes, matching GPT-5.6-Luna and with 28% less runtime. Similarly, on Agents’ Last Exam, it matches the performance of Kimi-K3 and Qwen-3.8-Max while requiring 34-36% less time.

3.3 Additional Analyses 

3.3.1 Effectiveness of Hybrid Action Space 

To investigate the effectiveness of the hybrid action space, we additionally compare CUA-Master under GUI-only, CLI-only, and hybrid execution modes. As shown in Figure 6, hybrid execution substantially improves both task performance and execution efficiency, particularly on complex benchmarks. On WeaveBench, the hybrid mode achieves a score of 43.9, outperforming GUI-only (6.6) and CLI-only (11.5) by 37.3 and 32.4 points, respectively, while reducing execution steps by 23.8% compared to GUI-only. Especially on the more long-horizon, complex Agents’ Last Exam benchmark, hybrid execution improves the score from 5.9 to 25.0 over GUI-only, with 26.7% fewer steps; on OSWorld 2.0, it achieves competitive scores while reducing execution steps by 43.8%. As demonstrated in Figure 5, the visualization of trajectories further illustrates the complementary strengths of GUI and CLI actions: CLI enables file processing, batch computation, structured data manipulation, and other efficient operations, while GUI supports visual inspection and interactive application operations. 3.3.2 Analysis of Data Flywheel 

We analyze the effectiveness of our data flywheel from two perspectives: task diversity and data construc-tion efficiency. As illustrated in Figure 7, the t-SNE visualization suggests that tasks generated by our data flywheel exhibit broad coverage in the embedding space, spanning multiple distributions of existing CUA datasets: AgentNet [51], VideoCUA [20], ScaleCUA [28], WildGUI [57], CUA-Gym [49], and ProCUA-SFT [21], while extending into sparsely covered regions. Furthermore, as reported in Table 3, the rollout success rates consistently surpass those of the baseline, which utilizes naive environments to rollout with the same retry budget but re-rollout from the start, across all difficulty levels. Notably, when synthesizing tasks with K = 40 primitives across N = 3 applications, which is comparable to the average difficulty of Agents’ Last Exam, our method achieves a nearly 50% success rate compared to 14.6% for the baseline. The advantage remains substantial under the most challenging configurations, achieving 18.7% versus 0.0% at K/N = 50 /8,which involves more primitives and applications than the extreme cases in Agents’ Last Exam. These results demonstrate that our data flywheel achieves both broad task coverage and a substantially higher yield of successful rollouts, enabling reliable and efficient construction of diverse, challenging data. 

3.3.3 Analysis of World-data-model Flywheel 

We analyze the effectiveness of our world-data-model flywheel from two perspectives. As shown in Figure 8, successive training iterations progressively improve performance across all four benchmarks, with OSWorld Verified and WeaveBench increasing by over 25 points. Notably, when SFT improvements on relatively simple tasks approach saturation, the final RL stage further boosts performance on OSWorld 2.0 and Agents’ Last Exam, which are more challenging and demand stronger exploration, by approximately 15 points, respectively. Moreover, as illustrated in Figure 9, stronger annotation models consistently yield greater performance gains throughout the iterative process. The self-policy strategy, i.e. , using the model from each training iteration as the annotator to generate data for the next iteration, can bring about some improvements, but its gains are relatively limited. These results highlight the effectiveness of our flywheel in establishing a positive feedback loop that progressively enhances computer-use capabilities, particularly on challenging long-horizon tasks. 

4 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce CUA-Master, a nested double-loop recursive framework that advances CUA systems beyond unidirectional data pipelines and closed-world self-improvement. At the system level, the world-data-model flywheel continuously incorporates open-world knowledge and leverages evolving model weaknesses to guide further iterations. At the data level, we establish a complementary flywheel that cou-ples open-world seeding with autocatalytic propagation, which integrates visual-semantic trajectory recon-struction, primitive-based task composition, and feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retries to enable diverse, controllable, and reliable data synthesis. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CUA-Master-27B achieves competitive performance across GUI-only and GUI-CLI hybrid benchmarks, with strong execution efficiency and consistent improvements across recursive iterations. Appendix A Related Works 

A.1 CUA Specialists 

Research on CUAs has progressed from visual grounding to autonomous interaction and long-horizon task execution. Early works, including OS-Atlas [54], Tong-UI [71], and CogAgent [15], establish foundational GUI perception and grounding capabilities. Building upon these advances, Aguvis [59], UI-TARS-2 [34], Agent S [1], and MAI-UI [74] integrate visual understanding, reasoning, and action planning for autonomous GUI interaction. To further improve performance in complex environments, OpenCUA [51], CUA-Gym [49], EvoCUA-1.5 [17], ScaleCUA [28], UI-Mate [9], and UI-Venus-2 [48] advance reliable data synthesis and specialized training paradigms. More recently, research has expanded beyond GUI-only interaction toward hybrid action spaces. UI-TARS-2 [50] incorporates terminal and file-system operations, while ToolCUA [16] and HybridCUA [8] coordinate GUI actions with structured tools and command-line interfaces. Collectively, these advances mark a shift from fine-grained visual grounding toward scalable, practical, and reliable CUAs. 

A.2 Scalable Data Synthesis 

Scalable data synthesis has emerged as a key paradigm for overcoming the scarcity of high-quality computer-use trajectories. One line of research leverages naturally occurring interaction data and instructional resources. OpenCUA [51], WildGUI [57], and VideoCUA [20] exploit real-world interaction traces or videos, while AgentTrek [60] and TongUI [71] transform web tutorials into agent supervision. Another line synthesizes tasks and trajectories through environment interaction. OS-Genesis [44] adopts reverse task synthesis, whereas AutoPlay [39] discovers executable tasks through environment exploration. CUA-Gym [49], EvoCUA [61], and ScaleCUA [28] further scale supervision through verifiable task synthesis, large-scale environment interaction, and iterative policy optimization. Despite these advances, jointly achieving real-world diversity, controllable task complexity, reliable verification, and efficient execution remains challenging. Our work unifies open-world workflow acquisition with primitive-based compositional synthesis, enabling systematic expansion from authentic operational knowledge to diverse, verifiable, and increasingly complex computer-use tasks. 

A.3 Recursive Improvements 

Recursive improvement aims to expand agent capabilities through iterative data generation, experience re-finement, and policy optimization. Early approaches such as Re-ReST [11] and Agent-R [70] exploit envi-ronmental feedback and iterative refinement to improve agent trajectories. Moving toward adaptive task generation, WebRL [33] introduces self-evolving curricula driven by failed executions, and Self-Challenging Agents [75] jointly develop task generation and policy learning through self-generated, verifiable challenges. B Details of Training Data 

Our training data are constructed through open-world seeding and autocatalytic propagation, yielding a dataset of 127K samples (115K for SFT training and 12K for RL training). To ensure diverse applica-tion coverage, we organize data construction around a hierarchical taxonomy encompassing 8 domains, 36 subdomains, and over 650 applications. As illustrated in Figure 10a, the taxonomy spans Business & Admin-istration (18.4%), Creative Production (16.5%), Daily Life (13.4%), Developer Tools (11.4%), Engineering (10.6%), Data & Analytics (10.3%), Productivity (10.1%), and Science & Research (9.4%). This broad coverage supports the acquisition of heterogeneous operational skills across both everyday and specialized workflows. As shown in Figure 10b, the training data distribution evolves across successive iterations rather than following a fixed domain mixture. Compared with the initial SFT stage, subsequent iterations increase the relative representation of Software Development, Science & Research, and Productivity, while reducing the initial concentration in Business & Administration. The final RL stage adopts a more balanced distribution across domains. This adaptive reallocation aligns data construction with evolving model capability gaps while maintaining broad application coverage.

E Limitations 

Despite its effectiveness, CUA-Master still has two main limitations. First, the recursive improvement pipeline incurs substantial computational and infrastructure costs, particularly from large-scale environment interac-tions, trajectory collection, and iterative model optimization. Although our snapshot-rollback mechanism improves rollout efficiency, it requires large-scale extra support for the deployment of VMs, leading to chal-lenges for infrastructure. Second, the upper limit of the quality of synthesized data relies on capable annotator models, with stronger annotators consistently yielding greater performance gains.
