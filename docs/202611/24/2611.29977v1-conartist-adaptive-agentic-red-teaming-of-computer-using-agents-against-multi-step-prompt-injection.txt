Abstract—Harnesses secure Computer-Using Agents by enforc-
ing security constraints and defending against prompt injection
attacks. However, we find that two core security mechanisms of
agent harnesses remain vulnerable. First, consistency checking
prevents agents from deviating from legitimate user objec-
tives, while attackers can bypass it by embedding malicious
actions that appear logically consistent with legitimate task
execution. Second, risk auditing proactively examines high-risk
operations. Yet, the auditing process itself would introduce
additional exploitable actions that violate security boundaries.
To evaluate above defects we found, we propose Conartist,
a cascaded adaptive prompt injection framework comprising
two progressive stages. First, Conartist employs a goal-pollution
strategy to establish deceptive causal dependencies between
legitimate user objectives and malicious operations, progres-
sively embedding attack steps into legitimate task workflows.
However, these task-coupled attacks remain susceptible to risk
auditing. To address this limitation, Conartist further analyzes
security-auditing feedback from controlled attack exploration
to identify exploitable auditing patterns. Conartist then adap-
tively refines its deception strategies to either avoid triggering
risk auditing or induce additional high-risk operations. Ex-
tensive experiments across five backbone models demonstrate
the effectiveness of Conartist, achieving attack success rates of
32.12% on 576-instance tool use benchmark and 66.67% in
the Reddit GUI benchmark, outperforming the SOTA attack
by 14.41 and 29.17, respectively. By analyzing one of the
most popular Agent, Openclaw, we furthermore concluded
seven realistic exploitable attack patterns, which reveals that
security mechanisms designed to prevent malicious actions can
themselves be manipulated to facilitate attacks.

1. Introduction
Computer-Using Agents (CUAs) have demonstrated im-
pressive capabilities in autonomously completing multi-step
tasks, such as file operations, email correspondence, or web
browsing, by directly controlling users’ computers [6], [50].
By automating routine computer interactions, these agents
can free users from tedious operational tasks and enable
them to focus on higher-level goals and creative work [5],
[16], [31], [32]. However, such powerful capabilities also in-
troduce new attack surfaces. These agents are often granted
overly broad permissions over users’ computers, while many
complex tasks require users to share sensitive personal in-
formation, such as login credentials, financial details, or
card information. Meanwhile, they interact with external
sources where malicious instructions may be embedded in
content or tool outputs. As AI agents struggle to distinguish
between instructions and data [8], [20], [21], they become
susceptible to prompt injection attacks, where seemingly
harmless instructions can gradually lead to harmful actions.
Recent studies have demonstrated the vulnerability of
CUAs to prompt injection attacks [14], [15], [21], [26],
[27]. These findings highlight the emerging threat of multi-
step prompt injection attacks. Rather than directly exposing
malicious intent through a single overt instruction, attackers
can conceal their objectives across a sequence of seemingly
benign interactions, gradually influencing the agent’s behav-
ior and ultimately inducing harmful actions. For instance, an
attacker may first instruct an agent to create a shell script,
then embed commands for deleting sensitive files into the
script, and finally induce the agent to execute it. While each
step may appear benign when considered in isolation, their
combination can result in unauthorized file deletion, making
such attacks difficult to detect through individual-step safety
checks.
Existing studies have proposed various methods for
multi-step prompt injection attacks. Traditional single-step

attacks typically present malicious instructions directly,
making their intent readily apparent and potentially trig-
gering refusals from safety-aligned models. To improve
attack stealthiness, recent studies have explored multi-step
attack strategies. For example, StepJack [26] decomposes
malicious objectives into a sequence of seemingly benign
substeps, while STAC [47] combines individually innocuous
tool operations into potentially harmful execution chains.
However, these approaches primarily rely on preconstructed
sequences of steps or tool operations, which may be dis-
rupted when the agent triggers security checks during crit-
ical operations. More recently, researchers have explored
feedback-driven adaptive attack optimization [23], [46]. For
instance, IterInject [36] iteratively refines injection payloads
based on attack feedback, while MUZZLE [12] leverages
agent execution trajectories and failure feedback to adapt
attack strategies. Although these methods have improved
attack stealthiness and adaptability to some extent, they have
not sufficiently considered the proactive behavioral security
auditing performed by agents during task execution.
Our motivating examples in Fig. 1 reveal two key ob-
servations. First, agents may make different safety decisions
about the same high-risk operation depending on its rele-
vance to the user’s task. As shown in M1, an agent that
initially refused to execute a destructive script proceeded
when the script was presented as part of a task-related repair
procedure. Second, proactive security auditing can introduce
unexpected agent behaviors. In M2, an agent investigating
a suspicious request independently inspected processes and
source code beyond its designated working scope, revealing
potential attack opportunities despite ultimately refusing
the request. These observations motivate our exploration of
task-coupled deception and weaknesses in agents’ security-
auditing behavior.
Designing Conartist presents three key challenges.
C1: Construction of Task-Coupled Deceptive At-
tacks. The first challenge is to automatically generate multi-
step deceptive attacks that are tightly coupled with task
execution. Unlike single-step prompt injections, multi-step
deceptive attacks require integrating user tasks with a series
of interdependent deceptive steps, progressively constructing
coherent and credible causal chains to influence the agent’s
safety judgments about subsequent actions. However, estab-
lishing such causal relationships is inherently challenging.
Attackers must not only understand the task’s execution
logic but also coordinate dependencies among multiple de-
ceptive steps, allowing deceptive premises to be naturally
integrated into the task execution process.
C2: Adaptive Deception under Dynamic Execution
and Security Auditing. The second challenge is to adapt
multi-step deception strategies to the dynamic execution of
CUAs. On the one hand, agents plan and execute tasks by
interacting with dynamic environments through successive
tool invocations, making it difficult for attackers to predeter-
mine a complete deception chain. On the other hand, CUAs
may identify and question certain deceptive premises dur-
ing execution, potentially disrupting the causal relationships
within the original deception chain. More importantly, CUAs
may proactively initiate security auditing upon detecting
suspicious operations, making further deception challenging
as the agent has already recognized potential risks. Nev-
ertheless, such auditing behaviors may expose new attack
opportunities. Therefore, the key challenge is to dynami-
cally adapt deceptive steps based on observable execution
feedback while identifying and exploiting potential weak-
nesses in security-auditing behaviors, even under heightened
security awareness.
C3: Offline-to-Online Strategy Transfer under Lim-
ited Observability. The final challenge concerns transfer-
ring attack strategies from offline environments to online
settings under limited observability. Existing adaptive red-
teaming approaches typically rely on the target agent’s
execution trajectories and intermediate feedback to itera-
tively optimize attack strategies [12], [36], [47]. In practice,
attackers typically cannot observe other users’ task execu-
tion trajectories, nor can they access the agent’s execution
trajectories or input/output data in real time. Furthermore,
discrepancies between offline and real-world environments,
uncertainties in online injection timing, and difficulties in
adapting attack payloads to the target task context pose
additional challenges.
In this paper, we propose Conartist, an adaptive agentic
red-teaming framework for computer-using agents against
multi-step prompt injection attacks. Conartist builds task-
related deception chains by introducing misleading premises
that establish plausible connections between user tasks and
malicious actions. Rather than relying on fixed attack se-
quences, it adapts these premises and subsequent steps based
on the agent’s execution feedback. Conartist further ana-
lyzes proactive security-auditing behavior to uncover poten-
tial weaknesses, turning observed verification conditions and
refusal reasons into guidance for refining deception strate-
gies. Through automated attack generation, execution, and
evaluation, Conartist continuously improves its strategies
and distills reusable deception patterns from prior attempts.
Furthermore, we introduce ClawBench, an OpenClaw-
based benchmark for evaluating prompt injection attacks
against computer-using agents. ClawBench comprises 11
scenarios, 80 user tasks, and 576 attack instances spanning
30 attack goals and eight security risk categories from the
OWASP Agentic AI Top 10 [11]. It provides an isolated
sandbox with 40 MCP tools and simulated external services,
enabling reproducible attack execution and automated eval-
uation through environment state snapshots.
We evaluate Conartist against multiple computer-using
agents across tool-use and GUI environments, using Claw-
Bench and the Reddit scenario from the publicly available
RedTeamCUA [27] benchmark. On ClawBench, Conartist
achieves an attack success rate of 32.12%, outperforming
the strongest competing method by 14.41 percentage points.
On the Reddit scenario, Conartist achieves a 66.67% attack
success rate, exceeding the strongest competing method
by 29.17 percentage points. Beyond attack effectiveness,
our analysis identifies 7 potentially exploitable security-
auditing patterns and distills 7 reusable deception strate-
gies, providing insights into how adaptive deception exploits


weaknesses in agents’ security behavior.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose Conartist, an adaptive red-teaming frame-
work that constructs task-related multi-step deception
chains and exploits potential weaknesses in agents’
proactive security auditing. By leveraging execution
feedback and security-auditing analysis, Conartist con-
tinuously refines its attack strategies.
• We develop ClawBench, an OpenClaw-based bench-
mark comprising 11 scenarios, 80 user tasks, and 576
attack instances, covering 30 attack goals and eight
OWASP Agentic AI security risk categories. It provides
an isolated sandbox with 40 MCP tools for reproducible
attack evaluation.
• We evaluate Conartist across multiple agent models in
both tool-use and GUI environments. Results demon-
strate higher attack success rates than existing multi-
step prompt injection methods, highlighting its effec-
tiveness across diverse agent settings.
1.1. Backgrounds
Computer-Using
Agents.
Computer-Using
Agents
(CUAs) are LLM-based autonomous agents that interact
with computer environments to accomplish user-specified
tasks
[4], [6]. Unlike conventional LLM applications that
primarily generate textual responses, CUAs integrate model
reasoning with external tool execution through iterative
observation-reasoning-action
cycles
[1].
They
operate
across web browsers, graphical user interfaces (GUIs), file
systems, and terminals, enabling complex task execution
with limited human intervention
[2], [3], [7]. During
execution, CUAs frequently process external content, such
as web pages, emails, documents, and tool responses.
Since such content may originate from untrusted sources, it
introduces potential security risks when agents incorporate
external information into subsequent decisions and actions.
Multi-Step Prompt Injection. Prompt injection refers
to attacks that manipulate LLM behavior by introducing
malicious instructions into model inputs [10], [19]. In indi-
rect prompt injection, attackers embed malicious instructions
within lower-trust content encountered by agents during
legitimate task execution [9], [21], [22], [49]. Unlike single-
step attacks that attempt to induce an immediate deviation,
multi-step prompt injection distributes malicious instruc-
tions or behavioral manipulations across multiple execu-
tion stages. Individual actions may appear benign or task-
relevant, while their cumulative effects can lead to unautho-
rized operations, information disclosure, or deviations from
legitimate user objectives [35].

tacks. The preceding observations suggest that attack effec-
tiveness depends on task context, injection timing, and the
agent’s execution state. However, existing adaptive attacks
typically rely on continuous execution feedback, which is
unavailable to external adversaries in realistic black-box
settings. Controlled offline exploration can reveal relation-
ships among deception strategies, execution contexts, and
security-auditing outcomes. We therefore seek to transfer
these insights to online attacks, enabling effective strategy
selection without real-time access to victim execution traces
or security-auditing feedback. Motivated by these obser-


vations, we introduce ConArtist, an adaptive red-teaming
framework that constructs task-coupled deceptive premises,
explores potential weaknesses in proactive security auditing,
and transfers reusable attack strategies from offline discov-
ery to online execution under limited observability.
2. Conartist Design
2.1. Threat Model
We consider a realistic external adversary who can legiti-
mately use publicly available CUAs, like OpenClaw, Codex,
and Claude Code [17], [24], [25].
We consider a realistic black-box adversary operating
in two distinct phases: offline vulnerability discovery and
online attack execution.
During discovery, the adversary can obtain observable
information from the agent’s execution traces and the LLM’s
inputs and outputs, but cannot access internal configura-
tions such as its internal implementation, underlying model
information, or runtime permissions. In online mode, the
adversary cannot access this information or the user’s task
trajectories. The adversary aims to use covert, multi-step
indirect prompt injection to gradually trick the CUA into
performing actions that contradict the user’s intent or ex-
ceed established permission boundaries, thereby resulting
in the leakage of sensitive information, the execution of
unintended or harmful actions, damage to system resources.
Furthermore, the impact may persist into subsequent ses-
sions through memory mechanisms or manifest during the
execution of future tasks.
Adaptive attack framework: We select OpenClaw [17]
as our CUA framework for its open-source implementation
and deployability, which facilitate reproducible experiments
in a controlled evaluation environment. Unlike the custom
agent environments used in existing benchmarks [13], [49],
OpenClaw [17] provides an actual agent execution workflow
and tool-interaction mechanisms, enabling us to evaluate
the impact of indirect prompt injection on multi-step tasks
under conditions closer to real-world deployment. To further
ensure fair comparisons with existing attack methods, we
additionally evaluate Conartist on the publicly available,
realistic RedTeamCUA benchmark [27].
To balance experimental realism and security, we deploy
local mock services on localhost to emulate external inter-
action environments, such as third-party websites and email
systems, and use sandbox isolation to confine experiments
to a controlled environment.
We assume that the Red Team system interacts with
the target CUA in a black-box manner, influencing the
target agent solely through the content channels accessible
in each evaluation scenario. Based on behavioral feedback
and execution results obtained during controlled evaluations,
the Red Team system analyzes the observable behavior of
the target CUA and adaptively adjusts its attack strategy
accordingly. The Red Team system cannot directly access
the target’s internal model parameters, nor can it modify its
internal implementation or security configurations.
2.2. Overview
As illustrated in Fig. 2, ConArtist consists of two stages:
(I) Offline Discovery and (II) Online Execution. The offline
stage constructs multi-step task-consistent deception strate-
gies through automated attack exploration and identifies
exploitable behaviors in security auditing. The online stage
transfers the discovered strategies to black-box environments
under restricted observability.
(I) Offline Discovery. ConArtist first identifies candidate
injection surfaces from legitimate user tasks and benign
execution trajectories, and employs a state machine to guide
iterative deception strategy evolution. As shown in Fig. 2,
given legitimate task information I1, the framework gener-
ates deceptive information P1 to induce the victim agent to
incorporate it into its task context, thereby achieving infor-
mation pollution. Building upon the adopted false premise,
ConArtist generates a subsequent attack payload E1 that
presents the attacker-desired action a1 as a necessary step
toward completing the legitimate task, thereby deceiving
model-level task-consistency checks. However, when the
attack progresses to a high-risk operation a2, the harness
may still reject its execution through security auditing, even
if the agent has accepted its apparent task relevance.
To address this limitation, the state machine tracks in-
formation pollution, attack execution, and security-auditing
feedback to guide subsequent strategy refinement. Be-
yond modifying deceptive premises and attack payloads,
ConArtist further explores exploitable auditing behaviors.
Fig. 2 illustrates two representative patterns: Evidence
Forgery, which supplies fabricated supporting evidence to
influence auditing decisions when required information is
insufficient, and Check-as-Landing, which turns auditing-
induced verification actions into attack landing points.
Through iterative execution, feedback analysis, and strategy
refinement, the framework distills attack trajectories, audit-
ing records, and strategy evolution outcomes into reusable
deception strategies.
(II) Online Execution. ConArtist transfers the discov-
ered deception strategies to black-box environments through
a rule-based strategy engine. Based on attacker-observable
context and historical attack effectiveness, the engine selects
applicable strategies and instantiates corresponding payloads
for deployment. Unlike offline discovery, online execution
does not rely on privileged victim execution traces or real-
time security-auditing feedback. Instead, it leverages accu-
mulated offline experience to select and deploy strategies
under restricted observability, enabling their transfer and
reuse in realistic black-box settings.

2.4. Adaptive Deception
Given a user task T, an attack goal G, a benign ex-
ecution trace τ0, identified candidate injection surfaces P,
attack constraints C, a historical experience library L, and
an interaction budget M, Conartist initializes an attack
instance in a controlled environment. Rather than generating
a fixed attack sequence, Conartist dynamically constructs
and exploits deceptive premises based on the victim agent’s
observable execution feedback.
Deceptive Premise Construction. We define a deceptive
premise as task-relevant information designed to introduce
confusion or misperceptions and establish a plausible causal
connection between the user task and subsequent attack
steps. Rather than explicitly instructing the victim agent
to perform malicious actions, deceptive premises typically
take the form of plausible factual statements that the agent
may incorporate into its understanding of the user task and
subsequent decisions.
Using the candidate injection surfaces and correspond-
ing World Edit operations identified in the preceding At-
tack Surface Identification stage, Conartist injects deceptive
premises into attacker-controllable resources that the victim
agent is likely to encounter during execution, such as task-
related files and webpages. All modifications must remain
within the attacker’s permitted control scope and satisfy
predefined attack constraints.
However, injecting a deceptive premise does not guaran-
tee its intended effect. A premise may never be encountered,
fail to influence the victim agent’s behavior, or be challenged
during security auditing. To address these uncertainties,
Conartist allows premises to be constructed and revised


throughout task execution rather than requiring all premises
to be injected beforehand.
Conartist
distinguishes
two
core
behaviors:
Pollu-
tion and Exploitation. Pollution introduces new deceptive
premises through World Edit operations, either before or
during the victim agent’s execution of the user task. Ex-
ploitation builds subsequent attack steps upon established
and unconsumed premises, progressively establishing causal
dependencies between deceptive steps and the ongoing user
task to steer the victim agent toward the attack goal. These
behaviors do not follow a predetermined sequence. In-
stead, Conartist employs a rule-based state machine to track
premise usage and execution feedback, enabling adaptive
transitions between Pollution and Exploitation.
Premise-Aware State Tracking. Conartist maintains
two complementary states: an attacker state St and a de-
fender state Dt. The attacker state tracks established and
consumed premises, invalidated injection carriers and strat-
egy structures, and previously addressed refusal grounds.
The defender state captures observable execution behavior,
including security-auditing evidence, refusal grounds, and
tool-execution traces.
Each deceptive premise is assigned an LLM-generated
symbolic identifier. Let Bt and Kt denote the sets of es-
tablished and consumed premises, respectively. The set of
available premises is defined as

To assess premise effectiveness, Conartist records two
observation signals: content exposure and behavioral align-

to identify observable evidence that the victim’s subsequent
responses are consistent with incorporating the premise into
the user task context. These signals provide complementary
evidence of potential premise influence without assuming
access to the agent’s internal beliefs.

subsequent strategies to reduce suspicion. Repeated refusal

these patterns exploit security-auditing behavior through
concrete case studies in the evaluation.
2.5. Offline-to-Online Strategy Transfer
To transfer offline attack experience to online environ-
ments, ConArtist employs a rule-based strategy machine
that distills discovered deceptive patterns into structured
strategies. Each strategy comprises (1) applicability condi-
tions, including attack scenarios, goal types, and injection
locations; (2) operation templates for deceptive informa-
tion injection, suspicion mitigation, and security-auditing
exploitation; and (3) historical performance, including ex-
ploration counts, attack success rates, and security refusal
information.
Observation Constraints. To reflect realistic black-
box attacker capabilities, ConArtist defines a three-level
observation contract: L1 (Self-Action Observation) confirms
attacker-initiated actions, such as payload deployment; L2
(Attacker-Channel Feedback) captures victim interactions
observable through attacker-controlled channels, such as link
visits or emails; and L3 (Privileged Execution Feedback)
covers internal execution traces and refusal reasons, which
are excluded from online decision-making.
Rule-Based Strategy Selection and Adaptation. The
strategy machine filters candidates by observable attack
context and attacker objective, then selects the highest-
scoring strategy using deterministic scores based on histor-
ical exploration counts, success rates, and contextual simi-
larity. It instantiates the corresponding template and deploys
the payload through an attacker-controlled surface. Without
L2 feedback, strategies are executed according to offline-
derived rules, with outcomes collected only after execution.
When L2 feedback is available, the machine applies
three predefined rules: (1) No response: If no interaction
occurs within a predefined window, it attempts a different
injection location. (2) Response without expected follow-up:
It retains the strategy and advances the operation, applying
suspicion mitigation or terminating if the expected interac-
tion remains absent. (3) Expected interaction observed: It
terminates execution and records the strategy.
This mechanism supports strategy adaptation under lim-
ited external observability, without requiring access to the
victim’s internal execution.

openclaw Sandbox. We construct an isolated sandbox

was originally designed for web agents, we retain its core

environments considered in our evaluation. Implementation

Analysis of Baseline Methods. We further analyze the
failure trajectories of existing baselines. StepJack decom-
poses malicious objectives into multiple seemingly benign
steps connected by prerequisite constraints. However, these
dependencies are primarily constructed around predefined
attack steps, offering limited adaptability to the evolving
state of legitimate task execution. In our experiments, ap-
proximately 29%–35% of attacks fail to generate effec-
tive payloads during the planning stage. Against GPT-5.4,
StepJack achieves an overall ASR of only 3.47%, with a
task utility of 55.21%. Among 401 cases in which attack
payloads were successfully delivered, 298 (74.3%) fail to
induce the intended malicious operations. These findings
suggest that although StepJack’s prerequisite steps may di-
vert agents from their original tasks, such deviations do not
necessarily sustain subsequent high-risk operations. When
the attack eventually involves unauthorized access, sensitive


information handling, or other operations subject to security
scrutiny, attack steps that fail to maintain consistency with
legitimate task execution are likely to be blocked. Con-
sequently, early-stage behavioral deviations rarely translate
into successful attacks.
In contrast, MUZZLE dynamically optimizes context-
aware attack payloads based on execution feedback, achiev-
ing better preservation of legitimate task execution. How-
ever, it does not explicitly incorporate strategies for address-
ing agent-initiated proactive security auditing. Our failure-
trajectory analysis reveals that approximately 62.9% of
failed cases involve security auditing behaviors, including
the identification and verification of injected instructions,
operational risks, or information trustworthiness. These find-
ings indicate that even when attack payloads adapt to the
task context, proactive security auditing can remain a critical
barrier to completing subsequent malicious operations.
To address these limitations, ConArtist dynamically in-
troduces and refines deceptive premises, progressively in-
tegrating malicious operations into the execution logic of
legitimate tasks. Furthermore, ConArtist leverages auditing
feedback collected during offline exploration to identify ex-
ploitable auditing patterns and adapt its deception strategies
accordingly. This enables ConArtist to achieve higher attack
success rates across diverse victim models and execution
environments.
RQ2: Effectiveness of Risk Auditing Exploitation. As
shown in Fig. 4, we measure the proportion of attacks in
which accepted deceptive premises are subsequently blocked
by proactive security auditing across attack rounds. This
proportion increases with iterative deception refinement,
indicating that security auditing becomes a major obstacle to
attack success. However, we identify exploitable weaknesses
in credibility assessment, auditing actions, audit coverage,
and execution timing. By analyzing auditing behaviors
encountered during failed attempts, ConArtist categorizes
these weaknesses into seven exploitable patterns. Among
the 185 successful attacks, 144 (77.84%) bypassed proactive
security auditing. We present seven representative cases to
illustrate these weaknesses and their exploitation.
P1: Audit-Enhanced Credibility. This pattern induces
agents to verify fabricated information, reinforcing its cred-
ibility for reuse in subsequent tasks. In SC02 (Email), the
attacker forges a business email claiming that an external
vendor collects acknowledgments for AWS key rotation.
After verifying this procedure, the agent adopts the attacker-
specified address as a legitimate acknowledgment channel
and reuses it in subsequent tasks, causing information leak-
age.
P2: Audit-Expanded Attack Surface. This pattern ex-
ploits additional information access introduced by auditing
to induce unauthorized operations. In SC03 (Database), the
agent must verify account and order records and log the
results in audit_log. The attacker plants a fabricated
payment-token anomaly in orders, inducing the agent to
access the sensitive api_keys table during verification.
Although the agent eventually detects the injection, the
unauthorized read has already occurred.

influence later actions. In SC06 (File System), the attacker

predict the actual tasks performed by victim agents. In both
settings, a rule-based strategy machine selects and deploys
deception strategies accumulated during offline exploration,
without relying on the victim agent’s internal execution
traces or security refusal feedback.

4. Related Work
Multi-Step Prompt Injection Attacks. Existing stud-
ies have investigated prompt injection attacks against tool-
using agents and CUAs, including tool-selection manipu-
lation [22] and memory poisoning [29], [30], [33], [34].
Multi-turn jailbreak and automated red-teaming methods
have also explored progressive attack construction [44],
[48]. MT-AgentRisk [45] examines security risks arising
from multi-turn agent interactions, while AgentLAB [28]
investigates vulnerabilities in long-horizon agent execution.
AgentHarm [18] evaluates agent misuse through explicitly
harmful requests, whereas our work investigates indirect
prompt injection through task-coupled deceptive premises.
StepJack [26] decomposes malicious objectives into seem-
ingly benign substeps, whereas STAC [47] combines indi-
vidually innocuous tool operations into harmful execution
chains. Other studies explore feedback-driven attack opti-
mization. IterInject [36] iteratively refines injection pay-
loads using attack feedback, while MUZZLE [12] lever-
ages agent execution trajectories and failure feedback to
adapt attack strategies. In contrast, ConArtist focuses on
progressively establishing deceptive dependencies between
legitimate tasks and malicious operations, while adapting
attack strategies according to security-auditing feedback.
Prompt Injection Defenses. Existing defenses against
indirect prompt injection employ mechanisms such as
instruction-source separation [39], [42], preference-based
alignment [38], context-aware detection [43], and execution-
behavior analysis [35]. Other studies investigate the effec-
tiveness and limitations of prompt injection defenses [40].
Task Shield [37] examines whether agent actions re-
main consistent with legitimate user objectives, while
MELON [41] analyzes agent execution behavior to iden-
tify suspicious deviations. Although these approaches pro-
vide protection against instruction-level manipulation and
unauthorized actions, attacks that distribute malicious intent
across multiple execution stages introduce additional chal-
lenges. ConArtist investigates how deceptive task dependen-
cies and security-auditing interactions can be exploited to
induce malicious operations despite these protective mech-
anisms.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we presented ConArtist, an adaptive
multi-step prompt injection framework targeting computer-
using agents (CUAs). By constructing deceptive premises,
ConArtist establishes false dependencies between legitimate
tasks and malicious operations, inducing agents to perceive
attacker-desired actions as part of legitimate task execu-
tion. Building on this deception, ConArtist further analyzes
security-auditing feedback to exploit auditing behaviors,
either by forging supporting evidence to influence auditing
decisions or by turning verification actions into attack op-
portunities. Through offline discovery and online strategy
transfer, the framework reuses effective deception strate-
gies in black-box environments with restricted observability.
Experiments on OpenClaw and RedTeamCUA [27] demon-
strate its effectiveness across diverse victim agents and ex-
ecution environments. Our findings reveal the limitations of
task-consistency checks in detecting malicious dependencies
established through deceptive premises. Moreover, security
auditing itself may introduce new attack surfaces, turning
defensive verification actions into exploitable opportunities
for attackers.

6. Ethical Consideration
This research aims to identify security weaknesses in
computer-using agents through controlled red-teaming ex-
periments and contribute to the development of more robust
security defenses. All attack experiments were conducted
exclusively in authorized and isolated evaluation environ-
ments. Although some experiments involved real operating
systems and cloud infrastructure, all attack targets were con-
fined to dedicated testing instances rather than production
systems. Simulated services and test data were used where
appropriate to reproduce security-sensitive scenarios while
minimizing risks to real user information and legitimate
services.
We acknowledge the potential dual-use risks of auto-
mated prompt injection techniques. To mitigate these risks,
we restricted attack execution to controlled experimental
environments and did not target unauthorized systems or
real-world production services. Our findings are intended
to advance the understanding of security risks in computer-
using agents and support the development of more effective
defenses.

Appendix A.
ClawBench Details
A.1. World Edit Specifications
World Edits translate identified attack surfaces into
resource-specific environment modifications. Each operation
specifies how attacker-controlled content is embedded into
a resource that the victim may subsequently access during
legitimate task execution.
Surface-to-World-Edit Mapping. After identifying candi-
date attack surfaces, Conartist maps each resource to a cor-
responding environment modification, termed a World Edit.
Given a candidate surface s = (m, id), the mapping process
selects a modality-specific modification operation, resolves
the target resource, and determines the payload placement
field. These operations instantiate attacker-controlled con-
tent in resources that the victim may subsequently access
during legitimate task execution.
World Edit
Repository File (SC01).
Operation: Append or overwrite file content and record modifica-
tions through Git commits.
Attack Scenario: An attacker modifies repository files through per-
mitted Git collaboration, which the victim subsequently accesses.
Mailbox (SC02).
Operation: Insert an email with the payload in body and regenerate
the inbox index.
Attack Scenario: An attacker sends an email that the victim subse-
quently retrieves during task execution.
Database (SC03).
Operation: Insert a schema-compatible record with the payload in
a designated text column.
Attack Scenario: An attacker submits data through an authorized
application interface that the victim later queries.
Web (SC04).
Operation: Insert or modify page content in page.content and
register new pages in search results.
Attack Scenario: An attacker publishes web content that the victim
subsequently discovers through search or browsing.
Travel (SC05).
Operation: Append a review to an existing listing, placing the
payload in review.text.
Attack Scenario: An attacker posts a review on a hotel, restaurant,
flight, or attraction listing that the victim later consults.
Filesystem (SC06).
Operation: Append content to a file identified by its filesystem path.
Attack Scenario: An attacker modifies a file in a shared or otherwise
writable filesystem location that the victim subsequently reads.
UI (SC07–SC09).
Operation: Insert or update a UI object, placing the payload in
object.value.
Attack Scenario: An attacker controls third-party content displayed
in an application interface that the victim subsequently inspects.
Memory (SC10).
Operation: Insert or update a memory entry, placing the payload in
entry.value.
Attack Scenario: In a multi-user setting, an authorized collaborator
modifies shared memory content subsequently retrieved by the
victim.
Scheduler (SC11).
Operation: Insert or update a scheduled-job record, placing the
payload in job.last_output.
Attack Scenario: An attacker influences scheduled-job output sub-
sequently inspected by the victim.
Reddit (SC12).
Operation: Post comments, create submissions, or edit attacker-
owned comments through authenticated HTTP requests.
Attack Scenario: An attacker publishes or modifies forum content
subsequently read by the victim.

B.2. StepJack Adaptation
StepJack originally targets GUI agents through a two-
stage pipeline consisting of iterative goal decomposition and
payload delivery. For RedTeamCUA, we extend its original
codebase to support our experimental configurations. For
ClawBench, we adapt StepJack to the OpenClaw execution
environment while preserving its core multi-step attack gen-
eration mechanism.
Goal Decomposition. StepJack decomposes an adver-
sarial objective into a sequence of seemingly benign instruc-
tions through multiple independent generation chains and
iterative refinement. We preserve its decomposition strat-
egy, including goal-faithfulness evaluation, per-step safety
scoring, feedback-driven refinement, and best-candidate se-
lection. Specifically, candidate decompositions are evaluated
according to their consistency with the adversarial goal and
the apparent benignness of individual steps. The evaluation
feedback guides subsequent refinement, and the highest-
scoring decomposition is selected for payload construction.
To accommodate our model interface, we replace the orig-
inal local inference backend with stateless model calls and
consolidate per-step safety scoring into a single evaluation
request.
Payload Delivery and Evaluation. For RedTeamCUA,
we retain StepJack’s GUI-based payload delivery and victim
execution pipeline. For ClawBench, the selected decompo-
sition is converted into an instruction payload and inserted
into scenario-specific attacker-writable surfaces through the
benchmark’s environment interfaces. Unlike the original
implementation, which modifies live application content
through database updates or file operations, our adaptation
directly modifies the corresponding sandbox environment
stores. The payload is planted before victim execution,
followed by a single OpenClaw agent run. Attack success
is determined using scenario-specific ground-truth evalua-
tions, while legitimate task completion is assessed using the
benchmark’s utility criteria.
