# Visual-RFT++: Extending Visual Reinforcement Fine-Tuning to Multimodal Tool Use 

Ziyu Liu, Zeyi Sun, Xiaoyi Dong, Yuhang Cao, Haodong Duan, Dahua Lin, Jiaqi Wang, Yuhang Zang B

Abstract —Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) has recently be-come a central paradigm for advancing Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), as demonstrated by systems such as OpenAI’s o3 and the R1 series. Despite significant progress in LLMs, how to leverage RFT to unify visual perception, step-by-step reasoning, and agentic tool use into one coherent training paradigm remains an open challenge. In this work, we present Visual Reinforcement Fine-Tuning++ (Visual-RFT++), a unified framework that extends RFT to both visual perception tasks and multimodal agentic workflows, enabling LVLMs to reason with images, act upon images, and evaluate their own outputs using verifiable reward. With Visual-RFT++, open-source LVLMs not only show significant gains on diverse visual perception tasks, but also acquire agentic abilities such as web searching for real-time information and code-based image manipulation (e.g., cropping, rotation, and analysis). Across visual perception tasks, including fine-grained classification, few-shot detection, reasoning grounding, and open-vocabulary detection, Visual-RFT++ delivers strong gains over SFT, including a +24.3 percentage points improvement in one-shot fine-grained classification and +21.0 percentage points on COCO’s 4-shot setting and +15.4 percentage points on LVIS with around several hundred samples. To evaluate agentic behaviors, we introduce the Multimodal Agentic Tool Bench (MAT) with MAT-Search and MAT-Coding. Visual-RFT++ surpasses its baseline by +18.6 F1 / +13.0 EM percentage points on MAT-Coding and +10.3 F1 / +8.7 EM percentage points on MAT-Search. It also shows gains of 29.3 F1 and 25.9 EM percentage points on multi-hop QA benchmarks such as 2Wiki and HotpotQA, demonstrating strong cross-modal generalization. Visual-RFT++ introduces a data-efficient and reward-driven framework that strengthens both visual reasoning and agentic adaptability. Our code is available at https://github.com/Liuziyu77/Visual-RFT.

Large Vision Language Models (LVLMs) like GPT-4o [18] achieve excellent visual understanding ability by integrating both visual and textual data. This integration enhances the models’ ability to understand complex multimodal inputs and enables more advanced AI systems [19]–[29] capable of processing and responding to both images and text. Generally, the training of LVLMs involves two steps: (a) pre-training and (b) post-training, which contains supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. Post-training is crucial in improving the model’s response quality, instruction following ability, and reasoning abilities. While significant research has focused on using reinforcement learning to enhance LLMs during post-training [30]–[40], the progress for LVLMs has been slower. In this paper, we propose Visual-RFT++. Our method uses GRPO-based reinforcement optimization and verifiable rewards to enhance not only the model’s visual perception and reasoning abilities, but also its multimodal agentic capabilities. 

Reinforcement Learning Recently, with the emergence of reasoning models like OpenAI’s o1 [1], the research focus in Large Language Models (LLMs) has increasingly shifted towards enhancing the models’ reasoning capabilities through reinforcement learning (RL) techniques. Studies have explored improving LLMs’ performance in reasoning tasks such as solving mathematical problems [12], [41]–[44] and coding [45]– [48]. A notable breakthrough in this area is Deepseek-R1-Zero [3], which introduced a new approach to achieving robust reasoning capabilities using only RL, eliminating the super-vised fine-tuning (SFT) stage. Recent studies have extended reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to LVLMs. R1-V [49], VLM-R1 [50], Vision-R1 [51], Seg-Zero [52], MM-Eureka [53], MMR1 [54], LMM-R1 [55], OpenVLThinker [56], Curr-ReFT [57], and Reason-RFT [58] collectively investigate multimodal VQA, mathematical reasoning, grounding, and segmentation. Although these methods share GRPO-style optimization or verifiable rewards, their task definitions, output spaces, supervision scales, and evaluation protocols differ substantially. Building on Visual-RFT [59], Visual-RFT++ complements these efforts by studying fine-grained classifi-cation, few-shot detection, reasoning grounding, and open-vocabulary detection under a unified reward formulation, and further extending the framework to multimodal agentic settings. Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning and Evalua-tion. Recent agentic-RL methods train LVLMs to interact with external tools during visual reasoning. OpenThinkIMG [60], DeepEyes [61], Pixel-Reasoner [62], and VisTA [63] pri-marily study visual operations such as cropping, zooming, and grounding, while GUI-R1 [64] focuses on actions in graphical user interfaces. ToolRL [65] and Nemotron-Research-Tool-N1 [66] instead emphasize text-based function calling. Related benchmarks also target different application scenarios: GAIA [67] evaluates general-assistant tasks involving browsing and tool use, AgentBench [68] covers heterogeneous interactive environments, and VisualWebArena [69] focuses on website interaction. MAT instead targets two multimodal tool-use settings: MAT-Search requires iterative web querying with evidence integration, while MAT-Coding requires image analy-sis followed by code-based image processing for answering. MAT therefore differs from existing methods and benchmarks in terms of task definitions, application scenarios, and tool spaces. Visual-RFT++ further connects these agentic settings with perception-oriented visual reinforcement fine-tuning. III. M ETHODS

This approach simplifies the reward mechanism while maintaining strong alignment with the task’s inherent correctness criteria. B. Visual-RFT++ for Visual Perception 

The framework of Visual-RFT++ for visual perception is shown in Fig. 3. The multimodal input data from the user consists of images and questions. The policy model πθ outputs a reasoning process and generates a group of responses based on the input. Each response is passed through a verifiable reward function to compute the reward. After group computation of the rewards for each output, the quality of each response is evaluated and used to update the policy model. To ensure the stability of the policy model training, Visual-RFT++ uses KL divergence to limit the difference between the policy model and the reference model. We will further discuss how to design the verifiable reward for visual perception tasks in Sec. III-B1, and the data preparation steps in Sec. III-B2. 

1) Verifiable Reward in Visual Perception: The reward model is a key component in reinforcement learning (RL) that aligns models with preference alignment algorithms, which can be as straightforward as a verification function that checks for exact matches between predictions and ground-truth answers. The RL training process in the recent DeepSeek-R1 [3] model achieves a significant improvement in the model’s reasoning ability through the verifiable reward design. To transfer this strategy to the visual domain, we design different rule-based verifiable reward functions for various visual perception tasks.

In the agentic coding setting, the input image may suffer from visual degradation or contain redundant content, where only specific regions are relevant to the question. These conditions impose a significant burden on the model’s visual understanding capabilities. Visual-RFT++ is designed to handle this by training the model to actively generate executable code conditioned on the visual question. The model submits the code to an external environment to preprocess the image (e.g., rotation, cropping), thereby extracting useful information and enabling accurate question answering. During training, the model typically requires multiple reasoning steps or repeated tool invocations to complete a single task. To guide this process, we design a rule-based verifiable reward that evaluates both the model’s tool usage and final answers. Rewards are assigned at each step and provide learning signals for policy optimization. We will further discuss how to design the verifiable reward for multimodal agentic tasks in Sec. III-D. 

D. Verifiable Reward in Multimodal Agentic Tasks 

The design of reward functions plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of reinforcement learning. The rewards we design for agentic tasks follow a similar structure to those used in visual perception, consisting of a combination of format rewards and accuracy rewards. 

Format Reward. In agentic tasks, we require the model to generate outputs that strictly follow a predefined format. This form of structured reward encourages the model to engage in step-by-step reasoning and to invoke tools as instructed, thereby improving the reliability of tool execution. In both the Coding and Searching tasks, the model’s reasoning process is enclosed within ‘ <think></think> ’ tags. When the model needs to invoke a search or code execution tool, it outputs a ‘ <search></search> ’ or ‘ <code></code> ’tag, respectively. The content within each tool tag, either the search query or the generated code, is then extracted and used to interact with the external environment: 

Rformat (o) = I [o contains valid tags ] . (10) 

Accuracy Rewards. The accuracy reward in Visual-RFT++ is modular. For the final answers in both the searching and coding tasks, we use the F1 score as the reward to evaluate answer quality ( RF 1). F1 rewards offer greater tolerance than exact match, providing smoother and more informative learning signals. This design better captures the fluency and variability of natural language responses, and contributes to more stable reinforcement learning. For evaluating the effectiveness of search tool usage, we compute the semantic similarity between the model-generated search query and the ground-truth query using a Sentence Transformer. Compared to F1-based rewards, semantic similar-ity rewards ( Rsem ) are more robust to surface-level variations and better reflect whether the model captures the true intent of the retrieval objective. This is especially important for open-ended or paraphrased queries, where lexical overlap may be low but semantic equivalence still holds. For the coding component, we assign a reward of 1 to all outputs that fall within executable code blocks, without directly supervising the content of the generated code. Instead of evaluating the correctness of the code itself, we allow the model to freely synthesize code based on its own reasoning. This design encourages flexibility and open-ended problem-solving. In preliminary experiments, we observed that applying strict correctness-based rewards to code generation led the model to converge on a small set of deterministic solutions, which in turn reduced its ability to generalize across diverse visual reasoning scenarios. a) Total Rewards: The total reward used in agentic tasks combines two components: a format reward that encourages the model to follow the required output structure, and an accuracy reward that evaluates the quality of answers and tool usage. The format reward ensures proper tagging for reasoning and tool invocation, while the accuracy reward provides feedback on the final answer and search content. The overall reward function is defined as: 

Rtotal (q, o ) = Rformat (o) + Racc (q, o ). (12) 

Multi-Turn Reward. For multi-turn agentic interactions, we decompose each complete trajectory into multiple trajectory-prefix samples according to the interaction turns. Specifically, a trajectory containing T model responses is converted into T

training samples, each retaining the preceding model actions and tool feedback as context and ending at the corresponding response. GRPO is then applied independently to each prefix, and the reward is computed only for its final response. Therefore, rewards are neither accumulated nor discounted across turns. This decomposition enables the model to condition each action on the preceding interaction history while assigning credit only to the action generated at the current turn. IV. M ULTIMODAL AGENTIC TOOL (MAT) B ENCHMARK 

Given that existing benchmarks for agentic search are predominantly language-only [71]–[75], and that there is a lack of standardized evaluation protocols for multimodal agentic coding tasks, we construct and annotate a new benchmark to fill this gap. We refer to the full dataset as the Multimodal Agentic Tool Bench (MAT), which comprises two sub-settings: MAT-Search and MAT-Coding. Specifically, we create a set of multimodal multi-hop VQA examples for evaluating agentic searching capabilities, and another set of VQA examples involving image distortions that require tool-based image processing for agentic coding. 

MAT-Search. For the agentic search task, as illustrated in Fig. 5 (a), we manually construct a multimodal multi-hop VQA dataset. Specifically, we build the MAT-Search benchmark by annotating 150 high-quality multimodal multi-hop VQA examples. The questions in MAT-Search vary in difficulty and require different levels of reasoning depth—more complex queries involve more inference steps and factual knowledge, challenging the model’s ability to handle composite problems and retrieve relevant external information. Each example is carefully crafted and verified by human annotators to ensure clarity, consistency, and suitability for evaluating agentic reasoning and tool use. 

MAT-Coding. For the agentic coding task, as shown in Fig. 5 (b), we design an automated data annotation pipeline. Starting from existing VQA examples, we apply various types of image distortions—including rotation, brightness adjustment, blurring, noise injection, and hybrid distortions—to increase the difficulty of visual understanding. Under this setup, directly reasoning over the processed image is challenging for the model. We annotate 200 test samples to evaluate the model’s ability to complete the image distortion tasks. While the model may attempt to answer questions directly from the processed image, it must overcome various forms of visual noise and irrelevant content. In contrast, advanced agentic systems, such as OpenAI-o3 and our Visual-RFT++, can invoke code, manipulate the image, and then utilize the processed result to more effectively complete the VQA task. 

MAT Training Data. While OpenAI-o3 demonstrates strong capabilities in agentic search and coding, existing open-source multimodal models perform poorly on such tasks. Therefore, we construct a multimodal agentic training dataset JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 8

for Visual-RFT++. Given the simplicity and regularity of the search task format, we carefully annotate 20 multimodal QA examples requiring multi-step reasoning, each paired with a well-defined chain of thought and final answer, which are used to train Visual-RFT++ in the agentic search setting. In contrast, the agentic coding task involves more complex workflows, including the model’s autonomous generation of code. To support this, we construct a training set of 1,200 examples to enable effective learning under this setting. 

Data Splits, Difficulty, and Quality Control. We divide both benchmarks into Simple and Hard subsets. For MAT-Search, difficulty is determined primarily by the number of retrieval hops and supporting evidence pieces required to derive the answer; for MAT-Coding, Simple examples contain a single distortion, whereas Hard examples combine multiple distortions. The training and evaluation sets described above are constructed independently, with no overlap in questions or images. Two PhD-level experts independently inspected all evaluation examples. For MAT-Search, they verified question clarity, visual grounding, reasoning and retrieval chains, and final answers; for MAT-Coding, they checked source images, distortion labels, required image-processing operations, and QA consistency. Ambiguous, incorrectly labeled, or unreliable examples were revised or removed. Training and evaluation questions were written by different annotators and involve disjoint entity sets. V. Search Behavior Diagnostics. To further examine whether these gains reflect improved tool use, we analyze the model’s search behavior. During training, the semantic similarity between generated and annotated target queries reaches 0.95 and 0.92 for the 7B and 3B models, respectively. Remaining failures mainly arise from incorrect initial visual recognition, incomplete evidence collection, or repeated query reformulation in long reasoning chains. 

Coding Behavior Diagnostics. Beyond final-answer ac-curacy, we examine whether the generated programs are executable and perform the intended image operations. Overall, 98.5% and 96.5% of the expected coding steps from the 7B and 3B models produce executable code, respectively, indicating that basic executability is rarely the primary bottleneck. For the 7B model, 86.34% of the generated programs match the intended operation family, with higher rates for dark (94.6%) and overexposure (90.9%) than for rotation180 (68.2%), where incorrect rotation angles or directions remain common. As illustrated in Fig. 9, appropriate cropping or restoration can expose question-relevant content, whereas incorrect operations may remove useful evidence and hurt the final answer. Thus, executable code alone is insufficient; accurate visual diagnosis and operation selection are also essential for effective tool use.
