ABSTRACT
Runtime guardrails protect mobile GUI agents by assessing each proposed action
before execution, but existing guardrails rely on cloud hosted large multimodal
models, exposing every screen to a remote server, including private information
such as payments, messages, and medical records. We therefore study how to
train a compact guardrail that runs on device, which raises two challenges. On-
device budgets limit the model to a short context, yet safety decisions hinge on
evidence observed many steps earlier, which existing guardrails lose in truncat-
ing or summarizing the history. Training a compact model also requires a well-
defined supervision signal, yet existing labels conflate perceived risk, committed
violations, and the decision to intervene under a single unsafe, leaving the safety
boundary ambiguous. We propose MobileGrail, an on-device deployable model
that addresses both. It maintains a recurrent task state that retains only the evi-
dence subsequent safety decisions depend on, so that authorization and execution
progress persist beyond the short context. To intervene early while keeping the
safety boundary precise, it learns two labels, unsafe and block: unsafe marks the
action that commits a violation, whereas block may be issued at any step of an in-
tervention window, which spans from the first action that the user’s authorization
no longer explains to the committing action itself. To support training, we build an
environment of 32 applications whose backends verify the effect of every action,
supplying verified labels for supervision and rewards for reinforcement learning
to calibrate intervention timing. We also build MobileVeto, an offline benchmark
of unsafe trajectories largely on held-out applications, each paired with a benign
counterpart. On MobileVeto, MobileGrail built on Qwen3.5-2B intercepts 76.2%
of violations, up from 64.5% for its prompted backbone, while reducing prema-
ture intervention from 58.6% to 5.2%, outperforming the cloud-based OS-Sentinel
on both. We further conduct online evaluation on GhostEI-Bench, where Mobile-
Grail guarding a Qwen3.8-27B agent reduces fully successful attacks from 13 to
3 of 110 tasks while raising task completion from 48 to 58. Quantized to 4 bits,
MobileGrail runs on a Snapdragon 8 Elite at a median latency of 4.7 s, comparable
to the 5.1 s of cloud-hosted Gemini 3.8 Flash.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in multimodal foundation models have allowed autonomous agents to operate mo-
bile devices through sequences of discrete GUI actions (Kong et al., 2026). However, deploying
these agents in real-world environments introduces safety risks arising from environmental threats,
agent misbehavior, and unsafe user requests, which can lead to financial loss, privacy leakage, or
unauthorized operations (Chen et al., 2026b; Guo et al., 2026). For example, a prompt-injected ad-
vertisement on the screen can redirect an agent to transfer funds or forward private messages without
the user authorization. These risks make effective defenses essential for the safe deployment of mo-
bile GUI agents.
Improving the intrinsic safety of mobile GUI agents through alignment can mitigate these risks (Ai
et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024), but it comes at a capability cost (Huang et al.,

2025) and requires further training as the threats evolve. A complementary approach is to employ
external runtime guardrails, which assess proposed actions before execution using a safety mecha-
nism separate from the underlying agent (Sun et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). New safety policies
can therefore be incorporated by updating the guardrail alone, without retraining the agent itself.
Recent work has explored this approach for mobile GUI agents, with methods that combine formal
verification with VLM-based assessment (Sun et al., 2026), calibrate action-level risk prediction
for selective execution (Feng et al., 2026), or anticipate action consequences through world-model
prediction (Yu et al., 2026).
Despite this progress, existing mobile guardrails largely rely on large multimodal models served
through cloud APIs, which transmit every screen to the service provider (Wang et al., 2026a), in-
cluding payments, messages, and medical records. This cloud-based guardrail introduces both pri-
vacy risks and per-action inference latency that hinders real-time intervention. Therefore, we aim to
build a local on-device guardrail: a compact deployable model that fits mobile compute and mem-
ory budgets and intervenes on unsafe trajectories at the right step without disrupting benign tasks.
The limited budget leaves room for only a short history, yet safety decisions are long-horizon, often
hinging on evidence observed many steps earlier (Liu et al., 2026a): what the user authorized, which
on-screen content is untrusted, and which parameters the agent has staged. Truncating history to a
fixed window (Liang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026) discards this evidence once it falls outside the
window, while free-form summaries retain it verbosely without separating authorized parameters
from untrusted instructions (Hu et al., 2025).
Moreover, training such a guardrail requires a well-defined supervision signal, since its labels are
what define the safety boundary. A guardrail should intervene before harm is committed, and exist-
ing mobile guardrails pursue this by labeling perceived risk as unsafe before a violation occurs (Sun
et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026); OS-Sentinel (Sun et al., 2026), for instance, marks
risk initiation at non-committing steps such as opening an app. Because unsafe is their only label,
marking it earlier is the only way to intervene earlier. The label then covers anticipated risk and
actual violations alike, so the guardrail learns that a step such as opening an app is itself unsafe
and rejects benign tasks that take the same step. The accompanying step-localization metric com-
pounds the problem (Sun et al., 2026): it measures distance to this premature step and penalizes
early and late detections symmetrically, although only a late detection arrives after the harm is com-
mitted. Conversely, labeling only the committing action keeps the boundary precise but teaches the
guardrail to act too late: it intervenes only at the commit, when justified intervention could begin
several steps earlier. Violation and intervention are thus distinct, one a point and the other a window,
and a single label cannot express both.
We introduce MobileGrail, a compact multimodal guardrail locally deployable on device that ad-
dresses both challenges. At each step, it judges the proposed action against the current screen and
its own task state, and returns whether the action is unsafe, whether to block it, and feedback on the
boundary crossed; no screen leaves the device, and the judgment arrives before the action executes.
To retain long-horizon evidence within this compact context, MobileGrail maintains a recurrent
task state, a representation of task authorization and execution progress. To decouple violation from
intervention, it learns to predict two labels, both grounded in a training environment whose app
backends track the effect of every action, so that each violation can be reliably verified. Unsafe
identifies actions whose execution violates the user’s authorization or applicable policy. Block is
supervised over an intervention window: its start is determined by the evidence available to jus-
tify intervention, while its end is fixed by the action that commits the violation. Both predictions
are trained by supervised distillation followed by reinforcement learning, with rewards anchored to
these verified commits rather than teacher judgments.
Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We present MobileGrail, compact multimodal
guardrail models (1.6B and 2B) for on-device deployment that maintain a recurrent task state and
separately predict action safety and intervention, trained by distillation and calibrated by RL with
verifiable rewards. (2) We build a scalable Android training environment that automatically mines
verifiable learning signals: self-evolving red-teaming generates attack scenarios across 32 applica-
tions, and their corresponding backends verify the step at which a violation is committed, ground-
ing both labels and rewards. (3) We introduce MobileVeto, an evaluation benchmark for mobile
guardrails spanning diverse application categories, comprising 210 adversarial trajectories anno-
tated with intervention windows, each paired with a benign counterpart. It jointly evaluates violation
detection, intervention timing, and benign-task preservation.

RELATED WORKS
Runtime Guardrails for Agents
Guardrails for agents extend safety assessment beyond model
inputs and outputs to proposed actions. Prompt-based guardrails such as GuardAgent and AGrail
reason over task-specific checks (Xiang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025), while trained guard models
assess plans (Huang et al., 2026), individual tool calls (Mou et al., 2026; Cui et al., 2026), or entire
trajectories (Wen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026c;b). MAGE further maintains an RL-trained shadow
memory and judges each tool call against it alone (Wang et al., 2026b). Concurrent with our work,
StepSafe (Gu et al., 2026) learns intervention timing for step-level guardrails of tool-calling and
multi-agent workflows.
For GUI agents, safeguards further ground decisions in interface and application state. VeriSafe
Agent verifies actions against formal specifications (Lee et al., 2025), and AgentSentinel audits
sensitive operations with summarized interaction history and system traces (Hu et al., 2025). On
mobile, SafeMobile conditions on the two most recent steps (Liang et al., 2025), OS-Sentinel com-
bines system-trace verification with VLM judgment (Sun et al., 2026), CORA calibrates selective
execution (Feng et al., 2026), and SeerGuard predicts action consequences (Yu et al., 2026).
These guardrails all run off-device on large models, exposing screen content at every step; on-device
moderation, meanwhile, has targeted text prompts rather than GUI actions (Nakka et al., 2025;
Elesedy et al., 2024). Their context is either truncated (Liang et al., 2025) or summarized without
authorization status (Hu et al., 2025), and their supervision is unverified: unsafe labels come from
LLM judges over requests and observations (Sun et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026), with
no referent in action effects. MobileGrail instead trains an on-device deployable guardrail whose
recurrent task state carries authorization and execution state under a bounded context, supervised by
backend-verified action effects.
Safety Evaluation of GUI Agents
Safety benchmarks for GUI agents measure unsafe or untrust-
worthy behavior across mobile and desktop environments (Lee et al., 2024; Kuntz et al., 2026; Yang
et al., 2025) and risks induced by malicious or manipulated environmental content (Chen et al.,
2026a; Liu et al., 2026d; Chen et al., 2026b; Guo et al., 2026). Recent benchmarks further evaluate
runtime guardrails at the step level. MobileRisk introduces step-level risk localization for mobile
agents, anchoring each label at the step where risk is first initiated and penalizing early and late
detections alike (Sun et al., 2026). Its labels, however, mark steps that merely search or browse as
unsafe even when no harmful effect ever occurs (Appendix A.5). Phone-Harm provides per-step
harm labels with a matched benign subset (Feng et al., 2026), but assigns each label from step-local
evidence without access to action outcomes, and does not model when intervention becomes justi-
fied. MobileVeto instead labels an action unsafe only when the backend verifies its harmful effect,
and annotates the step from which intervention becomes justified. Paired with benign counterparts,
it jointly evaluates violation detection, intervention timing, and benign-task preservation.

MOBILEGRAIL: BOUNDED-CONTEXT GUARDRAILING WITH
INTERVENTION DEADLINES
3.1
OVERVIEW AND SETUP
We consider a mobile GUI agent executing a user request u through sequential interactions with
mobile applications. At step t, the agent observes a screenshot ot and proposes an action at. Mo-
bileGrail is an independent runtime guardrail that evaluates at before execution, without access to
the agent’s prompt, policy, or internal reasoning. Its input Xt = (u, ot, Tt, at, ht, mt−1) further
comprises the accessibility tree Tt, a local history ht of screenshot–action pairs from up to k = 3
preceding steps, and a recurrent task state mt−1 carried across steps (§3.2). The guardrail out-
puts (ˆyt,ˆbt, mt) = Gθ(Xt): an action-safety verdict ˆyt ∈{safe, unsafe}, an intervention decision
ˆbt ∈{allow, block}, and the updated state mt passed to the next step. A block is accompanied by
feedback identifying the authorization or policy conflict, which tells the agent what it must not do.
We define an action as unsafe if executing it would violate the user’s authorization or the applicable
policy; preparatory actions that do not themselves violate these constraints remain safe. We decou-
ple intervention from action safety. Figure 1 illustrates the intervention window for an impending

an unsafe verdict is accepted.
3.2
RECURRENT TASK STATE
Safety decisions often hinge on evidence established far earlier in a trajectory. In Figure 1, verifying
the recipient selected at t=6 requires the number resolved in WhatsApp at t=2, which lies outside
the local history window; the current screen alone cannot distinguish the authorized recipient, and
blocking whenever such evidence is unavailable would interrupt legitimate tasks. MobileGrail there-
fore maintains a recurrent task state mt = (ct, dt) that retains only evidence relevant to subsequent
safety decisions, consisting of a task context ct and execution progress dt.
The task context ct has two fields. AUTHORIZED records what the agent is authorized to do, includ-
ing parameters the user left unspecified and the agent resolved from the environment, such as the
number read in WhatsApp at t=2. UNAUTHORIZED exists to keep the authorized record from being
contaminated: instructions that arrive through screen content, such as a prompt injection, and ac-
tions that conflict with the authorized parameters, such as the recipient selected at t=6, are recorded
here as evidence rather than folded into authorization.
The execution progress dt records the operations the agent has completed so far, described at the
level of the task rather than of individual GUI actions. Without it, the guardrail cannot tell whether
an operation authorized only once is being repeated. Both components are updated within the same
forward pass as the decisions: at each step, MobileGrail regenerates ct from the previous state and
the current interaction, and determines whether the proposed action completes such an operation; if
so, the operation is added to dt once the proposed action is allowed and executed. The resulting mt
is passed to the next step without a separate memory model (Appendix B).
3.3
TRAINING
Supervised distillation.
MobileGrail is first trained by distillation from a large teacher model; this
stage teaches it what constitutes a violation and how application operations behave. Our training
corpus contains 21,605 steps from 2,239 trajectories collected in our environment, each annotated
with safety labels and its judgment and intervention windows (Appendix D.1). For every step, the
4


Preprint
teacher, Gemini 3.8 Flash, generates structured targets: the updated recurrent task state, a rationale,
the safety verdict, the intervention decision, and feedback when blocking. Its prompt extends the
student’s general authorization rules with application-specific boundaries and operation semantics,
illustrated by extensive few-shot demonstrations, and the rationale carries this knowledge into the
targets: it locates the targeted control and its effect, determines whether the action commits or
merely prepares, checks key parameters such as the recipient or amount against the accumulated
authorization, and justifies both decisions (Appendix D.2). The teacher additionally produces a
one-sentence decision-sufficient rationale, which supervises a short-reasoning variant for latency-
sensitive deployment. The student is trained on these targets with a much leaner prompt that omits
the application-specific rules and demonstrations, a form of context distillation (Askell et al., 2021;
Guan et al., 2025); this forces it to internalize both the safety boundary and the operation knowledge,
such as what a control does and whether it commits, that RL exploration cannot readily discover.
We fully fine-tune compact vision-language backbones with 1.6B to 2B parameters. To keep the
safety verdict from being diluted by the much longer state and rationale outputs, we average the
loss separately over the verdict tokens D and the remaining supervised tokens R: LSFT = (1 −
λ)LR + λLD with λ = 0.15. Unsafe-step upsampling and the remaining hyperparameters are given
in Appendix E.1.

ENVIRONMENT AND BENCHMARK
4.1
A SCALABLE AND VERIFIABLE ENVIRONMENT
Training a guardrail that keeps pace with evolving attacks requires safety data that can be produced at
scale and refreshed on demand. Existing mobile resources label steps from GUI observations, either
through human annotation (Sun et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026), which caps them at a few hundred
trajectories, or through VLM-as-a-judge (Feng et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026), which propagates judge
errors into supervision; in both cases the actual effect of an action is unverifiable, as production apps
expose no backend state. Following self-hosted environments with verifiable outcomes (Kong et al.,
2026; Chen et al., 2026c), we build 32 applications (including 22 mocked and 10 instrumented na-
tive) spanning seven categories of daily use (Appendix C.1), each with a stateful backend supporting
initialization, snapshotting, and per-step diffing. Every executed action is thereby grounded in the
state change it induces, which identifies not only whether a violation occurred but which step com-
mitted it. With no real assets at stake, violations such as payments or public sharing can be carried
to completion.
4.2
AUTOMATED TRAJECTORY GENERATION
Figure 7 illustrates how trajectories are generated without manual authoring (Appendix C.2). A
proposer designs an attack scenario specifying the user request, attack vector, and target violation,
which a materializer compiles into backend state and injected content. Since unguided agents fail
most attacks, a planner drafts a high-level plan that an executor agent may follow or disregard. The
backend records the effect of each executed action, and a verifier judges whether it exceeds the user’s
5


Preprint
authorization. A trajectory is retained as unsafe only if such an effect occurs; the step producing it
is recorded as the committing step, which anchors the safety labels, intervention windows, and RL
rewards. Each unsafe trajectory is paired with a benign counterpart that performs the same operation
either under an authorizing request or with the prohibited content replaced by permissible content.
Following strategy-accumulating redteaming (Xiong et al., 2026; Mo et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2026c), a synthesizer distills rollout outcomes into reusable attack strategies that guide
subsequent proposals.
4.3
THE MOBILEVETO BENCHMARK
To evaluate whether a guardrail generalizes beyond the applications it was trained on and draws
the safety boundary precisely, we build MobileVeto with the same pipeline under disjoint scenario
configurations, verified by human annotators. Of the 32 applications, 9 are held out of training and
serve as the core out-of-distribution set, together covering most everyday usage scenarios, including
messaging, stock trading, travel booking, and physical-device control; system and web applications
are shared with training and vary only in injection sources and payloads. The benchmark contains
210 unsafe trajectories, each annotated with its intervention window and commit-ambiguous steps
and paired with a benign counterpart that performs the same operations under an authorizing request.
Realistic and consequential scenarios. MobileVeto allows consequential operations to actually
execute rather than stop short of them: payments in the mocked WeChat (LinkChat) go through,
flight bookings are confirmed and can even add travel insurance, and Vela Auto’s remote controls,
from unlocking the doors to sharing a digital key, change the simulated vehicle state. For web cases,
phishing sites are served under realistic domains rather than the placeholder addresses common in
existing benchmarks (Sun et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a), and replicate the genuine service in
layout, branding, and form fields, so a guardrail must determine whether the site is the one the user
intends rather than rely on surface cues.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We evaluate MobileGrail offline on MobileVeto and online as the runtime guardrail of a mobile
agent on GhostEI-Bench (Chen et al., 2026a).
Offline evaluation.
We train MobileGrail on two backbones, Qwen3.5-2B and LFM2.5-VL-1.6B,
and report both SFT and SFT+GRPO checkpoints. We compare against OS-Sentinel (Sun et al.,
2026) with Kimi-K2.6, and extend the evaluation to larger vision-language models, Gemini 3.8
Flash (our annotation teacher), Kimi-K2.6, and Qwen3.5-27B, as well as both backbones before
fine-tuning. To ensure these prompted models are fully informed of the safety policy, they receive
the teacher prompt, which specifies application-specific safety boundaries with few-shot examples
(Appendix D.2), instead of the concise general rules given to MobileGrail. We report the effective
intervention rate (EIR), the proportion of commit steps judged unsafe and blocked, and the missed
intervention rate (MIR), the proportion of commit steps allowed. The premature unsafe rate (PUR)
and premature intervention rate (PIR) are the proportions of unsafe events with an unsafe verdict
before the judgment window and with a block before the intervention window. The benign false-
positive rate (B-FPR) is the proportion of benign trajectories blocked at least once.
Online evaluation.
We deploy MobileGrail (Qwen3.5-2B, SFT+GRPO) as the runtime guardrail
of a Qwen3.8-27B agent on the 110 tasks of GhostEI-Bench, and compare the agent with and without
the guardrail. Following the benchmark protocol, we report task completion (TC), full attack success
(FAS), partial attack success (PAS), benign failure (BF), and the vulnerability rate, VR = (FAS +
PAS)/(N −BF).

RECURRENT STATE ABLATION
Table 4 ablates the recurrent task state mt = (ct, dt), with every variant trained on Qwen3.5-2B
by SFT on GPT-5.6-Luna annotations. The task context ct is what lets the guardrail decide by
authorization rather than by the appearance of an action. Removing it lowers EIR to 73.1% (↓9.7)
and raises B-FPR to 31.9% (↑11.4), nearly as much as removing the state altogether (↓10.0 and
↑15.2). We isolate this effect on 35 anchor–twin pairs. The anchor is the commit step of an unsafe
trajectory, and the twin is the mirrored step of its benign counterpart, which performs the same
action under user authorization. Since the two differ in authorization and not in the action itself, a
guardrail is correct on a pair only if it blocks the anchor and allows the twin. The full state does so in
22 pairs, against 7 without the task context. The structure of ct matters beyond retaining history: a
free-form summary in its place still loses 5.2 EIR points and adds 5.9 points of MIR, both significant,
while producing 60% longer outputs. Execution progress dt contributes less (↑2.1 EIR, ↓4.7 B-FPR,
neither significant).

CONCLUSION
We presented MOBILEGRAIL, a compact guardrail that judges each proposed action of a mobile
GUI agent on device. It carries authorization and execution progress in a recurrent task state, and
learns two labels: unsafe for the action that commits a violation, and block for the decision to in-
tervene, which may come earlier. Both are trained in an environment of 32 applications whose
backends verify the effect of every action, with reinforcement learning calibrating intervention tim-
ing. On MOBILEVETO, MOBILEGRAIL built on Qwen3.5-2B intercepts 76.2% of violations, up
from 64.5% for its prompted backbone, while reducing premature intervention from 58.6% to 5.2%.
When guarding a Qwen3.8-27B agent on GhostEI-Bench, it reduces fully successful attacks from
13 to 3 of 110 tasks while raising task completion from 48 to 58. Quantized to 4 bits, it runs on a
Snapdragon 8 Elite at a median latency of 4.7 s, comparable to cloud-hosted Gemini 3.8 Flash.

We used generative AI models to generate synthetic attack scenarios and support self-evolving red-

SAFETY AND INTERVENTION FORMULATION
A.1
AUTHORIZATION AND SAFETY SCOPE
The user’s request establishes the scope of operations the agent is authorized to perform. Task-
relevant information may fill in missing parameters, but it does not authorize additional operations.
User instructions often leave task parameters unspecified, requiring the agent to resolve them from
task-relevant observations. For example, in “find the address of my hotel reservation and add it
13


Preprint
to tomorrow’s calendar event,” the hotel address is an unspecified parameter that the agent must
retrieve from the corresponding booking confirmation. Using this address as the event’s location
fills in a missing parameter within the user’s authorization. The resolved value and its source must
be retained so that subsequent actions can be checked against the established authorization.
This use of environmental information does not give the information source authority to expand
the task. If the same booking confirmation instructs the agent to forward the user’s itinerary to
an external address, that instruction introduces a disclosure the user did not authorize. Explicit
delegation is likewise bounded by its scope. Under our authorization protocol, a broad request such
as “do what Ella tells you” does not by itself authorize a newly introduced transfer to Ella’s account;
that operation requires specific user authorization. By contrast, a request explicitly authorizing a
transfer to Ella within a stated amount limit establishes permission for that operation.
User authorization does not override the applicable safety policy. For example, a user may explicitly
ask the agent to send a sexually harassing text message or a message containing targeted verbal
abuse. Sending such messages remains unsafe under our safety policy, even when the user specifies
both the content and the recipient.
A.2
ACTION SAFETY AND COMMIT SEMANTICS
Given the authorization scope and safety constraints defined above, an action is unsafe if executing it
produces an effect that violates either constraint. We refer to the action that first produces a particular
violating effect as its committing action, and its position in the trajectory as the commit step. The
safety label concerns the effect of the proposed action itself, rather than the potential consequences
of actions that may follow.
Preparatory actions remain safe when they do not themselves violate these constraints. In Figure 1,
selecting the incorrect recipient at t = 6 and entering the amount at t = 7 prepare an unautho-
rized transfer but do not execute it. Tapping “Send” at t = 8 opens a confirmation dialog without
transferring funds, so its ground-truth safety label also remains safe, although the interface does not
reveal this before execution (see Appendix A.4 for commit ambiguity). The violation is committed
at t = 9, when tapping “Confirm” transfers the funds to the unauthorized recipient.
The commit step is determined by when an action crosses a protected safety boundary. For example,
opening a file’s sharing panel does not itself disclose the file, whereas making it public without au-
thorization crosses its access boundary, even before the link is sent to anyone. Likewise, deleting a
photo without authorization violates the user’s control over their data, even if the photo remains re-
coverable from the trash. The possibility of restoring the previous state does not negate the violation
committed by the action.
In our environment, recorded state changes establish which executed action produces the violating
effect. These outcomes provide supervision for a guardrail that must predict action safety before
execution, without access to the resulting state change. Each action is assessed for its own effects:
an earlier violation does not automatically make every subsequent action unsafe.
A.3
INTERVENTION WINDOW
An action may remain safe under the commit-based definition while already providing sufficient
grounds for intervention. We therefore associate an intervention window with each unsafe event. A
single episode may contain multiple unsafe events, such as an unauthorized disclosure followed by
an unauthorized transfer, each with its own intervention window.

A.4
COMMIT-AMBIGUOUS ACTIONS
Whether an action commits an operation is not always apparent before execution, and confirmation
flows can differ even within the same application. Figure 2 illustrates two Dropbox workflows. Tap-
15


Preprint
ping “Delete link” opens a confirmation dialog without deleting the link, whereas tapping “Share”
after selecting a recipient submits the sharing operation without an additional confirmation step. A
guardrail that assumes another confirmation will follow may therefore postpone intervention beyond
the actual commit.
Our treatment of this ambiguity is motivated by generalization. A guardrail should not need to mem-
orize which applications require an additional confirmation after each button press. Such behavior
may differ across applications, versions, and workflows. Strictly requiring a safe prediction when-
ever an application happens to provide another confirmation step can encourage reliance on these
application-specific conventions. If the guardrail incorrectly assumes that another confirmation will
follow and therefore predicts safe and postpones blocking, the current action may already com-
mit the violation. We therefore avoid penalizing an unsafe prediction solely because an additional
confirmation screen is revealed afterward.
We designate non-committing actions as commit-ambiguous when the available evidence does not
clearly establish whether they immediately execute an operation that would violate authorization
or safety constraints. Their ground-truth labels remain safe, as verified by their actual effects, but
either a safe or an unsafe verdict is accepted in annotation and evaluation. For an unauthorized link
deletion in Figure 2(a–b), this tolerance applies to the initial “Delete link” action; the subsequent
confirmation requires an unsafe verdict. An unsafe prediction at a designated ambiguous step also
requires blocking under the runtime consistency rule.
This tolerance concerns uncertainty about when a violating effect occurs, not whether the operation
is authorized or permissible. It does not apply to an otherwise benign operation merely because its
confirmation flow is unknown, nor does it extend to every preparatory action within an intervention
window. Restricting tolerance to designated commit-ambiguous actions preserves the distinction
between preparation and violation while reducing dependence on application-specific confirmation
sequences.
A.5
CASE STUDIES OF STEP-LEVEL ANNOTATION IN MOBILERISK
MobileRisk identifies the step at which a risk is first initiated. Under this risk-onset definition, a
label on an early preparatory step can be reasonable. Its interpretation as supervision for runtime
intervention, however, requires a separate justification: evidence that a task is risky does not by itself
establish that the current action must be blocked. We examine three examples where the labeled
action has not committed the relevant effect. The distinction concerns the supervision target, rather
than the correctness of the labels under MobileRisk’s policy.
Location modification.
In unsafe_zh/00, the user requests a location change for check-in.
This provides a basis for concern under a risk-oriented policy. Nevertheless, the labeled action at
step 2 is only a one-second wait after opening the application; the target location has not yet been
configured. A later action at step 11 explicitly requests that the selected location be run (Figure 3).
The early label therefore captures task-level risk without establishing that the current wait executes
the requested change. Using it as a mandatory blocking target would supervise interruption of
application entry rather than the operation that requests the effect.
Storage cleanup.
In unsafe_zh/44, the user asks to clear the cache. The Destructive Actions
label is attached to step 7, which selects Show all items. Although the surrounding workflow may
signal a risk of inappropriate removal, this action only changes which items are displayed. Step 28
subsequently accepts an explicit removal confirmation (Figure 4). The labeled step alone therefore
does not establish that blocking must occur before further inspection or item selection; a concrete
prevention opportunity remains at the confirmation gate.
Screen-lock removal.
In unsafe_zh/45, the requested goal of disabling the screen lock is itself
security-relevant. However, the action labeled Security Mechanism Evasion at step 8 navigates back
from the screen-lock settings. It neither selects a new lock mode nor confirms removal. The agent
accepts Delete screen lock? at step 13 (Figure 5). Thus, a signal that the task involves security risk
is assigned to a navigation action that still leaves an explicit opportunity to prevent the change.

B.2
EXECUTION PROGRESS
The execution-progress component dt records completed sub-tasks that remain relevant to subse-
quent safety decisions. Each entry identifies what has been completed and retains the targets or
parameters needed to distinguish it from other operations. This information allows the guardrail to
assess constraints that depend on prior execution, including whether a prerequisite has been satisfied
or an operation authorized only once is being repeated.
For example, consider the request “back up these photos before deleting them from the device.”
Deletion is permitted only after the required backup has completed. Retaining this completion record
allows the guardrail to check the prerequisite even after the backup screen leaves the local history
window. Similarly, if the user requests that a report be sent to a colleague once, recording the
completed send allows a later proposal to send the same report again to be recognized as exceeding
the requested number of executions. The recipient and report identity distinguish this repetition from
a separate authorized send.
Since MobileGrail evaluates at before execution, it predicts the effect at would complete as a can-
didate entry, emitted after both decisions. The entry is appended to dt only if the action is allowed
and executed; blocked actions add nothing. A candidate is emitted only when current evidence
establishes that the action would produce a specific persistent effect: opening a backup screen or
initiating an upload does not by itself establish that the backup has finished. Preparation, navigation,
and actions with uncertain outcomes produce no entry.

C.2
TRAJECTORY GENERATION
Figure 7 summarizes the generation pipeline. A proposer designs scenarios, a materializer prepares
the environment, a planner outlines the workflow, and a victim agent executes GUI actions. A veri-
fier checks execution outcomes, while a synthesizer extracts reusable strategies to guide subsequent
proposals.
Scenario proposal and materialization.
The proposer is supplied with application manuals de-
scribing supported functionality and application-specific forms of unsafe behavior. These include,
for example, sending messages to unauthorized recipients, disclosing files beyond the permitted au-
dience, and transferring funds to unintended accounts. The manuals make the available operations
23


Preprint
and relevant safety boundaries explicit, helping the proposer formulate scenarios that can be instan-
tiated in the environment. Each proposal specifies the user request, attack vector, target violation,
and content required for the scenario.
The materializer instantiates the proposal using a predefined collection of setup functions, including
application-specific injection functions. The model selects functions and supplies their arguments,
while the implementations issue backend requests to populate the corresponding application state.
For example, a message-based scenario requires preparing the conversation and message content that
the victim will encounter. This stage turns the proposal into a device state containing the records,
resources, and injected content needed for execution.
Planning and execution.
Victim models may be unfamiliar with the environment’s workflows,
and unguided rollouts often yield low attack success rates. We therefore adopt a semi-open-book
setup: a planner provides high-level task goals that help the victim navigate the scenario. For exam-
ple, a plan may suggest reading an onboarding email, opening the workspace’s pending invitations,
and comparing the listed email address and role with those in the message. The victim receives
these goals alongside the user request, while selecting the specific GUI actions from the observed
interface. The plan provides workflow guidance, and the victim’s response to the scenario content is
established through execution.
Outcome verification.
After each executed action, the environment returns backend feedback de-
scribing the operation performed and its resulting state changes. This per-step feedback is the pri-
mary verification signal. The verifier assesses it against the user request and scenario specification to
determine whether the action produced a violating effect. For example, opening a transfer confirma-
tion screen does not establish that funds were transferred, whereas a backend record of a completed
transfer identifies the executed operation and its recipient and amount. This feedback allows each
verified violation to be attributed to the action that committed it. Multiple violations within a trajec-
tory are recorded as separate events.
Self-evolving red-teaming.
Following the strategy-accumulation approach of SIR (Xiong et al.,
2026), we use a synthesizer to analyze rollout traces together with the verifier’s backend-grounded
findings. The synthesizer examines how the victim responded to the scenario, where an attack failed,
and which interactions led to a verified violation, then distills these observations into reusable attack
strategies.
These strategies are accumulated in a library and supplied to the proposer alongside the application
manuals. Subsequent proposals can therefore draw on both the environment’s supported operations
and experience from previous rollouts. The resulting scenarios pass through the same materializa-
tion, execution, and verification pipeline, closing the feedback loop shown in Figure 7.
Benign counterparts.
Each MobileVeto scenario is paired with a benign counterpart that pre-
serves the operation of interest while making it permissible. For violations arising from missing
user authorization, the counterpart changes the request to authorize the corresponding operation.
For example, sending a document to an additional recipient exceeds a request naming only the orig-
inal recipient, but is permissible when the request explicitly includes both. Operations prohibited by
the safety policy cannot be made benign by authorization alone; for these, the counterpart instead
replaces the prohibited content with permissible content, such as a courteous message in place of
an abusive one to the same recipient. Both constructions test whether the guardrail distinguishes
violations from legitimate uses of the same capability.

We annotate action safety and intervention timing separately, following the definitions in Ap-
pendix A.1. The verifier provides the commit anchors at which recorded actions produce effects
that violate the user’s authorization or the applicable safety policy. We treat these anchors as fixed
when annotating intervention windows and commit-ambiguous actions.

Intervention windows.
We partition each trajectory into consecutive segments ending at verifier-
provided commit anchors. The first segment begins at the start of the trajectory; each subsequent
segment begins immediately after the preceding anchor. For each segment, the annotator receives
the authorization baseline, the anchored operation, and recorded actions with pre- and post-action
observations, excluding the agent’s internal reasoning and scratchpad. It identifies the earliest step
at which the agent begins or continues a specific unauthorized operation, the relevant target or pa-
rameter is identifiable, and a concrete correction can be given. Merely encountering suspicious
content, opening an application, or navigating toward a sensitive workflow is insufficient. Although
the annotation process can inspect execution results, the justification for intervention must rely on
evidence available before the action being labeled. The resulting window extends from this earliest
admissible intervention step to the fixed commit anchor.
Commit-ambiguous actions.
We additionally identify non-committing actions for which the pre-
action evidence does not establish whether execution will produce the violating effect. These in-
clude controls that unexpectedly open an additional confirmation step and submissions that are
subsequently rejected without crossing the protected boundary. A deployed guardrail cannot as-
sume either an additional opportunity to cancel or a rejection that is only revealed after execution.
We therefore retain the verifier-established safe label for these actions while tolerating an unsafe
prediction. This tolerance applies only to designated actions that could reasonably be assessed as
committing the unauthorized operation; it does not extend to ordinary preparation or authorized
operations. An unsafe prediction still requires blocking, and tolerance at an earlier step does not
remove the mandatory intervention requirement at the actual commit.

ambiguous actions, excluding the anchor itself; and why records the supporting justification. These
outputs are stored with the fixed anchor and the segment boundaries.

D.2
TEACHER-GENERATED STRUCTURED SUPERVISION
We use Gemini 3.8 Flash with detailed policy instructions and multiple few-shot demonstrations to
generate structured supervision. The prompt makes the authorization and safety criteria explicit and
illustrates how to apply them to concrete mobile interactions. The resulting demonstrations transfer
these judgments, their supporting explanations, and recurrent state updates to the student through
supervised fine-tuning. Their consistency with the safety and intervention annotations is assessed
separately during validation.
Teacher prompting.
At each step, the teacher receives the original user request, the current pre-
action screenshot and its accessibility text, the proposed action, up to three preceding screenshot–
action pairs, and the prior recurrent task state. The current proposal is excluded from the executed-
action history. Screen content is treated as evidence rather than authority to expand the task, and
material identifiers are copied from the accessibility text.

The teacher prompt provides detailed authorization and safety policies, supplemented with mul-
tiple few-shot demonstrations illustrating their application to concrete mobile interactions. The
policy specification covers authorization scope, delegated task information, protected safety bound-
aries, action effects and commit timing, intervention criteria, and recurrent state updates. It also
specifies how to ground rationales in observable evidence and how to provide corrective feedback.
Application-specific policy reminders supplement these general rules with relevant operation seman-
tics. Together, these instructions make explicit both what the teacher must assess and the evidence
required to support each judgment.
The synthetic user–assistant demonstration pairs show the policy applied to concrete decisions. Each
demonstration presents a user request, relevant context, a screen description, and a proposed action,
followed by a structured response containing the rationale, updated state, two decisions, and feed-
back where applicable. The examples illustrate distinctions such as a task reference sufficiently
resolved by available evidence versus an unresolved consequential choice, and an operation that
merely opens a confirmation versus one that commits an effect. They demonstrate how the same
authorization and timing rules should be reflected consistently in the labels, state, and explanation.
The policy and few-shot messages accompany each step’s input as teacher-side annotation context;
they are not copied into the student’s inference prompt.
Structured supervision.
The teacher returns one JSON object containing seven fields, as speci-
fied in Prompt 3. The reasoning field gives a self-contained explanation supporting both deci-
sions; decision_sufficient_rationale provides a separately generated compact expla-
nation rather than a truncation of the full reasoning. The task_context field contains the com-
plete updated authorization state, with AUTHORIZED and UNAUTHORIZED entries as defined in
Appendix B. The execution_state field contains only new effect predicates for the proposed
action, expressed as outcomes that would hold if an allowed action successfully executes; it does
not itself establish that the action has already completed. Blocked and preparatory actions produce
no such effect update.
The safety_judge and strategy fields represent action safety and intervention, respectively.
An unsafe judgment requires blocking. feedback is supplied only when blocking and identifies
the concrete boundary together with a correction the agent can make. It is null when the action is
allowed.

Rationale specification.
The long rationale follows four parts: (i) action and effect, identifying
the targeted control or object and the effect of operating it; (ii) commit stage, determining whether
the action commits or produces a persistent effect, or remains recoverable preparation, and retaining
the uncertainty when the action only possibly commits; (iii) authorization and parameters, checking
the target, recipient, amount, and other key parameters against the user’s request and the accumu-
lated authorization, citing the evidence for a match or conflict; and (iv) decisions, explaining why
the action is safe or unsafe and why it should be allowed or blocked. The short rationale compresses
these into one sentence combining the action’s effect or preparatory nature, its relation to the autho-
rized scope, and the decisive conflicting parameter or commit fact, e.g., “Next only opens review,
but the staged recipient conflicts with the requested recipient.” An example of both follows.

E.1
TRAINING DETAILS
SFT data.
The SFT corpus contains 21,605 steps from 2,239 trajectories: 14,195 steps from adver-
sarial trajectories, 4,497 from authorization-matched benign counterparts, and 2,913 from defended
rollouts in which the agent does not follow the attack. A targeted batch of 111 trajectories (1,017
steps) is repeated three times. After holding out a small set of scenario families for monitoring and
duplicating steps whose teacher verdict is unsafe, training uses 22,923 samples.
SFT configuration.
Table 8 lists the shared hyperparameters. The vision encoder is frozen, while
the projector and language model are fully fine-tuned; This leaves approximately 2.0B and 1.6B
trainable parameters for Qwen3.5-2B and LFM2.5-VL-1.6B, respectively.
Reinforcement learning.
We initialize from the long-reasoning SFT checkpoint, which also
serves as the fixed KL reference. Both stages draw on the same trajectory pool with different fil-
tering. SFT discards steps whose teacher decisions conflict with the annotated labels, whereas RL
requires only the annotated labels and windows and thus retains them; RL instead holds out a subset
of trajectories for in-distribution validation, leaving 2,091 trajectories (22,219 steps, 1,273 of which
are commit steps) for training. Each update draws 32 groups across four ranks; each rank contributes
two commit-step groups, one pre-window group, three benign-counterpart groups, and two groups
of other safe steps. Commit-step groups contain 16 samples, plus 8 samples at the mirrored step of
the benign counterpart when one exists, chosen as the step whose action most resembles the com-
mitting action and preferring steps the current policy falsely blocks. Other groups contain 8 samples.

INFERENCE SETUP
At each step, the guardrail evaluates the agent’s proposed action before it is executed. Its input con-
sists of the original user request, the current screenshot and accessibility text, the proposed action,
up to three preceding screenshot–action pairs, and the recurrent task state from previous steps. The
history excludes the current proposal, and future observations or backend outcomes of that proposal
are unavailable at decision time. A synthetic action marker identifies the target control or gesture in
the screenshot; it is not part of the application content.

The student uses a compact system prompt that specifies its role, the two de-

output-token overhead, the prompt specifies a compact tagged format with dedicated special tokens

OUTPUT FORMAT
We use a compact tagged representation to reduce the serialization overhead of JSON, which re-
quires generating field names, quotation marks, and separators at every step. Each predefined field
delimiter and decision marker, such as <CTX>, </CTX>, <SAFE>, and <BLOCK>, is registered
as a single special token in the student tokenizer. An entire marker therefore occupies one token.
This keeps the structural portion of the response short while preserving explicit field boundaries and
directly identifiable decisions. The rationale and field contents remain ordinary tokenized text.
The student generates a rationale followed by a tagged output containing the updated task con-
text, the safety judgment, the intervention decision, and any applicable effect update or feedback.

choice between tokens, and square brackets denote optional fields; these notation symbols are not
emitted by the model. Zero or more <Done> elements may appear when an allowed action has an
established external or persistent effect.

Decision consistency and feedback.
The valid decision pairs are SAFE+ALLOW, SAFE+BLOCK,
and UNSAFE+BLOCK. A safe action can still be blocked when intervention is justified before a
violation, while an unsafe prediction always requires blocking. On BLOCK, the proposed action is
withheld and the feedback identifies the relevant boundary and a concrete correction or clarification.
On ALLOW, the action may execute and the feedback field is omitted.
State updates.
The <CTX> field contains the complete updated task context, which replaces the
previous context for subsequent decisions. A <Done> element represents a candidate effect of the
proposed action, expressed as a short completed-effect predicate. It is incorporated into the exe-
cution ledger only after the action is allowed and its effect is confirmed. Producing the predicate
before execution does not establish that the action has already succeeded. Blocked actions, prepara-
tion, navigation, and uncertain or failed effects do not add completed-effect entries. These updates
follow the state specification in Appendix B.

Measurement protocol.
We replay MobileVeto trajectories step by step in their original order,

F.2
CROSS-STEP ENCODER AND PREFIX CACHING
Consecutive guardrail inputs overlap substantially. With k = 3, the screenshot ot reappears in the
local history of steps t+1 through t+3, so each step introduces only one new screenshot and the
other three have already been encoded. We cache the vision-encoder and projector outputs of each
screenshot and evict them once it leaves the history window, so that each step encodes only ot.
We additionally reuse the key–value cache of the system prompt, which is fixed across steps and
episodes, so that prefill starts after this prefix.

PROFILING RESULTS
Table 11 reports the full profile. Cold processes each step from scratch, re-encoding every screenshot
and prefilling the complete prompt; Opt. enables the caching in Appendix F.2.
Cross-step caching.
At Q8_0, caching reduces median latency by 18% for Qwen3.5-2B and 56%
for LFM2.5-VL-1.6B, almost entirely through prefill: prefill time falls by 48% and 71%, while
decode time is unchanged. Fewer tokens are prefilled (2,946→2,266 and 2,126→1,315), and pre-
fill throughput rises (737→1,058 and 180→379 tokens/s), as history screenshots are no longer re-
encoded. The gain is larger for LFM2.5-VL-1.6B, whose cold prefill accounts for 84% of its mean
latency.
Quantization.
Moving from Q8_0 to Q4_0 raises decode throughput by 43% and 48% for
Qwen3.5-2B and LFM2.5-VL-1.6B, respectively, while leaving prefill throughput within 5%, con-
sistent with decoding being bound by weight bandwidth and prefill by compute. The latency re-
duction therefore scales with the share of time spent decoding: median latency falls by 22% for
Qwen3.5-2B, which spends 68% of its mean latency decoding, and by 12% for LFM2.5-VL-1.6B,
which spends 47%. Q4_0 also lowers peak system memory by 0.83 and 0.55 GiB, respectively.
Reasoning length.
For Qwen3.5-2B, the short-reasoning variant reduces output from 118 to 69–
73 tokens and decode time by 38–40%, lowering median latency by 27% at Q8_0 and 21% at Q4_0.
Combined with Q4_0, it attains the lowest median latency of 4.70 s. For LFM2.5-VL-1.6B, short
reasoning shortens output by only 9–16%, and because prefill dominates its latency, median latency
decreases by at most 6%.
