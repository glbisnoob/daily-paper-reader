## Abstract 

GUI grounding requires precise clicks at low latency. Recent models such as GUI-Decisions and GUI-Swift predict coordinates in one forward pass using fixed global bins with local refinement. However, screenshot downsampling removes detail needed to locate small controls, while fixed bins cover larger pixel ranges at higher resolutions. We introduce PixelOne , which transfers spatial knowledge from a search teacher to a student that predicts clicks directly from screenshots at native resolution. The teacher uses VideoJEV’s text-option scorer to select rows and columns and progressively zoom into candidate regions. The student inherits the teacher’s parameters and matches its distributions over explored regions through spatial distillation alongside box supervision. At inference, the same decision head selects a visual token, and a lightweight pixel head predicts the click within it, all in one forward pass. A click-aligned loss encourages pixels inside the target box to score above those outside. Across four benchmarks, PixelOne-Pro-35B-A3B achieves 83.8% mean accuracy, the highest among the evaluated single-forward grounders, with a mean latency of 0.43 s per target.

## 1 Introduction 

A GUI agent translates instructions, such as “open the alignment menu”, into corresponding click locations on a screenshot [1, 3, 13, 22, 25, 28, 36, 39, 40, 42, 43]. This requires accurate visual grounding, particularly in high-resolution interfaces where small interactive targets are often surrounded by visually similar controls, making the preservation of fine-grained spatial details essential [14, 18, 45]. Consequently, a practical GUI agent is required to localize target elements accurately with low latency [2]. Spatial readouts predict clicks directly from scores over image regions or target masks [32, 34]. By contrast, fixed-slot methods such as GUI-Decisions and GUI-Swift predict distributions over global coordinate bins at predefined token positions and refine the coordinates using neighboring-bin probabilities [8, 31]. Both approaches avoid autoregressive coordinate generation and ground targets in a single forward pass. However, these models downsample high-resolution screenshots, removing details needed to locate small controls, while their global bins cover large pixel ranges at higher resolutions. Extending it to pixel localization requires spatial candidates tied to screenshot regions and a way to resolve a click within the selected region. We introduce PixelOne , a teacher-student framework for accurate GUI grounding from full-resolution screen-shots in a single forward pass. During training, a search teacher learns to localize targets by choosing rows and columns from textual spatial options. ## 2 Related Work 

Coordinate generation and direct readout. Autoregressive grounders represent coordinates and action fields as text [6, 24, 30, 38]. This flexible interface requires sequential decoding, whereas direct readouts predict locations in a single forward pass. GUI-Actor and V2P score visual patches with a dedicated head, while GUI-AIMA supervises attention within the backbone [5, 34, 46]. Seg2Click instead predicts action masks at the pixel level [32]. Fixed-slot methods such as GUI-Decisions and GUI-Swift take another route, reading coordinates from predefined positions through the vocabulary head [8, 31]. Although these methods avoid sequential decoding, precise grounding still depends on sufficient image detail and reliably converting spatial scores into clicks. 

Spatial detail and inference cost. Higher-resolution screenshots preserve the detail needed to locate small targets but increase inference cost [10]. GUI-Decisions and GUI-Swift limit the number of visual tokens and therefore downsample large screenshots [8, 31]. They also predict coordinates using fixed global bins with refinement from neighboring-bin probabilities; as resolution increases, each bin covers a larger pixel range. Other methods recover local detail through additional computation. Zoomed search processes cropped regions, while Re-Prefill uses attention to guide a second prefill stage [18, 19]. InnerZoom avoids another round of image encoding by refining regional evidence within the decoder layers, although it still generates coordinates autoregressively [21]. An alternative is to move spatial refinement into training, allowing the model to learn from detailed local views without repeating the process at inference time.

## 3 Method 

PixelOne takes a screenshot I and an instruction u as input and predicts a click location ˆp. The prediction is considered correct if ˆp lies inside the target box B. Our goal is to predict this point directly from the full screenshot while learning from the finer spatial decisions made during search. To this end, we first train a teacher to locate the target through a sequence of row and column choices (Figure 2). We then transfer its knowledge to a student that operates without search. The student uses the teacher’s decision head to select a visual token and a lightweight pixel head to determine the click within that token (Figure 3). It inherits the teacher’s parameters and learns from both the teacher’s spatial distributions and the target box. At inference time, the student receives only (I, u ) and predicts the click with one visual encoding and one pass through the model. 

3.1 From Native Options to Spatial Localization 

VideoJEV’s native interface scores candidate options rather than predicting image coordinates directly [48]. We adapt this interface to spatial localization by representing a target through row and column choices on a grid. Applying the same choices to progressively zoomed crops gradually narrows the target region. A single forward pass scores all questions associated with one search state. For each question, let q denote the hidden state after the option list and hc the hidden state at the end of candidate c. The native query/key head assigns candidate c the score

∥Wkhc∥ ∥ Wq q∥ + b. (1) 4Here Wq q and Wkhc denote the native affine projections, with their biases implicit. The scale α = 10 and bias b remain fixed during teacher training. Softmax is applied separately to the row, column, and visibility option sets. The three-round search (T3) begins with a 4 × 4 grid over the full screenshot. The score of each cell is the product of its row and column probabilities. The teacher retains the two cells with the highest scores. In each of the next two rounds, the teacher places an 8×8 grid over a crop corresponding to each retained cell. For every crop, it receives both the enlarged crop and a full-image overview that marks the crop location. It also answers a visibility question that asks whether the target is present in the crop. A child score is obtained by multiplying its row and column probabilities by the visibility probability of the crop and the score of its parent. The teacher then retains the two highest-scoring children across both crops. After the third round, it returns the center of the best final cell as ˆpT . The two crop states in each refinement round are processed as a batch, resulting in three language-model calls and five visual-encoder calls per target (Figure 2). We first train the teacher on global and local states derived from target boxes. We then run the partially trained teacher on a subset of the training examples and collect the states visited during search. For each visited crop, the center of the target box defines the row and column labels whenever it falls inside the crop. The visibility label is positive when the crop fully contains the target box and negative when the crop does not overlap it. For partial overlaps, we omit the visibility loss while retaining any valid row and column supervision. Training then continues on a mixture of visited states and states derived from target boxes [29]. Finally, we freeze the teacher and cache its search traces (Section S2.2). The teacher’s weights initialize the student, while the cached distributions provide spatial supervision. 

3.2 From Search to Single-Forward Pixels 

The student transfers the learned decision parameters from textual options to visual tokens in the screenshot. Each of the N tokens represents an s × s image patch, where s = 32 .

Selecting a region. A single forward pass produces a state hj for each visual token and a query state q after the instruction. We apply the decision head in Equation (1) to obtain a score aj for every token. Scoring each token against the query makes the resulting scores depend on the instruction. 

Selecting a pixel. A visual token identifies only an image region, so a lightweight pixel head predicts the precise point within it. The head uses the same token states, conditions them on the query q, and produces a logit zjk for each of the s2 pixel centers k in token j (Section S2.1). Let Vj denote the set of valid pixel indices in token j, excluding padding outside the original image. Decoding first selects the token with the highest score and then the highest scoring valid pixel within that token:

4.1 Main Grounding Results 

Leading single-forward accuracy. Across the four benchmarks in Table 1, PixelOne-Pro reaches 83.77% mean accuracy at 35B-A3B and 80.62% at 4B. Pro-35B-A3B leads the single-forward grounders, outperform-ing the strongest external baseline, Seg2Click-9B, by 8.34 percentage points. 

Faster and more accurate than GUI-Owl-1.5. Pro-4B outperforms GUI-Owl-1.5-4B [38] by 1.41 per-centage points with a 3.11 × speedup. Pro-35B-A3B similarly outperforms GUI-Owl-1.5-8B by 2.43 points with a 2.53 × speedup. 

A lower-latency alternative to UI-Venus-2. Pro-35B-A3B achieves 26% lower mean latency than UI-Venus-2-9B [30], with 83.77% mean accuracy versus 84.96%. Both reach 72.04% on ScreenSpot Pro. Figure 1 summarizes these comparisons: the insets show the benchmark-level gains over GUI-Owl-1.5, and the main plot shows both PixelOne-Pro models on the accuracy–latency frontier. 

Parameter-efficient grounding. With language LoRA and a frozen visual encoder, the 200K PixelOne models reach 74.15% mean accuracy at 4B and 79.17% at 35B-A3B.

4.2 Inference Efficiency 

Eliminating sequential decoding. PixelOne produces a click with one visual encoding and one language-model forward, avoiding sequential coordinate decoding. Although UI-Venus-2-9B and both GUI-Owl-1.5 models also encode the screenshot once, UI-Venus-2-9B averages 10.68 language-model calls per target and each GUI-Owl-1.5 model averages 34.72, including the initial forward. These timing results are summarized in Table S3. 

Accuracy among direct readouts. Pro-4B improves mean accuracy over GUI-Swift-4B by 18.03 percent-age points, with a higher mean latency of 0.323 s per target compared with 0.120 s for GUI-Swift-4B. This accuracy–latency trade-off is reported in Table 1. 

Lower tail latency. Tail latency favors PixelOne-Pro on ScreenSpot, ScreenSpot V2, and OSWorld-G, where each Pro model’s P95 latency is below the median latency of UI-Venus-2-9B. On ScreenSpot, for example, Pro-35B-A3B has a P95 latency of 0.450 s, compared with a median latency of 0.531 s for UI-Venus-2-9B. ## 5 Conclusion 

PixelOne transfers a search teacher’s parameters and spatial distributions to a student that predicts clicks directly from the full screenshot in a single forward pass. Its shared decision head selects a visual token, while a lightweight pixel head trained with a click-aligned objective locates the click within that token. Experiments across four benchmarks validate the contributions of spatial distillation and click-aligned pixel learning. PixelOne-Pro-35B-A3B achieves 83.8% mean accuracy with a mean latency of 0.43 s per target, while both the 4B and 35B-A3B configurations lie on the accuracy–latency frontier of the evaluated systems. More broadly, PixelOne offers a design principle for efficient GUI grounding: move spatial exploration into training and align supervision with the executed click, enabling richer spatial learning without performing search at inference time.

The 200K and 500K runs share 2,000 held-out targets from 1,808 screenshots, with each source image and its crops assigned to the same split. The near-duplicate rule requires dHash distance ≤ 2, grayscale MAE ≤ 3, and log-aspect-ratio difference ≤ 0.02 . Both continuation sets use benchmark-filtered sources. The 500K expansion also excludes existing target IDs, duplicate screenshot–box pairs, and validation image groups.

Training readouts. For GT supervision, we read pixel logits for the union of the box-center token, up to four top-scoring tokens, and up to four top-scoring GT-valid tokens. The GT-valid subset of this union defines R in Equation (9). KD also reads teacher-observed tokens without changing R.

S2.2 Training the Search Teacher 

Search states. Each local state includes the full image and a crop with a 12% margin on each side. Each refinement round batches two local states. 

Schedule. We train the teacher for 500 updates on box-derived states, then 500 on an approximately 1:1 mixture of box-derived and visited states. We collect visited states on 4,096 training targets after the first stage. The final teacher is frozen for student initialization and trace caching. 

Labels. Row and column supervision uses the box center when it lies inside the crop. Visibility is positive when the crop contains the target box and negative when they do not overlap. We omit visibility loss for partial overlap. 

S2.3 Student Initialization and Training 

Initialization. Base-initialized controls use Qwen weights with the VideoJEV decision head and special-token embeddings. Teacher-initialized controls use T3 weights and a new pixel head. 

Controlled training. We train rank-16 language LoRA and both heads for 1,500 updates with a frozen visual encoder and causal attention. Matched pairs share pixel-head initialization and data order. 

Expanded configurations. At each scale, we train both variants independently from the same 21,557-target Top20+KD student, retaining its pixel head. For PixelOne , we train rank-16 language LoRA and both heads on 200K targets with a frozen visual encoder and causal attention. For PixelOne-Pro , we train all parameters, including the visual encoder, on 500K targets. Full-attention layers are bidirectional with padding-only masks. Linear attention is unchanged. Both variants use Top20 and observed-support pixel KD.

## S4 Evaluation and Timing Protocols 

S4.1 Benchmarks and Coordinate Conventions 

We evaluate on 1,272 ScreenSpot, 1,272 ScreenSpot V2, 1,581 ScreenSpot Pro, and 510 OSWorld-G rectan-gular targets. A click is correct if it lies inside the target box in original-image coordinates. Mean accuracy weights the four benchmarks equally, while Section S6.4 pools all 4,635 targets. Checkpoints are selected by update count. 

S4.2 Base-Model Coordinate Generation 

Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B use greedy decoding with thinking disabled and KV caching enabled. Screenshots are padded to multiples of 32 without resizing. Both models use the following prompt. 

Instruction: [JSON-quoted instruction] Locate the UI element to click for this instruction in the image, and output its point coordinates in JSON format. 

We map 0–1000 point_2d coordinates [4] from the padded canvas to original pixels. Malformed, ambiguous, out-of-range, and padding predictions count as misses. 

S4.3 External Model Configurations 

The six recent baselines use their released inference policies in Transformers, without action history. 

GUI-Decisions-2B uses its released slot readout with an input cap of approximately 1 megapixel. 19 GUI-Decisions-31B uses the BF16 merged Gemma-4-31B E3 release with a budget of 280 visual tokens. 

GUI-Swift-4B uses the Desktop131 adapter on GUI-Owl-1.5-4B with 131 slots in one causal forward and an adaptive image budget. Actions without coordinates count as misses. 

GUI-Owl-1.5-4B/8B both use Instruct checkpoints, the official grounding prompt and parser, and a cap of 9,800 visual tokens. 

UI-Venus-2-9B uses the official direct-coordinate prompt and parser with thinking disabled and a cap of 12,845,056 pixels. Linear attention uses native FLA and causal-convolution kernels. 

Training counts. Counts in Table 1 cover reported adaptation stages, excluding earlier or inherited training. The 31B Decisions stage includes natural images. Full training totals for GUI-Owl and UI-Venus-2 remain unverified. 

S4.4 Timing and Aggregation 

Setup. We measure latency on an H200 at batch size 1. The same 128 targets per benchmark are timed three times after warmup, giving 384 measurements per benchmark. 

Scope. Timing starts from a decoded RGB screenshot and includes preprocessing, visual encoding, inference, and click extraction. AR timing includes full response generation and parsing. Image-file reading, model loading, and warmup are excluded. 

Aggregation. Mean, P50, and P95 are computed per benchmark. Overall latency is the mean of the four benchmark means. Speedups are ratios of these averages. For cascades, we sum stage times paired by target and repetition, excluding model loading and switching.
