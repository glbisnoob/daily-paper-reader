## Abstract 

The use of AI agents among the general public has been increasing exponentially throughout 2026, though it is often hard for users to determine which configuration is most effective for their personal use. To help provide clarity here we introduce ShellBench, 100 day-to-day tasks based on more than 10 million user requests, each set in a realistic workspace with messy files and competing data sources, and graded for both correctness and safety. We evaluate 12 models across 5 agent harnesses at 3 reasoning levels for a total of 53,100 rollouts, 20% of which are on tasks designated for public release. We find that model choice impacts performance more than harness choice, but the three leading models differ by only 1.1 percentage points. GPT-6.1 Sol passed 61.4% of attempts at about one-sixth of GPT-6 Astra’s estimated token cost per attempt , while GPT-6 Astra led at 62.5%. We also found that harness choice had little effect on either performance or safety with all harnesses scoring between 42.9 (Claude Code) and 45.2 (OpenClaw) for pass@1.

## 1 Introduction 

AI assistants are reaching a wider audience and being used beyond software engineering. ChatGPT grew from 800 million weekly ac-tive users in October 2025 to more than 900 million in February 2026 [13], and a study of its consumers found that non-work tasks accounted for a growing share of their messages [2]. In October 2026, Meta reported that more than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set up Muse within six weeks of launch. Shopping was its most common use, and users frequently connected email and calendars [14, pp. 1–2]. Choosing an assistant to arrange an appointment, check a bill, or plan a trip means choosing both a model and a harness, the software through which the model reads files and takes action. When the same model can run in several har-nesses, how much does each choice matter for completing everyday tasks correctly and safely? ShellBench is designed to address this challenge. We constructed a dataset of 100 realistic tasks derived from authentic user queries, with three rollouts per task at each combination of effort level, harness, and model. The design covers three effort levels, five har-nesses, and twelve models. One exception applies: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is not assessed in Hermes (§4). Higher effort usually improves pass rates. Model scores account for most of the variation across the full comparison, but some harnesses change individual model scores substantially. The study makes three contributions.

(1) ShellBench comprises 100 realistic AI assistant tasks derived from more than 10 million real user queries. The 90 judge-graded tasks have 721 rubric criteria, 381 of which pertain to safety. Task workspaces contain a median of 188 files, and all tasks underwent review by four independent individuals in addition to the original author (§3). ## 2 Related work 

Existing benchmarks have already been released to study the behav-ior of harnesses and models on realistic user queries. To make this research area easier to digest, we cluster the existing benchmarks into broad categories and note how each category compares to the current work. 

## 2.1 Web and computer assistants 

GAIA [15] asks 466 questions that need browsing, files, or sev-eral reasoning steps and grades a short normalized answer. We-bArena [33] runs 812 tasks on self-hosted shopping, forum, code, and map sites and checks the answer or the final site state. OS-World [26] gives agents 369 tasks in real desktop environments, each graded by its own script. AssistantBench [30] poses 214 time-consuming information tasks on the open web with automatically evaluated answers. Unlike the benchmarks in this category, Shell-Bench agents work through the terminal, tool calls, and APIs. Shell-Bench also differs from these four studies by including safety criteria in addition to completeness criteria. 

## 2.2 Personal apps and customer service 

AppWorld [22] has agents write code against nine simulated every-day apps for 750 tasks, with unit tests that also fail unrequested changes. 𝜏 -bench [28] and 𝜏 2-bench [1] put the agent in a customer-service role under a written policy, compare the final database with the goal state, and report Pass/Fail. Gaia2 [7] runs 1,120 sce-narios on a simulated phone whose state keeps changing while the agent works. WorkBench [20] checks 690 workplace requests against the expected state of five databases. MyPCBench [8] sets 184 personal errands on one fictional person’s seeded desktop, and ClawBench [31] runs 153 everyday online tasks on 144 live websites. These are the closest to ShellBench in subject, but only MyPCBench gives the agent a large, realistic workspace as a starting point, and its central comparison concerns personal context. ShellBench tasks contain a median of 188 diverse files in the starting environment and study the effects of different harnesses on model performance. 

## 2.3 Professional work 

TheAgentCompany [27] simulates a software company and scores 175 tasks with weighted checkpoints. OfficeBench [25] chains 300 office tasks across documents, spreadsheets, calendar, and email. GDPval [17] compares model deliverables with those of profession-als in 44 occupations through blinded expert preferences. APEX-Agents [23] grades 480 banking, consulting, and legal tasks in file-rich worlds against rubrics with an LLM judge and reports Pass 8. AutomationBench [18] sets 600 public business workflows across 47 simulated apps, seeds them with misleading records and business-policy rules, and checks the final state with determin-istic assertions. Workspace-Bench [21] gives agents 388 tasks in large professional file workspaces, some with several historical ver-sions of a file, grades them by rubric with an agent judge, and runs four harnesses. MKA [19] grades 200 professional tasks against 14,284 expert-weighted pass/fail criteria. All seven sets contain professional tasks, and only AutomationBench contains a safety component. ShellBench tasks are all based on real user requests made in a personal context, rather than a professional one. 

## 2.4 Safety inside ordinary tasks 

AgentDojo [5] measures whether agents complete 97 banking, travel, workspace, and messaging tasks and whether prompt in-jections in tool outputs make them follow an attacker. OpenA-gentSafety [24] tests 356 workplace tasks with benign or malicious users and simulated colleagues. ST-WebAgentBench [11] grades 375 web tasks on completion and on compliance with explicit poli-cies, and OS-Harm [10] tests 150 desktop tasks for misuse, prompt injection, and costly mistakes. HANDBOOK.md [16] checks 65 pro-fessional tasks against standing policies. ClawsBench [12] grades safety constraints in 24 of its 44 tasks, and WildClawBench [6] in-cludes ten safety tasks among its 60, both with planted adversarial content. These benchmarks test whether an agent resists attacks or complies with stated policies. Similar to these benchmarks, Shell-Bench embeds safety criteria in everyday tasks, such as whether the agent withholds private information when replying to emails. What sets ShellBench apart is its emphasis on comparing safety outcomes across the same tasks for each model and harness at up to three effort levels. 

## 2.5 ShellBench 

The closest benchmarks each share part of ShellBench’s design. Workspace-Bench has large file workspaces with outdated file ver-sions, rubric grading, and several harnesses, but its tasks are profes-sional, and it grades only correctness. ClawsBench and WildClaw-Bench grade safety inside ordinary tasks across several harnesses, on tasks that are only partly everyday or file-based. Among the 27 benchmarks in Table 2, ShellBench is the only one that pairs every-day tasks and safety criteria with a nearly complete comparison of models, harnesses, and within-model reasoning effort (Section 4). Four independent annotators reviewed all tasks: two annotators accepted or rejected the environment, and two senior reviewers independently reviewed the finished task, which was kept only if both accepted it (Section 3). Seven of the other 26 benchmarks report that at least two people checked every task (Table 2).

## 3 Task details 3.1 Benchmark Design 

ShellBench tasks are designed based on a dataset of anonymized 10,456,576 real user queries observed from user traces of personal AI assistants. Users in this dataset opted in to share their traces for research and were asked not to disclose private information. This dataset and its aggregate statistics cannot be released to the public due to privacy concerns. To derive our tasks, we were able to cluster the user queries using the following methodology: (1) We embedded each query with Qwen3-Embedding-8B. (2) We performed K-means clustering across these to create clusters. (3) The largest 250 clusters were processed by GPT-6.1 Sol, which took 100 queries from each and determined whether the cluster was coherent. (4) 233 clusters passed this filtering, and GPT-6.1 Sol was fur-ther used to create an indicative scenario description to represent that cluster. These scenario descriptions served as the starting point for cre-ating ShellBench tasks, ensuring that all tasks were firmly rooted in common user requests and ground-truth examples. Next, these scenarios were expanded into typical reinforcement learning (RL) environments in the Harbor format and passed to independent developers. Task construction proceeded in six steps. (1) The developer is given the task scenario and instructed to create a realistic environment in which the task might take place, including realistic files that might be found on the user’s computer and applications accessible via API. (2) The environment is submitted to an automated grading system, which checks for environment realism. (3) Two separate human annotators then check any passing environments and either accept or reject them. (4) The original developer is then tasked with creating the full Harbor task from the environment. (5) Another round of automated accept/reject review is per-formed on the task, including rollouts generated on GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5. This round of review considers task difficulty and fairness. (6) A final round of human review is performed independently by two senior reviewers, and unless both accept the task, it is rejected.

To prevent developers from gaming the review system (con-sciously or unconsciously), if a task is rejected more than 3 times across this process, that task is rejected permanently. This method-ology, which Parsewave has deployed elsewhere to create RL tasks for leading AI labs, gives individual task authors significant leeway, resulting in maximum task diversity. Human review ensures that tasks are both fair and realistic. Developers were given strict in-structions on how to create LLM judge rubrics, but they were also allowed to create deterministic tests. This process ultimately results in a Harbor task holding files, emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, saved web pages, chat logs, receipts, and PDFs, as well as simulated applications. Some workspaces also contain phishing emails and outdated information. The 90 judge-graded tasks hold 16,296 files in total, and the ten code-tested tasks add 668. 

## 3.2 Rubrics and judging 

Each judge-graded task has a rubric of binary criteria, each a short true/false statement with a pass condition, marked as a correctness 

criterion (the work is right) or a safety criterion (the agent avoided a harmful or prohibited step). A task passes only if every criterion passes, which we call all-or-nothing grading. Across 77 tasks, 87 criteria are source criteria: the request asks which files the agent used, and the criterion checks that the answer names them. We identify them from the criterion identifiers, and they include cri-teria that ask the answer to name the record that settles a conflict. The judge is itself an agent. It opens the files the agent created or changed, grades one criterion per turn, receives at most two format corrections, and never sees the reference answer. In all cases, we use GPT-6.1 Sol at high reasoning effort as the judge model, running in the Pi harness. If no criterion fails but the judge finds one ambiguous, the run is unresolved , which we count as not passing. Of the 90 judge-graded tasks, 86 grade the final workspace (final-workspace tasks) and four grade an answer saved inside an application (app-state tasks); the other ten are graded by their own code tests (code-tested tasks). 

## 3.3 Example Task 

To give more context, we include some analysis of a task designated for public release, which we call the dinner task. This task asks the assistant to find one restaurant that all of the user’s dinner party attendees can reach, afford, and eat at (Figure 1). A saved listing shows Marlowe Bistro as open, but an email confirms a private hire for that evening. The available Baan Thai reservation is at the more distant of the two branches. The rubric requires the recommendation to name the files supporting its budget and timing. Of 531 attempts, 522 chose the right venue, 152 passed all nine criteria, and 331 failed on source naming alone (Figure 2). They received the same binary grade as a recommendation for a closed venue. With GPT-6.1 Sol, eight of nine Codex attempts failed on source naming alone, compared with one of nine OpenClaw attempts. Appendix E reproduces most of the task. In the portal handoff, rows from an obsolete export appear to fill gaps in the current one. In the clinic task, a private calendar entry helps the agent choose an appointment, but disclosing the entry in the reply violates the rubric. The dinner, portal, and clinic rubrics specify which uses of the evidence earn credit. 

## 4 Experimental setup 4.1 Model and Harness Configurations 

Model selection. We selected models from frontier AI labs along-side large open-weight models to compare how model and harness choices affect performance on everyday tasks. In a follow-up study, we plan to evaluate smaller open-weight models on ShellBench, including variants from the Nemotron and Qwen families. We evaluate twelve models (Table 1) in five agent harnesses with their default tools: Claude Code 2.1.292, Codex 0.163.0, Hermes v2026.9.24, OpenClaw 2026.10.5 and Pi 1.3.0. Every model runs every task in every harness at three reasoning-effort levels, low, medium, and high, with three attempts at each, between 7 and 20 November 2026. Kimi K3.1 Pro and the two DeepSeek models offer low, high, and maximum, which serve as their three levels, and effort levels are comparable within a model. There is one exception to the design. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash does not run in Hermes. Hermes’s standard configuration could not pin its requests to DeepSeek as the provider, and doing so would have required modifying the harness. Every other configuration uses each harness as shipped, with its default tools, so results from a modified Hermes would not be comparable with the rest, and we left that configuration out. ## 4.3 LLM Judge Consistency 

90 of the 100 tasks are scored using GPT-6.1 Sol at high effort as the LLM judge. This design choice allows open-ended answers, which is consistent with real-life AI agent use. Automated string checks are still appropriate in some cases, but they are often too brittle to handle arbitrary text output. The downside is that LLM judges are nondeterministic and may make incorrect determinations. To mitigate this possibility, we ensured that all rubrics were narrowly scoped. We also compared the judge verdicts to verdicts created by human graders. We assigned annotators to act in place of the LLM judge and grade 290 sampled submission–criterion decisions without seeing the original LLM judge verdicts. We sampled the decisions, stratified by criterion type and judge verdict. In each case, the annotators answered yes/no questions based on the criteria. The reference for a decision, which we call the consensus, is the majority label, or the single label where there is only one: 278 decisions have a consensus. Considering only the consensus decisions, the judge agrees with the human consensus 89.9% of the time (Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.75 [4]), and annotators ultimately confirmed 186 of the judge’s 198 passes but 64 of its 80 fails. The judge errs more often on criteria that annotators failed (passing 12 of 76) than on those they passed (failing 16 of 202). On safety criteria, annotators confirmed 81 of the judge’s 84 passes and 10 of its 17 fails. The judge and annotators agreed on 250 of the 278 decisions with a reference label. This comparison measures agreement on individual criteria in the stratified sample. It does not establish whole-task grading accuracy. 

## 5 Results 5.1 Models and harnesses 

Averaged over the harnesses, GPT-6 Astra passes 62.5% of tasks, Claude Fable 5.2 passes 61.9%, and GPT-6.1 Sol passes 61.4% (Fig-ure 3, Table 1; Appendix B gives every configuration by effort level). This amounts to a total spread among them of just 1.1 percentage points. The other nine models range from 58.7% (Gemini 4 Carbon) down to 18.8% (GPT-6 Luna). Across the 59 configurations, the mean single-attempt pass rate was 44.4% (Pass@1). When agents tried the same task three times at the same effort level, the mean rate was 61.2% for at least one success (Pass@3) and 28.2% for three successes (Pass 3). For GPT-6 Astra, the corresponding rates were 62.5%, 75.1%, and 48.8%. Across the eleven models evaluated in all five harnesses, model scores vary more than harness averages. ## 5.2 Harness variance 

Harnesses differ in how much they shift a model’s pass rate from their baseline. We measure this as the standard deviation of residu-als: each configuration’s score minus its model’s mean across all five harnesses over the eleven models with full harness coverage. OpenClaw has the smallest residual standard deviation (1.4 per-centage points): its scores primarily track model quality. Claude Code has the largest (4.7 percentage points), with the other three harnesses falling between 2.3 and 3.1 percentage points. The effect is sharpest in Claude Code. Muse Wonder Full scores 38.1%, 9.2 percentage points below its cross-harness mean, while GPT-6.1 Sol drops to 53.2%, 8.2 percentage points below its mean.

## 5.3 Cost and effort 

The three leading models differ far more in cost than in pass rate (Figure 4). Averaged over the harnesses, a run costs an estimated $0.19 with GPT-6.1 Sol, $0.83 with Claude Fable 5.2, and $1.10 with GPT-6 Astra, meaning GPT-6.1 Sol costs about one sixth as much as GPT-6 Astra. GPT-6 Astra costs more than Claude Fable 5.2 on average, though not across all harnesses. Harness choice also affects cost. Averaged over the ten models with a cost in all five harnesses, a run costs $0.29 in Pi, $0.32 in Codex, $0.33 in Hermes, $0.51 in Claude Code, and $0.53 in OpenClaw (Table 8). Pi is the cheapest harness for nine of the eleven models whose Pi runs record tokens, while the most expensive harness is either OpenClaw, Claude Code, or Codex (for Gemini 4 Carbon), depending on the model. All our cost observations pertain only to the model’s solution and exclude the LLM judge calls required for grading. Eleven of the twelve models evaluated at three effort levels scored higher at their highest effort than at their lowest. Gains ranged from 0.4 percentage points for Muse Wonder Full to 19.1 for Claude Sonnet 5.5. The three leading models gained 2.9 to 4.2 points. Gemini 4 Carbon fell 4.1 points (Figure 5). 

## 5.4 Behavior and resource use 

While the overall impact of harness choice on pass rate is small, harness choice does impact agent behavior (Figure 6). Compared with each model’s own average across harnesses, all eleven models with complete harness coverage read more file text in Claude Code (61% more on average) and rewrite their answers much more often (53% more). All eleven read less in Hermes and Pi (20% and 28% less than average). Tool calls in Claude Code rise by 59% on average; this is especially the case for the three OpenAI models (144% to 184% more). Regarding time, Pi is the fastest harness and OpenClaw the slowest: pooled across the eleven models, the median run takes 1.31 minutes in Pi and 2.86 in OpenClaw. Model-wise, the slowest harness takes 1.5 to 3.3 times as long as the fastest. Pi also uses the fewest input tokens; across the ten models measured in all five harnesses, Pi averages 192,000 input and 7,800 output tokens per run, against 500,000 and 10,800 in Claude Code. 

## 5.5 Safety by model and harness 

Across all 53,100 rollouts, 15.7% fail a safety criterion. The three leading models fail least often: GPT-6 Astra in 9.4% of runs, GPT-6.1 Sol in 9.7%, and Claude Fable 5.2 in 12.2%. DeepSeek V4 Pro fails most often (24.4%). Gemini 4 Carbon, fourth on pass rate, fails in 20.6%, which is 11.1 percentage points more than GPT-6 Astra (95% paired task-resampling interval [+6.4, +16.1]) and 6.8 more among runs that left files ([+2.1, +11.9]). The harness makes little difference to safety. Across 55 config-urations of the eleven shared models, model choice accounts for 75.5% of the variation in the share of runs failing a safety crite-rion, and harness choice for 4.7%. Claude Code, the highest harness, exceeds Codex, the lowest, by 3.0 percentage points (95% paired task-resampling interval [+1.9, +4.1]), and by 0.5 among runs that left files ([-0.4, +1.3]). ## 5.6 Which criteria fail 

Individual safety criteria pass 94.0% of the time, and the failures con-centrate in a few of them. The ten most-failed of the 362 safety cri-teria hold 39.0% of all failed safety criteria. On a release-designated freight reconciliation task, 214 of 531 submissions (40.3%) recom-mended a duplicate or incorrect payment. The most-failed safety criterion in the release subset requires a plan to keep a child’s school pickup covered, and 422 of 531 submissions (79.5%) failed it. An answer that says nothing about the pickup also fails it. By our reading of the rubric text, the same holds for six of the ten most-failed safety criteria in the release subset (Table 10), so these partly measure whether the answer is complete. 

## 6 Limitations 6.1 LLM Judges 

Ninety tasks rely on an LLM judge, whose verdict can vary between evaluations [3, 32]. An alternative is to combine scores from judges drawn from different model families [9]. Our blinded comparison yielded a reference label for 278 of 290 sampled criterion decisions (95.9%), including decisions with a single human rating. GPT-6.1 Sol agreed with that label on 89.9% of the 278 decisions (§4.3). We did not obtain independent human verdicts for whole submissions. 

## 6.2 Model Coverage and Compatibility 

The results cover twelve models, and other models may respond to the harnesses differently. A harness comparison changes the harness’s prompt, tools, and runtime together, so it does not show which of them causes a difference. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash was not run in Hermes, because pinning its requests to DeepSeek would have required modifying the harness (§4). For that reason, harness comparisons use the eleven models run in all five harnesses. The evaluation covers five harness versions. Other harnesses, including Meta’s Muse harness, are candidates for a future comparison. The safety results count failed safety criteria, which record com-pliance with each task’s rubric, but this study does not include a principled way to measure harm. This study provides only a binary safe/unsafe determination, which does not match the granularity of real-world safety consequences. Further work could benefit from more nuanced safety grading. Given the importance of safety in this context, further, more nuanced analysis could also determine how similar LLM judgments are to human judgments on safety criteria (§4.3). 

## 6.3 Rollout-Based Estimates 

Given practical constraints, we could not obtain model costings directly from providers, so all our costs are estimates based on our recorded rollout tokens at list prices. We use heuristics to judge which tokens were cache hits vs cache misses, and these likely do not match real caching behavior exactly. OpenClaw’s tool-output redaction also limits comparisons of how much file text agents read (§4). 

## 7 Conclusion 

Average pass rates ranged from 18.8% to 62.5% across the 12 models. The three leaders finished within 1.1 percentage points of one another. GPT-6.1 Sol passed 61.4% of attempts at an estimated token cost of $0.19 per attempt, compared with 61.9% at $0.83 for Claude

These estimates exclude the cost of grading. In OpenClaw, Sol passed 63.0% of attempts at $0.33 each, while Astra passed 62.4% at $1.94 (Tables 1 and 8). Astra’s lead over Fable also depended on the harness. It led by 4.3 percentage points in Pi and trailed by 7.6 in Claude Code. The harness ranking also depended on what the rubric required. When we rescored the same submissions without the source-use and stale-information criteria, Codex moved from fourth to first (Table 7). Of the 26,151 attempts that met every correctness criterion, 2,589 (9.9%) still failed a safety requirement. Our human review covered individual criteria. In a separate check, six of 40 unchanged sub-missions received both a pass and a fail over repeated automated judgments. Independent reviewers could grade whole submissions and record what they had to check or correct before they could use each one.

## C Safety results by configuration and criterion 

The overall safety analysis in §5.5 covers all 53,100 runs on the 100 tasks, where a run on a task without safety criteria counts as passing them. Of these, 8,340 failed a safety criterion: 2,589 met every correctness criterion, and 5,751 also failed one. A further 21,197 failed only correctness criteria, 23,552 passed, and eleven were unresolved. We count a run as leaving no files when it failed every criterion of its task (711 runs). The Correct column of Table 9 uses the 26,151 runs that met every correctness criterion. Runs that leave no files exceed 2.0% of runs in four of the 59 configurations, all in Pi or Claude Code (Table 9).
