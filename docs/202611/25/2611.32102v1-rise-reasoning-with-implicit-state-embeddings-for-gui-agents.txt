## Abstract 

Graphical user interface (GUI) agents translate user in-structions and screenshots into executable actions. To learn executable actions, existing approaches often rely on ex-plicit reasoning during training, which encourage over-reliance on specific reasoning patterns rather than gener-alizable action-relevant information. In this paper, we pro-pose an implicit framework, RISE, which integrates action-relevant reasoning into implicit state representations to guide GUI action prediction. To this end, action-relevant reasoning is captured through three complementary super-vision objectives: full-CoT state alignment preserves in-formation across the reasoning process, action-distribution alignment encodes action preferences, and reasoning-to-action boundary alignment captures the representation im-mediately before action generation. In this case, our RISE effectively learns informative implicit representations, thereby improving the accuracy of next-action token predic-tion. RISE achieves 70.6% accuracy on OSWorld-G, estab-lishing state-of-the-art performance among compared 8B models with a 4.3-point gain over its backbone. Results across two distinct backbones and six diverse benchmarks further demonstrate its effectiveness in GUI grounding and action prediction. Our code is available on our project page.

Ex-isting approaches commonly employ autoregressive vision-language models (VLMs) to generate actions as textual se-quences in a reasoning process [16, 24, 39]. These methods typically serialize thoughts, operation types, coordinate val-ues, and free-text inputs through a unified token-by-token generation process [3]. Despite these advances, such an explicit reasoning pro-cess does not necessarily ensure reliable alignment between reasoning and GUI action prediction. This limitation man-ifests in two aspects. (1) Reasoning diversity: Explicit reasoning can express the same intended action through different reasoning paths, introducing variability in textual reasoning even when the target action remains unchanged. Learning on explicit reasoning may encourage reliance on specific reasoning expressions rather than shared action-1arXiv:2611.32102v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 relevant information. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the in-struction ‘Save the changes’ may lead to different reason-ing paths, such as ‘Edits are complete, commit them’ and ‘Prevent data loss, save the edits’, both resulting in the same action, Click(Save) . (2) Weak reasoning–action coupling: Although actions are generated following ex-plicit reasoning, conventional token-level supervision does not explicitly enforce semantic consistency between reason-ing and the predicted action. Consequently, as shown in Fig. 1(b), the generated reasoning ‘Save the updated set-tings’ suggests saving the changes, yet the model predicts 

Click(Cancel) , contradicting its preceding reasoning. In this paper, we explore implicit reasoning for GUI ac-tion prediction. Compared with explicit reasoning genera-tion, implicit representations can capture action-relevant in-formation without relying on specific textual expressions. By learning from action prediction objectives, implicit states can encode reasoning information in a form directly useful for action prediction, without requiring intermediate reasoning text. This motivates us to leverage implicit states to guide GUI action prediction. This objective presents two key challenges. (1) Captur-ing shared reasoning information: Reasoning paths leading to the same action may differ substantially in length, word-ing, and intermediate steps, making it difficult to condense their shared action-relevant information into a fixed num-ber of implicit states. These states must also retain the vi-sual and instructional context needed for action prediction. (2) Ensuring effective reasoning–action coupling: Aligning implicit states with explicit reasoning representations alone does not guarantee that the action decoder will utilize the learned information. The implicit states must directly con-tribute to operation selection and visual target localization without losing the fine-grained visual information required for accurate grounding. In this paper, we propose RISE (Reasoning with Implicit State Embeddings), a framework that learns action-relevant reasoning through implicit states, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c). Specifically, RISE introduces learnable embeddings that are contextualized by the current screenshot and instruction to construct input-dependent implicit states. We first employ full-CoT state alignment to condense information across the reasoning process into a fixed number of implicit states, al-lowing the model to learn from reasoning without reproduc-ing specific textual expressions. We then introduce action-distribution supervision to capture action preferences, en-couraging the implicit states to retain information useful for distinguishing among possible actions rather than merely representing the reasoning process. We further perform reasoning-to-action boundary alignment to capture the rep-resentation immediately before action generation, providing complementary supervision on how reasoning information is organized for action prediction. Together, these strate-gies enable RISE to leverage implicit reasoning alongside the original visual and textual information for GUI action prediction without generating intermediate reasoning text. Our RISE achieves 70.6% accuracy on OSWorld-G, establishing state-of-the-art performance among the com-pared 8B models and outperforming its backbone baseline by 4.3 percentage points. Extensive experiments across six benchmarks further demonstrate the effectiveness of RISE in GUI grounding and action prediction. Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose RISE, an implicit reasoning framework that learns action-relevant information through contextualized implicit states to guide GUI action prediction without generating intermediate reasoning text. • We introduce three complementary supervision strate-gies that connect intermediate reasoning representations, action distributions, and reasoning-to-action boundary states to action prediction. • We demonstrate improvements in GUI grounding and offline action prediction across two backbones and six benchmarks, supported by matched comparisons, super-vision ablations, and inference-efficiency analyses. 

## 2. Related Work 

GUI Action Learning. Visual GUI agents learn to map screenshots and user instructions to executable actions, in-cluding operation selection and target localization. Early studies establish visual grounding capabilities through screenshot–language pretraining and cross-platform inter-action data [3, 6, 11, 34]. Recent approaches further in-corporate task interpretation and intermediate reasoning into action demonstrations to improve GUI action predic-tion [17, 24, 38, 39]. Feedback-based methods enhance action learning through preference optimization or reward-guided training [19, 21, 22, 24]. While these studies ad-vance GUI action learning through richer demonstrations and optimization strategies, RISE focuses on how action-relevant reasoning information is represented and utilized. Specifically, it learns implicit reasoning states that guide action prediction alongside the original screenshot and in-struction, without generating intermediate reasoning text. 

Implicit Reasoning. Implicit reasoning models inter-mediate computation through continuous representations rather than explicit reasoning text. Prior studies explore learning implicit representations through hidden-state su-pervision and continuous CoT compression [5, 26, 27], as well as recurrent and parallel latent reasoning mecha-nisms [9, 32]. Learnable continuous embeddings and ad-ditional computation positions provide alternative ways to condition model predictions [7, 13, 15]. In GUI inter-action, MIRAGE combines latent reasoning with a gen-erative world-model objective that aligns reasoning rep-resentations with future screenshots [41]. ## 3. Method 

An overview of RISE is presented in Fig. 2. We first formu-late GUI action prediction, then describe the construction of implicit states and three complementary supervision strate-gies, and finally present the training objective. 

3.1. Formulation 

Given a screenshot, a user instruction, and any available interaction history, the GUI agent constructs a multimodal prefix P ∈ Rn×D , where n is the input sequence length and 

D is the hidden dimension. Let s = ( s1, . . . , s L) denote an executable action serialized in the backbone’s native output format, where sj is the token at position j and L is the se-quence length. 3.2. Implicit State Construction 

RISE appends the learnable embeddings Z to the multi-modal prefix P to construct contextualized implicit states: 

R = Fθ ([ P ; Z]) n+1: n+K , (2) where Fθ denotes the VLM backbone, [P ; Z] denotes se-quence concatenation, and the subscript selects the K ap-pended hidden states. The resulting R ∈ RK×D contains the contextual implicit states. Through causal attention, each action token can attend to both the original multimodal prefix and the implicit states, allowing the model to leverage implicit reasoning alongside the original visual and textual information. By default, we use K = 8 implicit positions. 

3.3. Action-Relevant Reasoning Alignment 

We use a frozen copy of the pretrained VLM as the teacher to provide three complementary supervision targets for 3learning action-relevant implicit states. Let q denote the an-notated CoT associated with each training example and ϕ

the teacher parameters. Given the multimodal prefix P , the teacher processes [P ; q], while the RISE student conditions on P and learnable embeddings Z without accessing ex-plicit CoT. RISE learns action-relevant implicit states from the teacher’s full CoT representations, action-token proba-bility distributions, and reasoning-to-action boundary state. 

Full-CoT State Alignment. We align the student’s implicit states R with representations aggregated from the teacher’s hidden states across the full CoT. Specifically, we pool the teacher’s CoT hidden states into K ordered target represen-tations T ∈ RK×D , excluding reasoning delimiters and ac-tion tokens. Let M denote the number of teacher hidden states within the CoT body. When M ≥ K, we average the states within each of K contiguous segments. When 

M < K , we sample K uniformly spaced positions and round them to token indices, reusing states as needed. This pooling preserves the order of the reasoning sequence. The state-alignment objective is defined as

## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate RISE on two backbones for GUI grounding and offline action prediction, with matched SFT comparisons, supervision ablations, and inference-cost measurements. 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. For GUI grounding, we use OSWorld-G [36] with original and refined instructions, ScreenSpot-v2 (SS-v2), UI-I2E-Bench [18], and MMBench-GUI L2 [30]. For offline action prediction, we use the GUI-Libra adap-tations [39] of AndroidControl [14] and Mind2Web [4], namely AndroidControl-v2 (AC-v2) and MM-Mind2Web-v2, evaluating action types, spatial arguments, and values. 

Configurations. RISE uses eight implicit states on both backbones and preserves their native action formats and image budgets. Both Qwen and GUI-Owl are initially trained on the same 81,418 examples. GUI-Owl under-goes additional training on 167,684 records to further im-prove grounding performance. Training uses up to eight PPU-ZW810E accelerators (96 GiB each). ## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose RISE (Reasoning with Im-plicit State Embeddings), an implicit reasoning framework for GUI action prediction. RISE constructs contextual-ized implicit states from learnable embeddings to capture action-relevant reasoning information without generating intermediate reasoning text. We introduce three comple-mentary supervision strategies, including full-CoT state alignment, action-distribution alignment, and reasoning-to-8action boundary alignment, to guide implicit state learning for action prediction. RISE achieves 70.6% accuracy on OSWorld-G with GUI-Owl, outperforming its backbone by 4.3 percentage points, and 95.0% on ScreenSpot-v2 with Qwen. Experiments across two backbones and six bench-marks demonstrate improvements in GUI grounding and of-fline action prediction, while inference analyses show re-duced decoding overhead compared with explicit reasoning.
