## Abstract 

GUI grounding requires resolving fine visual details within the context that gives them meaning. Additional views can improve localization, but their value depends on what ev-idence they expose and how much context they preserve. We explore a capability of vision-language models (VLMs): even when their coordinate predictions are incorrect, they can describe visual evidence worth inspecting. ## 1. Introduction 

Interacting with graphical user interfaces (GUIs) requires agents to ground natural-language instructions in precise 1arXiv:2611.31909v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 screen locations [17, 22, 30, 38]. Despite advances in vision-language models (VLMs), this task remains chal-lenging in complex interfaces, where identifying a target re-quires resolving fine-grained visual distinctions within the appropriate functional context [6, 8, 33]. These require-ments place competing demands on visual observation: a broad view preserves contextual relationships but can ob-scure small details, while a closer view reveals local appear-ance but may exclude the panel or landmark needed to inter-pret it [28, 39]. Effective GUI grounding therefore requires deciding not only where to act, but also where to look, at what scale, and which visual evidence to inspect. To address these challenges, prior work has explored view-based methods (Fig. 1(a)), including zooming [13, 14, 37] and multi-view inference [40] to expose visual details that are difficult to resolve from the full screenshot. These approaches highlight the value of additional observations, whose effectiveness depends on both the regions selected and the context retained. A central challenge is therefore to determine what evidence each new view should reveal: re-visiting similar regions may add little information, while an overly tight crop may remove the context needed to distin-guish plausible targets. Choosing an informative view thus requires understanding what remains to be resolved about the target. This raises a natural question: 

Can a VLM itself identify what to inspect next to improve GUI grounding? 

The example in Fig. 1(b) suggests that useful inspec-tion guidance can emerge even before accurate localization. Although the VLM initially predicts an incorrect location, it can still describe relevant context and distinguishing de-tails that guide further inspection. We elicit this capability through semantic probes : short, task-conditioned descrip-tions of visual evidence to seek for localization. Crucially, these probes provide both a semantic account of what to inspect and an attention-based spatial response indicating where that evidence may lie. By extracting visual attention associated with the probe’s descriptive tokens, we translate its language guidance into spatial evidence maps. These maps guide observation placement and the extent of sur-rounding context, connecting what to inspect with where and at what scale to look .Building on this idea, we propose SPARK , Semantic 

Probe-guided Adaptive Region see King , a training-free framework that uses a frozen VLM’s semantic guidance to construct a limited set of local observations. SPARK rep-resents inspection guidance through hierarchical semantic probes : Context identifies relevant regions and landmarks, while Detail specifies the visual evidence needed to iden-tify or verify the requested control. This shared structure supports two complementary forms of inspection. An Ex-ploration Probe , conditioned on the instruction, describes evidence to seek; a Verification Probe , additionally condi-tioned on emerging localization hypotheses, describes ev-idence to inspect when assessing those hypotheses. The spatial attention associated with these descriptions jointly guides view placement, while the Context response deter-mines how much surrounding evidence to retain. A shared adaptive focusing mechanism translates this guidance into crop locations and scales, using observation history to en-courage spatial diversity. Finally, uncertainty-weighted vot-ing integrates the resulting coordinate predictions into the final grounding decision. We evaluate SPARK on five GUI grounding benchmarks using general-purpose and GUI-specialized VLMs. On ScreenSpot-Pro, SPARK improves accuracy over single-view inference by an average of 18.4 percentage points (pp) across the three backbones, with gains of up to 25.2 pp. It also delivers consistent improvements across the remaining four benchmarks. Our contributions are as follows: (1) We introduce se-mantic probes to elicit a frozen VLM’s latent inspection ca-pability, connecting descriptions of task-relevant evidence with their spatial attention to guide where and at what scale to observe. (2) We propose SPARK , a training-free frame-work that unifies exploration and verification through hier-archical semantic probes, adaptively guiding where to in-spect and how much context to retain within a limited local-view budget. (3) We demonstrate consistent improvements of SPARK across five GUI grounding benchmarks and four VLM backbones. 

## 2. Related Work 

GUI Grounding. GUI grounding maps natural-language instructions to actionable screen locations [6, 12, 18]. SeeClick [6], OS-Atlas [29], and UGround-V1 [7] de-velop this capability through GUI-specific supervision. UI-TARS [19] integrates perception, action modeling, and rea-soning, while UI-TARS-1.5 [3] and GTA1 [34] further im-prove GUI capabilities through reinforcement learning. Building on these grounding capabilities, test-time meth-ods improve localization by exploiting pretrained mod-els without additional task-specific training. TAG [32] and Trifuse [15] exploit pretrained attention and com-plementary spatial cues. RegionFocus [14] and DiMo-GUI [26] use zooming, MVP [40] aggregates attention-guided views, DRS-GUI [13] schedules perceptual actions, and BAMI [37] combines coarse-to-fine focusing with can-didate selection. SPARK instead uses semantic inspection guidance to organize adaptive observations for exploration and verification. 

Active Visual Perception and Reasoning. Active visual perception uses task understanding to guide the acquisition of relevant visual evidence. V* [27] and ZoomEye [21] en-able visual search, Visual CoT [20] supports region local-2ization, VisRL [5] learns to focus, and ViCrop [39] uses internal model signals for training-free cropping. SPARK elicits hierarchical descriptions from a frozen VLM and translates their spatial attention into task- and hypothesis-conditioned observations for GUI grounding.

3.2. Probe-Guided Adaptive Focusing 

SPARK organizes view construction around a hierarchical representation of inspection requirements. Different condi-tioning signals determine the purpose of inspection, while a shared attention-based mechanism translates these require-ments into adaptive local views.

## 4. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks, backbones and compared methods. We evaluate SPARK on five GUI grounding benchmarks: ScreenSpot-{Pro, v2, v1 } [6, 11, 29], UI-Vision [16], and OSWorld-G [31]. Our primary backbones are two general-purpose VLMs, Qwen3-VL-8B [1] and Qwen2.5-VL-7B [2], and a GUI-specialized model, UI-TARS-1.5-7B [3]. We additionally evaluate Qwen3-VL-32B [1] to examine scalability to a larger backbone. Our comparisons cover general-purpose VLMs, GUI-specialized models, and test-time grounding methods. The first two categories estab-lish single-view baselines, while the third includes Region-Focus [14], MVP [40], DRS-GUI [13], and BAMI [37]. 

Implementation details. All localization calls use greedy decoding and fixed backbone-specific prompts, with at most four local views per screenshot. For MVP, we adopted the same local-view budget to ensure comparability. We ex-tract attention at layer index 24 for Qwen3-VL-8B, 48 for Qwen3-VL-32B, and 20 for Qwen2.5-VL and UI-TARS, taking the per-patch maximum across heads and averaging over probe content queries.

Conclusion 

We presented SPARK , a training-free framework that turns a frozen VLM’s semantic understanding into guidance for visual inspection. Hierarchical probes support explo-ration and hypothesis-conditioned verification, translating descriptions of relevant evidence into spatial attention that guides view placement and contextual scope. History-aware selection encourages complementary local views, while uncertainty-weighted voting integrates their predic-tions. Experiments across GUI grounding benchmarks and backbones demonstrate consistent improvements over single-view inference and strong performance against exist-ing test-time methods. SPARK shows that knowing what to inspect can help a VLM determine where to act , connecting semantic understanding with precise GUI grounding.

## A. More Related Work 

Recent preprints investigate uncertainty-guided search and visual verification for training-free GUI grounding. UI-Zoomer [23] combines spatial consensus and generation confidence to trigger zooming, and adapts crop size to pre-diction variance. AutoFocus [35] converts sampled coor-dinates and their axial perplexities into spatial proposals, followed by visual candidate comparison. MHVF [4] ex-plores multiple hypotheses through zoomed re-localization and uses local verification scores to guide further search and final selection. AdaGround [25] adapts zoom parameters to estimated target size and uses a marked-click verifier to guide acceptance and refinement. SPARK derives observation guidance from explicit de-scriptions of the evidence to inspect. Attention associated with hierarchical probe content jointly guides view place-ment, while the exploration Context response determines the surrounding context to retain. Its verification probe describes evidence needed to assess emerging hypotheses, turning verification into guidance for constructing a new observation. Final coordinate predictions are integrated through uncertainty-weighted spatial voting. This design connects semantic evidence descriptions to adaptive visual inspection, complementing search strategies guided by co-ordinate uncertainty and candidate verification scores.

## F. Uncertainty-weighted Voting for MVP 

We apply uncertainty-weighted voting to MVP to examine its effectiveness beyond SPARK’s view construction. As shown in Table 7, this aggregation improves MVP across all three backbones, reaching 57.1%, 50.3%, and 66.4% with UI-TARS, Qwen2.5-VL, and Qwen3-VL, respectively. SPARK further achieves 58.2%, 52.5%, and 68.7%. These results demonstrate that uncertainty weighting can improve existing multi-view predictions, while SPARK’s complete framework delivers additional gains through probe-guided observation and aggregation.

## G. Comparison of Baseline Methods with More Time Budget 

As shown in Table 8, SPARK’s average inference time of 16.79 seconds falls between those of MVP with six and eight local views (13.75–17.53 seconds), while achiev-ing 3.2–3.5 percentage points higher accuracy. Increasing MVP’s view budget does not close this gap, consistent with its reported view-count ablation, where additional views yield limited gains and can even reduce accuracy [40]. 

## H. Performance on the Development Subset and Remaining Examples 

We developed SPARK and selected its 7B/8B hyperparam-eters using Qwen3-VL-8B on a 300-example development subset of ScreenSpot-Pro. We used the same settings for all evaluated 7B/8B backbones across all benchmarks with-out further tuning. For Qwen3-VL-32B, we selected hyper-parameters separately on the same development subset and held them fixed across all evaluated benchmarks. Table 9 reports accuracy on the development subset, the remaining 1,281 examples, and the full ScreenSpot-Pro benchmark. SPARK outperforms MVP on both subsets with both Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3-VL-32B. Together with the results on other backbones and benchmarks in the main paper, these findings suggest that the selected hyper-parameters transfer across benchmarks and backbones. 3I. Limitations and Future Work 

SPARK improves GUI grounding through training-free, probe-guided observations. As with other multi-view grounding methods, processing additional views incurs an inference cost. Adaptive stopping could help allocate the observation budget according to the evidence needed for each instruction. The usefulness of semantic guidance also depends on the backbone’s ability to describe relevant vi-sual evidence and associate it with spatial attention. Future work could explore more robust probe extraction and com-plementary grounding signals. 4
