Abstract
Wearable assistants must localize interface elements from
camera observations when direct screenshot access is un-
available, yet existing GUI grounding benchmarks primar-
ily evaluate native screenshots. We introduce GlassGround,
a new benchmark that pairs native screenshots with wear-
able camera observations while holding GUI states, in-
structions, and targets fixed. GlassGround contains 3,250
tasks across Android and iOS, captured using four wear-
able devices under four viewing conditions.
Across ten
open-source models, mean grounding accuracy drops from
94.8% on screenshots to 48.5% on camera images. Further
analysis reveals that changes in target scale, screen geom-
etry, and background cannot fully explain this performance
degradation. We then study two ways to narrow this gap. At
inference time, local zooming and screen rectification help
but leave most of the gap. For training-time adaptation, we
build on GlassGround’s paired-view design and introduce
PairDistill, a baseline that aligns target representations
across views alongside coordinate supervision. Trained on
independently collected data and using no screenshots at
inference, it improves camera-image accuracy by 9.4 and
12.2 points on two backbones and transfers to unseen de-
vices and operating systems. Combined with local zoom-
ing, it reaches 69.6% and 64.4%, but does not close the
gap. The GlassGround dataset and code are available at
https://github.com/yunnw/GlassGround.
1. Introduction
Wearable AI assistants on smart glasses can guide users
through smartphone tasks using egocentric camera views of
the phone screen [18]. For example, when a user is learning
an unfamiliar application, an assistant needs to identify the
next action based on the current screen and update its guid-
ance after the user acts [7]. Making this guidance actionable

requires visual GUI grounding, which localizes the inter-
face element corresponding to an action instruction, typi-
cally in a native screenshot [4, 19]. However, unlike con-
ventional GUI agents that operate directly on digital inter-
faces, wearable assistants may observe a smartphone only
through their cameras, without direct access to its screen-
shots or underlying interface state [50]. In these egocentric
camera views, the interface may occupy only a small part
of the frame and exhibit perspective distortion, while illu-
mination, reflections, and display-camera imaging effects
can reduce the visibility or legibility of its content [33, 49].
These visual changes may affect grounding: a model
can correctly localize a target in a screenshot yet fail
on the corresponding camera image (Fig. 1). Evaluating
physical GUI grounding therefore requires a benchmark
that captures real camera observations while controlling
for differences in task content.
However, existing GUI
grounding benchmarks [4, 19] primarily evaluate ground-
ing on native screenshots, while camera-captured screen
datasets [49, 55] focus on image restoration rather than
instruction-conditioned grounding.
To address this need, we introduce GlassGround, a new
benchmark for evaluating GUI grounding on real wearable

camera observations. It pairs native screenshots with cam-
era observations of the same GUI state, instruction, and tar-
get. To construct these pairs, we display each screenshot
on its original smartphone and capture the interface using
multiple wearable devices under varied viewing conditions.
Grounding tasks are constructed on native screenshots and
shared across their paired camera observations to avoid the
exclusion of targets that are difficult to discern in cam-
era images. This paired design enables us to compare the
grounding performance of the same model on native screen-
shots and camera observations, and to analyze performance
differences across devices and viewing conditions. When
evaluated on camera images, models receive only the image
and instruction, without access to the paired screenshot.
Our evaluation using GlassGround reveals a substantial
physical grounding gap. Across ten open-source models,
mean accuracy drops from 94.8% on native screenshots to
48.5% on physical observations, with considerable variation
across devices and lighting conditions. To investigate this
gap, we construct intermediate observations that account
for changes in target scale, screen geometry, and back-
ground. Yet the remaining gap between these transformed
screenshots and real camera images still accounts for nearly
two-thirds of the original degradation. We further evaluate
ReGround, a local crop-and-reground strategy to improve
grounding on small targets [19, 54]. Although ReGround
improves accuracy, it leaves the gap between transformed
screenshots and physical observations largely unchanged.
Building on GlassGround’s paired-view design, we fur-
ther introduce PairDistill, which uses target representa-
tions from native screenshots to supervise corresponding
representations in physical observations, alongside coordi-
nate supervision. We construct training pairs by displaying
annotated GUI screenshots on smartphones and capturing
them with wearable cameras. On GlassGround, PairDis-
till improves camera image grounding accuracy by 9.4 and
12.2 pp for UI-Venus-2-9B and MAI-UI-8B, respectively.
Combined with ReGround, their accuracies further increase
to 69.6% and 64.4%. These gains also generalize to un-
seen wearable devices, viewing conditions, and operating
systems during training. Our contributions are as follows.
• We introduce GlassGround, a benchmark for controlled
comparisons of GUI grounding on native screenshots and
real wearable camera observations.
• We characterize the observation gap through intermediate
observation stages, showing that most of the gap remains
between transformed screenshots and real camera images.
• We show that inference-time processing and training-time
adaptation (PairDistill) provide complementary gains,
narrowing but not closing the physical grounding gap.
2. Related Work
2.1. Visual GUI Grounding and Benchmarks
Visual GUI grounding localizes the interface element re-
ferred to by a natural language instruction and is a ba-
sic capability of GUI agents. Grounding models such as
UGround [9], OS-Atlas [40], and GUI-Actor [39] learn this
capability from large collections of GUI screenshots, and
GUI agents such as Aguvis [44] and UI-TARS [30] build on
it to operate interfaces from screenshots. Grounding bench-
marks have expanded alongside these models, starting with
ScreenSpot [4] across mobile, desktop, and web interfaces.
ScreenSpot-Pro [19] extends evaluation to small targets
in high-resolution professional software, OSWorld-G [42]
and VenusBench-GD [53] cover more complex ground-
ing tasks, and IGG [14] studies targets that become visi-
ble only after interaction. Recently, ScreenHaystack [17],
WinDeskGround [52], and GUI-Perturbed [37] analyze
grounding robustness by controlling target position, win-
dow occlusion, interface layout and rendering, and instruc-
tion phrasing. Whether they broaden task coverage or probe
robustness, these benchmarks evaluate grounding on digital
screenshots or rendered interfaces. GlassGround instead
evaluates GUI grounding on physical screens observed
through wearable cameras, introducing real display-to-
camera imaging effects into the evaluation.
2.2. Camera-Observed and Physical Interfaces
Prior work has examined camera-observed physical in-
terfaces from several perspectives. Screen recapture and
demoir´eing studies use the correspondence between source
images and their photographs to analyze imaging degrada-
tion [48, 49, 51], and PC-Restore [55] further provides pairs
of real camera-captured GUI images and clean references
for image restoration with OCR-based evaluation. Screen-
shotMatcher [33] retrieves the native screenshot that cor-
responds to a photograph of a screen, and document rec-
tification methods recover canonical geometry from pho-
tographs [5, 8, 24].
Systems such as RoScript [29],
NiCro [41], RoboTest [46], and BrushLens [20] use exter-
nal cameras to recognize, match, or operate elements on
physical interfaces. Egocentric studies address visual in-
tention grounding [34], visual question answering on smart
glasses [12], pointing comprehension [16], and the transi-
tion from scene perception to web tasks [47]. Unlike prior
camera-based interface work, GlassGround benchmarks
instruction-conditioned GUI grounding from wearable
views, pairing camera observations with native screen-
shots to measure the grounding gap on identical tasks.

3.1. Paired Task Formulation
A GlassGround task is defined as a triplet (s, q, e): a GUI
state s, an instruction q, and the target element e ∈E(s)
that q refers to, where E(s) is the set of UI elements in s.
An observation I is an image showing the interface in s.
For a given grounding task (s, q, e), each observation yields
an evaluation instance (I, q, y), where y is the region in I
corresponding to e. Given an instance, a grounding model
receives only I and q and predicts a point ˆp ∈R2 in the
coordinates of I. The prediction is correct if ˆp ∈y.
We consider two observation processes that produce dif-
ferent visual inputs for the same grounding task. The clean
observation Ic = Oc(s) is the native screenshot of the
GUI state. The physical observation Ip = Op(s; ϕ) is an
egocentric image of the same state displayed on a physical
screen and captured by a wearable device (see Fig. 2(a)).
Here, ϕ denotes the capture setting. For each GUI state, we
obtain one clean observation and collect K physical obser-
vations under different capture settings {ϕk}K

3.2. Physical Display Capture Protocol
We instantiate the physical observation process Op through
a capture protocol designed to preserve identical GUI con-
tent while varying the conditions under which it is observed.
Specifically, we consider two major sources of variation:
wearable imaging devices and viewing conditions. Their
cross product defines K = 16 capture settings, comprising
four devices and four viewing conditions (Fig. 2(b)).
Preserving GUI content.
Live interfaces may change
across captures due to dynamic content. To preserve an
3


identical GUI state across all paired observations, we dis-
play each native screenshot Ic full-screen on its original
smartphone instead of repeatedly navigating to the same
live interface. All physical observations of a state thus share
identical GUI content, so their differences from Ic arise
from the physical observation process.
Imaging devices.
Different wearable devices produce
distinct image characteristics due to differences in optics,
sensors, exposure control, and image signal processing
(ISP) [28, 32]. To capture this variation, we include Meta
Project Aria Gen 1 and Gen 2, research egocentric glasses
that record from onboard RGB cameras, and Meta Quest 3
and Samsung Galaxy XR, consumer XR headsets whose im-
ages are obtained through their built-in capture functions.
Viewing conditions.
We capture each GUI state under
four lighting conditions that affect screen visibility and leg-
ibility [21]: Indoor-Dark (room lights off), Indoor-Lamp
(fixed indoor lighting), Outdoor-Shade (daylight without di-
rect sunlight on the phone), and Outdoor-Sun (phone di-
rectly exposed to sunlight).
Handheld capture and quality control.
Operators wear
the device and hold the phone naturally without a mount, al-
lowing screen position, viewing distance, and viewing angle
to vary within each capture setting. Display settings remain
fixed for each phone and viewing condition, with adaptive
brightness disabled. We retain device-specific appearance
differences and natural artifacts such as mild blur, reflec-
tions, and partial occlusion, but retake captures if any target
falls partially outside the image boundary (Supp. Sec. 8.1).
3.3. Grounding Task Construction
We construct grounding tasks on native screenshots and
reuse them across the corresponding physical observations.
For each state s, we define an instruction q and target ele-
ment e from the clean screenshot Ic, together with the corre-
sponding target region yc (Fig. 2(c)). We avoid using phys-
ical observations here, as doing so could preferentially omit
tasks whose physical observations are hard to recognize.
To generate candidate tasks, we first identify poten-
tial target regions using OmniParser [22], supplemented by
OCR to recover text regions that the parser may miss. We
then assign Set-of-Mark labels [45] to the candidates, and
query a VLM (Claude Fable 5.1 [2]) for each candidate in-
dividually. It judges whether the candidate is a suitable tar-
get and, if so, writes a grounding instruction without pre-
dicting a target region. It also classifies each instruction as
text, visual, functional, or positional according to its pri-
mary grounding cue (see Supp. Sec. 8.2).
Since generated instructions may contain ambiguous in-
structions or invalid targets, human annotators review every
candidate. They verify target validity and clickability, in-
struction clarity and type, and bounding-box accuracy. An-
notators may retain, revise, or reject proposals and add valid
tasks missed by the automated pipeline. This yields the fi-
nal q, e, and human-verified region yc. The resulting tasks
are shared across all K physical observations of each GUI
state, with only their target regions transferred (Sec. 3.4).

3.5. Benchmark Statistics
GlassGround contains 536 GUI states from 102 applica-
tions across iOS and Android, comprising 303 iOS states
and 233 Android states, with 3,250 grounding tasks in to-
tal. The applications span eight categories: System, Media,
Work, Shopping, Travel, Lifestyle, Social, and Web. Cate-
gory, application, and instruction-type distributions are pro-
vided in Supp. Sec. 8.2. Each GUI state is captured un-
der the full 4 × 4 cross product of wearable devices and
viewing conditions, yielding 8,576 unique physical images.
Because multiple grounding tasks may share the same GUI
state, these captures correspond to 52,000 task–observation
instances. Thus, each of the 3,250 tasks forms one clean
observation paired with 16 physical observations.

4.1. Evaluation Setup
Models.
Our model selection prioritizes recent GUI-
specialized models with strong reported performance on
screenshot-based GUI grounding benchmarks, allowing
us to examine whether their grounding capabilities trans-
fer to physical observations.
Our open-source suite in-
cludes eight GUI-specialized models: MAI-UI-8B [54],
GUI-Owl-1.5-8B and GUI-Owl-1.5-32B [43], KV-Ground-
8B [15], UI-Venus-2-9B [35], UI-Mate-27B [6], Holo3.1-
35B-A3B [10], and Indeed-UI-32B [11]. To broaden the
comparison beyond GUI-specialized models, we evaluate
two general-purpose VLMs, Muse Glimmer-30B [25] and
Qwen3.8-27B [31]. For closed-source baselines, we con-
sider inference efficiency for wearable assistance and select
Claude Sonnet 5.5 [1] and GPT-6 Luna [26], two efficiency-
oriented VLMs, and Jev 1.14 [36], which targets fast struc-
tured prediction. Open-source models are evaluated on the
full benchmark, while closed-source models use a fixed sub-
set comprising 500 clean and 8,000 physical instances.
Inference and evaluation.
By default, the model pre-
dicts the target location from the input image and instruc-
tion in a single call (Single-pass). In physical observations,
the screen occupies only part of the image, so target ele-
ments occupy a smaller fraction of it than in clean observa-
tions. We therefore also evaluate ReGround, following prior
work showing that region-focused refinement can improve
small-target GUI grounding [19, 23, 27, 54]. ReGround re-
localizes the target in an enlarged crop around the initial
prediction, using at most one additional model call. For
evaluation, we use the predicted point or the center of the
predicted bounding box, depending on the model’s output
format. Grounding accuracy is the percentage of evaluated
instances satisfying the point-in-polygon criterion. Further
details and matched-subset comparisons are in Supp. Sec. 9.

5. Learning from Paired GUI Observations
The previous analysis reveals that existing GUI grounding
models struggle with camera-captured screen content, even
after inference-time processing. Building on GlassGround’s
paired-view design, we present PairDistill, which explores
leveraging corresponding native screenshots and physical
observations to improve grounding through representation
alignment. Specifically, PairDistill aligns representations of
the same GUI target across the two views while supervising
target prediction. Native screenshots provide supervision
only during training. At inference, the adapted model takes
only the physical image and task instruction.

6. Adaptation Results
Experimental setup.
Following Sec. 5.1, we construct
training data independently of GlassGround from Android
screenshots in AMEX [3], displayed on a smartphone and
recaptured with a fixed rig. The training set contains 4,636
image pairs, covering 76,224 targets across four device and

are selected on a screenshot-disjoint AMEX validation split,
and all adapted results are averaged over three seeds. By

(ablated in Table 2). Further details are in Supp. Sec. 11.

7. Conclusion and Limitations
We presented GlassGround, a paired benchmark that holds
GUI states, instructions, and targets fixed to study ground-
ing across native screenshots and wearable camera observa-
tions. Our analysis reveals substantial losses when moving
from transformed screenshot content to real camera images,
even with local zooming. We further introduced PairDistill,
which uses target correspondences between paired views to
supervise grounding on physical images. Its gains extend
to unseen wearable devices, capture configurations, and
operating systems without paired screenshots at inference.
These results highlight observation conditions as an impor-
tant dimension of GUI grounding evaluation and demon-
strate the value of paired supervision for adaptation.
Our benchmark focuses on single-step grounding in
static images and does not evaluate complete interaction
sequences. The evaluation is also limited to smartphone
screens, and performance on other physical displays re-
mains to be assessed. Attributing the Warped-to-Physical
gap to specific imaging factors is also left to future work.

8. GlassGround Benchmark Details
8.1. Physical Capture Setup and Protocol
Source smartphones and display settings.
GlassGround
uses three smartphones to provide and display clean screen-
shots. The iOS subset contains 303 screenshots from an
iPhone 14 Pro at a resolution of 1179 × 2556. The Android
subset contains 102 screenshots from an AT&T Maestro 3
at 720 × 1600 and 131 screenshots from a Samsung Galaxy
S20 Ultra 5G at 1080 × 2400. Each screenshot is displayed
on its source smartphone, which is used consistently across
the corresponding 16 physical capture settings.
Adaptive brightness is disabled on all smartphones. In-
door captures use a fixed manual screen brightness, while
outdoor captures use the maximum brightness setting.
Indoor-Dark is captured with the room lights turned off,
and Indoor-Lamp uses normal laboratory lighting. Outdoor-
Shade is captured in outdoor shaded areas, while Outdoor-
Sun is captured under direct sunlight.
Wearable image acquisition.
Table 3 summarizes the
image acquisition methods, saved resolutions, and export
processing for the four wearable devices.
Frame selection and quality control.
Aria Gen 1 and
Aria Gen 2 continuously record RGB video, from which we
extract the images corresponding to each GUI state. We first
use a sharpness score based on the variance of the Laplacian
to identify candidate frames. Annotators then manually se-
lect frames with clear screen content and complete target
regions. Quest 3 and Galaxy XR instead capture system
passthrough images directly. Captures that do not satisfy
the acquisition requirements are retaken. For all devices,
observations with incorrect screen content or targets cut off
by the image boundary are recaptured or replaced.
Image processing.
Apart from the orientation adjustment
and format conversion listed in Table 3, no additional crop-
ping, resizing, image enhancement, or lens undistortion is
applied during dataset preparation.
Brightness-enhanced
images available in the annotation interface are used only
for visual inspection and are not included in the benchmark.
Model evaluation uses the stored physical images.

Automatic
screen
corner
initialization.
We
use
YOLO11n-pose [13] to predict the four ordered screen
corners in physical images.
The detector was initially
trained on 268 manually annotated pilot images not
included in GlassGround and progressively updated as
additional human-verified annotations became available.
The predicted corners provide an initial estimate that
annotators can directly inspect and refine, avoiding the
need to locate all four corners from scratch.
13


Since small corner localization errors can affect the
alignment of transferred GUI targets, later versions incorpo-
rate an additional target projection loss alongside the stan-
dard YOLO-pose loss. This loss penalizes discrepancies
between clean target boxes projected using predicted and
human-verified screen corners. The final detector was fine-
tuned from an earlier version on 5,852 images, comprising
268 pilot images, 3,526 previously annotated GlassGround
images, and 2,058 AMEX camera images. Training used an
input size of 960 pixels, 30 epochs, a batch size of 32, and
an initial learning rate of 0.001.
On 5,040 subsequent physical images not used to train
their corresponding detector versions, the median per-image
mean corner displacement relative to human-verified anno-
tations is 5.83 pixels, or 0.89% of the screen diagonal.
Human annotation protocol.
Six annotators participated
in physical image geometry verification, including two ex-
perienced annotators responsible for quality control. An-
notators corrected the predicted screen corners and in-
spected the target regions transferred from human-verified
clean screenshots. When transferred regions remained mis-
aligned, annotators refined the geometric mapping through
the screen corners or internal grid control points. Physical
target regions were thus obtained through verified geomet-
ric transfer rather than independent target annotation.
Mesh refinement.
A single homography may not fully
capture local geometric deviations caused by camera imag-
ing or image processing.
We therefore provide optional
mesh refinement using thin plate spline (TPS) interpolation.
For a clean screenshot point x, the refined mapping to phys-
ical observation k is
Tk(x) = π(Hk˜x) + fk(x),
(5)
where Hk is the screen homography and fk is a TPS dis-
placement field defined in clean screenshot coordinates.
The grid is initialized from the homography projection.
Annotators adjust selected grid points to specify local resid-
ual displacements. Only adjusted points contribute addi-
tional TPS constraints, while the four screen corners have
zero residual displacement. The tool supports adjustable
grid densities, with six rows and nine columns being the
most common configuration. The median number of ad-
justed control points is eight.
For mesh-refined images, six points are sampled along
each edge of a clean target box, including the starting vertex
but excluding the ending vertex. The resulting 24 boundary
points are transformed to construct the physical target poly-
gon. Without mesh refinement, the target region is trans-
ferred directly using homography.

9. Evaluation Details
9.1. Model Configurations and Evaluation Protocol
Open-source models.
We evaluate ten open-source mod-
els locally using vLLM with bfloat16 precision and greedy
decoding. We use a temperature of 0, a maximum genera-
tion length of 512 tokens, and a random seed of 42. Infer-
ence runs on NVIDIA RTX A6000, L40S, and A100 GPUs
with tensor parallelism of 1 or 2 according to model size.
Each request contains one image and its grounding instruc-
tion. For each model, the checkpoint, prompt template, and
decoding settings remain fixed across observation condi-
tions. Each model uses its own visual processor with the
image budget and inference mode listed in Table 5.
Closed-source models.
We evaluate Claude Sonnet 5.5,
GPT-6 Luna, and Jev 1.14 through their respective APIs.
Claude Sonnet 5.5 uses adaptive thinking with low effort
and a maximum output length of 8,192 tokens. GPT-6 Luna
uses low reasoning effort and a maximum output length of
16,000 tokens. Both use the same grounding prompt, with
images submitted according to their respective API inter-
faces. Jev 1.14 uses a structured grounding interface that
returns normalized bounding boxes. All three models are
evaluated on the same fixed subset described in Sec. 9.3.
Prediction parsing and scoring.
We convert model out-
puts into two-dimensional points in the original coordinate
system of each observation. Open-source models, Claude
Sonnet 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna use a coordinate scale of 0
to 1000. Jev 1.14 returns bounding boxes on a scale of 0
to 1. For models predicting bounding boxes, we use the
box center as the grounding point. Normalized coordinates
are mapped using the original image dimensions. A pre-
diction is correct when its point lies inside the correspond-
ing ground-truth polygon. Unparseable outputs or invalid
final predictions are counted as incorrect. Accuracy is the
proportion of correct predictions among the evaluation in-
stances.
Intermediate observation construction.
For each task,
Resized uniformly scales the clean screenshot so that its
target area matches the corresponding physical target area.
The scaled screenshot is placed on a gray background with
the target center aligned to its position in the physical ob-
servation. Warped projects the clean screenshot onto the
screen region of the physical image using a homography
from human-verified screen corners. The surrounding back-
ground is retained from the physical observation. For both
conditions, target regions are transformed consistently with
the image construction.
Rectified observations and scoring.
We construct Rec-
tified observations by applying a perspective transforma-
tion to each physical image using either human-verified
screen corners (Oracle) or automatically predicted corners
(Auto). Both settings use the same rectification procedure
and grounding inference configuration, differing only in the
source of screen corners. The rectified screen is mapped to
a front-facing canvas matching the clean screenshot dimen-
sions.
Predicted screen corners may contain localization errors,
so target positions in the rectified image do not necessarily
align exactly with their original bounding boxes in the clean
screenshot. Furthermore, some physical target regions in-
clude local mesh refinement that is not captured by a single
homography. We therefore do not score predictions directly
against clean target boxes in the rectified image. Instead,
we map each predicted point back to the original physical
image using the inverse of the transformation applied during
rectification. The mapped point is then evaluated against the
final human-verified physical ground-truth polygon. This
procedure avoids treating geometric misalignment with the
clean target box as a grounding error while preserving the
effect of rectification quality on model predictions.
17


9.2. ReGround Implementation
ReGround directly reuses the Single-pass prediction as its
initial result. Given a valid initial point, we extract a crop
centered on the prediction, with its width and height set to
half of the original image dimensions, rounded down to in-
tegers. If the crop extends beyond the image boundary, we
shift the window back inside the image while preserving
its size, without padding. For open-source models, Claude
Sonnet 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna, the crop is resized to the orig-
inal image dimensions. For Jev 1.14, the crop is resized to
the image dimensions used in the initial API request. We
then perform a second grounding inference using the same
model, instruction, prompt template, and inference config-
uration.
Let (x0, y0) denote the top-left corner of the crop, and let
(wc, hc) denote its width and height. We convert the second
prediction to normalized crop coordinates (u, v) ∈[0, 1]2,
taking the center of the predicted box for box-output mod-
els. The final prediction in the original image coordinates
is
ˆp = (x0 + uwc, y0 + vhc).
(6)
If the second prediction is invalid, we retain the initial
prediction. If the initial prediction is invalid, we skip the
second inference and count the instance as incorrect. Every
valid initial prediction triggers one refinement inference.
Each instance requires at most two model inferences in to-
tal, with the first prediction reused from Single-pass eval-
uation. Neither ground-truth target annotations nor screen
corner annotations are used during cropping or refinement.
9.3. Evaluation Subset and Matched Comparison
Subset construction.
To control the inference cost of
closed-source models, we construct a fixed evaluation sub-
set of 500 grounding tasks from the 3,250 clean tasks in
GlassGround. We stratify the tasks along three dimensions:
operating system (Android or iOS), instruction type (text,
visual, functional, or positional), and tertiles of clean target
bounding-box area. This produces 24 strata, from which
we sample tasks approximately in proportion to their fre-
quencies in the full benchmark. We generate candidate sub-
sets using 50 random seeds from 0 to 49 and select seed
39, which minimizes the mean absolute accuracy difference
between the full benchmark and the corresponding subset
across the ten open-source models.
Subset size and evaluation coverage.
The selected sub-
set contains 500 grounding tasks from 332 distinct clean
GUI states. Each task is paired with all 16 combinations of
four wearable devices and four lighting conditions, yield-
ing 8,000 physical grounding instances across 5,312 distinct
physical images. All three closed-source models are eval-
uated on this fixed subset under Clean, Resized, Warped,
Physical, Oracle, and Auto conditions, as well as ReGround
on Resized, Warped, and Physical observations. Clean con-
tains 500 evaluation instances, while each of the other con-
ditions contains 8,000 instances.
Matched-subset comparison.
The open-source baseline
results in the main paper are evaluated on the full bench-
mark. For direct comparison with closed-source models,
we extract the corresponding predictions from the full-
benchmark open-source evaluations and recompute accu-
racy on the same 500-task subset. We also evaluate the two
PairDistill-adapted models on this subset and report their
mean accuracy and standard deviation across three training
seeds. Table 6 presents the resulting matched comparisons
across all evaluation conditions.
9.4. Held-Out Automatic Rectification
Held-out evaluation protocol.
As described in Sec. 8.3,
the screen corner detector was trained using a subset of
GlassGround physical images. To assess its performance
on unseen observations, we construct a held-out evalua-
tion subset that does not overlap with the detector’s training
data. This subset contains 315 distinct clean GUI states and
1,883 grounding tasks, yielding 30,128 physical grounding
instances. Neither the physical images nor their correspond-
ing clean GUI states were used to train the corner detector.
We evaluate Physical, Oracle, and Auto on identical held-
out instances using the same model configurations and the
scoring protocol described in Sec. 9.1.
Downstream grounding results.

11. PairDistill Details
11.1. Paired Training Data Collection
Data source and capture.
We construct PairDistill train-
ing data from AMEX [3], which provides element function-
ality descriptions for approximately 21K of its more than
104K screenshots. We use these descriptions as ground-
ing instructions and the corresponding element bounding
boxes as target regions. Due to capture costs, we select
approximately the first quarter of these annotated screen-
shots in filename order. We display 5,098 distinct screen-
shots on a smartphone and record them with wearable de-
21


Figure 11. Training data capture setup (Aria Gen 1). The smart-
phone and the wearable device are mounted on fixed holders. Left:
an AMEX screenshot displayed in full screen. Right: a numbered
green separator frame displayed between consecutive screenshots,
used to segment the recorded video.
vices, both mounted on fixed stands. Their relative posi-
tions remain fixed within each capture session. The screen-
shots are shown sequentially in full-screen mode, with num-
bered green separator frames inserted between consecutive
screenshots. Figure 11 illustrates the capture setup and the
separator frames used for video segmentation. The wear-
able device continuously records video, which we segment
using these separator frames to identify the correspond-
ing screenshot intervals and extract physical images. Each
screenshot is captured only once under one of the four de-
vice and lighting combinations. After human verification
of screen corners, we transfer clean target boxes to the
physical images using homography. We exclude captures
requiring non-homographic alignment and invalid capture
records, yielding 4,880 valid pairs of clean screenshots and
physical images.
Training and validation split.
We randomly reserve ap-
proximately 5% of the valid image pairs for validation at the
clean screenshot level using seed 42, ensuring that identical
screenshots do not appear in both training and validation.
This produces 4,636 training pairs and an initial validation
set of 244 pairs. To reduce near-duplicate interfaces in val-
idation, we resize clean screenshots to 32 × 32 grayscale
thumbnails and compute normalized cross-correlation be-
tween each validation screenshot and all training screen-
shots. We exclude validation samples whose maximum cor-
relation with any training screenshot is at least 0.85. The
resulting validation set contains 83 image pairs and 1,266
grounding targets and is used for checkpoint selection. The
training set contains 4,636 image pairs and 76,224 ground-
ing targets. Of these, 1,960 pairs with 33,755 targets were
captured using Aria Gen 1 under Indoor-Lamp, and 953
pairs with 14,782 targets under Indoor-Dark. The remain-
ing pairs were captured using Quest 3, comprising 945 pairs
with 15,310 targets under Indoor-Dark and 778 pairs with
12,377 targets under Outdoor-Sun.
11.2. Training Implementation
LoRA configuration.
We adapt UI-Venus-2-9B and
MAI-UI-8B using LoRA with rank 16, scaling parameter
32, and dropout 0.05. For MAI-UI-8B, LoRA is applied
to the attention and MLP projections of all 36 language
model layers. For UI-Venus-2-9B, LoRA covers the atten-
tion projections of eight standard attention layers and 24
linear attention layers, together with the MLP projections
of all 32 layers. For both backbones, LoRA also covers
the attention and feed-forward projections of the 27 vision
encoder blocks, the patch embedding projection, and the
patch merger. MAI-UI-8B additionally adapts three Deep-
Stack mergers. This yields 52.5 million trainable parame-
ters across 369 modules for MAI-UI-8B and 51.3 million
trainable parameters across 359 modules for UI-Venus-2-
9B.
Optimization.
We use AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 =
0.999, and weight decay 0.01. The learning rate is 10−4
with cosine decay and linear warmup over the first 3% of
optimization steps. Each training sample contains one phys-
ical image and one of its grounding targets. Targets are
grouped into four tiers by width relative to the image width.
In each epoch, the images are shuffled and each image con-
tributes one target, drawn at random with a fixed seed from
the smallest tier present in that image. We use a microbatch
size of one and gradient accumulation over four steps, giv-
ing an effective batch size of four. Training runs for three
epochs, with 1,159 optimizer updates per epoch and 3,477
updates in total. The frozen base weights use bfloat16 pre-
cision, while LoRA parameters are maintained in float32
without automatic mixed-precision casting. We enable gra-
dient checkpointing and clip gradient norms at 1.0.
Scale augmentation.
Scale augmentation is applied inde-
pendently to each physical training image, while the cor-
responding clean screenshot remains unchanged. For each
sample, we leave the image unchanged with probability 0.2,
enlarge it with probability 0.4, or shrink it with probabil-
ity 0.4.
For enlargement, we sample a scale factor log-
uniformly from [1, min(smax, 3)], where smax is the largest
factor that permits a crop containing both the screen and tar-
get regions. We randomly position the crop and resize it to
the original image dimensions. For shrinking, we sample
a scale factor log-uniformly from [1/3, 1], resize the entire
image, and place it at a random position on a canvas of the
original dimensions. The canvas background is filled with
either a random gray value or the mean color of the origi-
nal image. We apply the same scaling and translation to the
physical target quadrilateral, coordinate labels, and screen
corners. Student-side feature pooling uses the transformed
target region, while teacher-side pooling uses the original
target region in the clean screenshot.
Feature alignment.
We extract 4,096-dimensional visual
22


features from the patch merger outputs of the frozen teacher
and adapted student.
For MAI-UI-8B, the features are
taken from the main patch merger rather than the Deep-
Stack mergers. Following Sec. 5.2, we subtract the mean
visual token feature of each image before pooling token
features according to their overlap with the target region.
The teacher is the frozen original backbone without LoRA.
Teacher target features are precomputed from clean screen-
shots and cached for training. The visual input pixel bud-
gets are 4,194,304 for MAI-UI-8B and 4,800,000 for UI-
Venus-2-9B. Student features are computed from physical
observations during training.
We use the alignment loss
1 −cos(zp, zc) with weight λ = 1.
Checkpoint selection and weight interpolation.
We
evaluate checkpoints every 400 optimizer updates using the
83 AMEX validation image pairs and all 1,266 correspond-
ing grounding targets described in Sec. 11.1. Validation
accuracy is measured by checking whether each predicted
point falls inside the projected target quadrilateral in the
physical image. For MAI-UI-8B, we use the directly pre-
dicted point, while for UI-Venus-2-9B, we use the center
of the predicted bounding box. Unparseable predictions are
counted as incorrect, with a fixed denominator of 1,266. We
use greedy decoding with maximum generation lengths of
48 tokens for MAI-UI-8B and 32 tokens for UI-Venus-2-
9B. For each training seed, we select the checkpoint with
the highest validation accuracy. Following WiSE-FT [38],
we use a weight interpolation coefficient of α = 0.5 for the
final adapted models. In our LoRA implementation, this is
achieved by reducing the LoRA scaling parameter from 32
during training to 16 at inference.

13. Limitations, Ethics, and Dataset Documen-
tation
Limitations.
GlassGround
focuses
on
single-step
grounding in static GUI states and does not cover se-
quential interaction, action feedback, or complete task
execution.
The benchmark is limited to smartphone
screens.
By holding GUI content fixed while varying
wearable devices and lighting conditions, our controlled
capture protocol enables paired comparisons of grounding
performance across physical observations.
However, it
does not cover dynamic interfaces, other types of physical
displays, or the broader range of conditions encountered in
everyday use.
Ethical
considerations.
This
study
evaluates
GUI
grounding models rather than human behavior or perfor-
mance. Physical observations were captured by members
of the research team following a predefined protocol,
without recruiting external participants as research sub-
jects. We do not analyze individual behavior, physiological
characteristics, or other personal attributes. We use only
RGB images from the wearable devices to construct and
evaluate the benchmark, without using audio, eye-tracking,
or other sensor data. For these reasons, we did not seek
institutional review board (IRB) review.
Privacy protection.
The GUI screenshots in Glass-
Ground were obtained partly from test accounts and partly
from personal accounts belonging to members of the re-
search team. Before public release, we redacted non-public
personal information, such as names, profile pictures, phone
numbers, email addresses, and residential addresses of team
members and their contacts. The corresponding physical
images and grounding instructions were de-identified con-
sistently. Manual inspection confirmed that no bystanders
appeared in indoor captures.
For outdoor captures, we
blurred or redacted identifiable faces, license plates, and
other information that could reveal personal identities. As
a result, the public release contains localized differences in
some images and task instructions compared with the ver-
sion used for the evaluations reported in this paper.
Dataset documentation and intended use.
We release
GlassGround in our repository,
including clean GUI
screenshots, corresponding wearable camera observations,
grounding instructions, target region annotations, and
human-verified screen corner annotations. The accompa-
nying documentation will describe the devices, lighting
conditions, image correspondences, and evaluation proto-
cols needed to support reproducibility. GlassGround is in-
tended for research on GUI grounding, visual robustness,
and wearable vision understanding. It is not intended for
personal identification or the analysis of individual behav-
ior.

Pipeline.
The headset handles the interface, camera cap-
ture, and phone tracking. Speech transcription and ground-
ing run on a remote GPU server, and Claude models are
called through their API. The spoken request is transcribed
with Whisper large-v3-turbo. Claude Sonnet 5.5 then de-
composes it into steps and writes a grounding instruction
and a completion condition for each step. For each step, the
grounding model (UI-Venus-2-9B + PairDistill) takes the
grounding instruction and predicts the target once on the
original headset camera frame, as in Single-pass inference.
The screen corner detector from Sec. 3.4 separately locates
the phone screen in the same frame, and the resulting ho-
mography converts the prediction to screen coordinates. A
headset-side version of the same detector tracks the phone
in real time. The overlay is continuously re-projected onto
the live passthrough view according to the tracking result,
so it follows the phone as it moves. A side panel shows
the rectified screen from the captured frame together with
the prediction. While a step is active, the system repeat-
edly captures a frame and rectifies the screen, and Claude
Haiku 5.5 checks it against the completion condition. Once
the condition is met, the system advances to the next step
automatically.
Latency.
In this setting, the user waits for the overlay
before acting at each step, so per-step latency directly af-
fects how smoothly the guidance proceeds.
This is also
why Sec. 4.1 considers inference efficiency when selecting
closed-source baselines. The prototype calls the ground-
ing model once per step. Across the six steps in the video,
grounding takes 1.17 to 1.18 s on the server. The round trip
from sending the grounding request to receiving the result
takes 1.56 to 1.84 s. It includes upload, server processing,
and download, and excludes the preceding frame capture
and the subsequent rendering.
Live operation and recording.
Grounding, overlay ren-
dering, and step verification in the video all run live while
the user operates the phone.
They are not produced by
offline processing of a recorded video. The video frames
are the headset’s screen recording from a single continuous
take, with no cuts between the three tasks. The overlays
and panels are what the prototype rendered live in the head-
set and what the user saw, although the recorded field of
view is slightly narrower than the view when wearing the
headset. The user completed each step by following these
cues. Apart from trimming the clip, post-production only
affected the audio and subtitles. In this recording, the head-
set’s screen capture contains no sound while the application
is using the microphone. The speech in the video therefore
comes from the same recording that the application saved
for transcription, voice-altered for privacy and placed back
at its original timing. The subtitles show the application’s
transcription and were overlaid afterwards. Ambient sound
was removed. The video illustrates the application setting
and is not an evaluation result.
