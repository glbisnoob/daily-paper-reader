Abstract —GUI grounding maps a screenshot and a natural language instruction to the coordinates of the intended interface element. Existing approaches improve grounding through visual scaling , instruction scaling , and sampling scaling , yet it remains unclear which grounding bottlenecks these strategies address and where their gains saturate. To address this gap, we introduce ScaleDiag, a controlled diagnostic framework that isolates the effects of visual input, instruction information, and prediction sampling on GUI grounding by independently varying target scale and surrounding context, target-specific information and auxiliary descriptions, and candidate coverage and selection, respectively. We evaluate ScaleDiag across five benchmarks and up to eight vision-language models and find that each scaling strategy operates within a much narrower regime than commonly assumed. (1) Visual scaling is governed by target occupancy rather than a fixed crop ratio, with full-screen errors typically rescued once the target occupies only 0.32% of the cropped view, while further zooming eventually becomes harmful. (2) Instruction scaling is driven by information that identifies the target rather than description richness itself, as even ground-truth-derived attributes such as color, size, and shape are often neutral or harmful, while appearance and spatial descriptions account for over 90% of the achievable gain. (3) Repeated sampling exposes a different limitation, as correct coordinates are frequently generated but remain difficult to select, leaving 5.1–11.0 points of oracle headroom while geometric consensus provides almost no improvement. Together, these results show that test-time scaling is constrained by distinct operating regimes rather than benefiting monotonically from more visual focus, richer instructions, or additional samples. Our code is available at https://github.com/YenanLiu/ScaleDiag.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

GUI grounding maps a screenshot and a natural language instruction to the coordinates of the intended interface element, enabling GUI agents to translate high-level goals into concrete actions [1]–[3]. Recent vision-language models have achieved strong performance on standard grounding benchmarks [4]– [7], yet their predictions remain sensitive to small targets, dense interfaces, irrelevant visual content, and ambiguous instructions. Existing work improves grounding through local cropping [8]–[12], richer target descriptions [2], [5], [13], [14], and repeated sampling [4], [15], [16]. To address this gap, we introduce ScaleDiag , a controlled diagnostic framework that isolates the effects of visual in-put, instruction information, and prediction sampling on GUI grounding by independently varying target scale and sur-rounding context, target-specific and auxiliary descriptions, and candidate coverage and selection, respectively. The model size is treated as a cross-cutting factor and examine whether these effects persist across models of different scales and specialization. We evaluate ScaleDiag across five GUI ground-ing benchmarks and up to eight vision-language models spanning different scales and levels of GUI specialization. For visual scaling, we vary target-centered crop size and irrelevant context. For instruction scaling, we vary how much information the instruction provides to identify the target and what additional target descriptions are included. For sampling scaling, we test whether repeated sampling generates a correct coordinate and whether that coordinate can be reliably selected from the sampled candidates. Our experiments reveal where each form of test-time scaling helps and where its benefit breaks down. First , visual scaling is governed by target occupancy rather than by a universal crop ratio. Full-screen errors rescued by cropping require a median target occupancy of only 0.32%, while further enlargement eventually becomes harmful as useful context is removed. Appropriate cropping rescues 24.1% of full-screen errors while harming only 1.3% of correct predictions. Second , instruction scaling helps when added information makes the target easier to identify, rather than simply making the description richer. arXiv:2611.25297v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 Removing target-identifying information reduces accuracy to 4%–6%, while even ground-truth-derived attributes such as color, size, and shape provide little consistent benefit. Ap-pearance and spatial descriptions account for over 90% of the achievable gain, with function and goal descriptions con-tributing little further improvement. Third , repeated sampling improves the chance of generating a correct coordinate but not of selecting it. Oracle@ 8 improves accuracy by 5.1–11.0 points, while coordinate-medoid selection provides almost no gain. Correct candidates often appear only once or twice among eight samples, making them difficult to recover through geometric consensus. Together, these results show that visual focus, richer descriptions, and additional samples have distinct limits rather than providing monotonic gains. In summary, our contributions are threefold:  

> •

A controlled diagnostic framework. ScaleDiag inde-pendently varies visual input, instruction information, and prediction sampling to separate effects that are coupled in existing scaling strategies.  

> •

A quantitative characterization of scaling limits. We identify the target-occupancy range in which cropping helps, which instruction information contributes mean-ingful gains, and why additional sampled candidates fail to translate into better final predictions.  

> •

Evidence for selective test-time scaling. Our results show that visual focus, richer descriptions, and additional samples are useful under different conditions, motivating test-time computation that is allocated according to the source of grounding error rather than applied uniformly. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Instruction Semantic Enhancement 

Successful GUI grounding requires instructions that provide enough information to identify the intended interface element [1]–[3]. Early methods such as SeeClick [1], UGround [2], and OS-Atlas [3] learn mappings from language to GUI elements using visible text, control attributes, spatial relations, and template-based referring expressions. These formulations work well when the target can be identified from explicit interface cues, but provide limited support for instructions expressed through appearance, function, or user intent. More recent methods enrich the instruction with additional target descriptions [5], [13], [14]. Aria-UI [14], UI-E2I-Synth [13], and UI-Ins [5] incorporate cues such as appearance, location, function, and interaction intent, while UI-Ins further reformu-lates high-level intent into a target description before coor-dinate prediction. Other approaches, including MEGA-GUI [17], ZoomUI [18], and AdaZoom-GUI [19], combine richer instructions with visual search to narrow the target region. Despite these advances, it remains unclear which additional descriptions actually help identify the target and which merely add target-related information without improving grounding. Existing work also does not systematically test how these effects change with model scale or whether larger models can compensate when target-identifying information is missing. 

B. Visual Perception and Representation Enhancement 

Accurate GUI grounding requires sufficient visual detail to distinguish the target while preserving the surrounding context needed for localization [6], [7], [20]–[22]. Early methods improve small-target grounding through higher-resolution or multi-view encoding. CogAgent [20] combines global and local visual representations, while Ferret-UI [6], [7] adapts image partitioning to GUI layouts to better preserve small icons and text. Later work reduces visual redundancy or focuses computation on task-relevant regions. ShowUI [21] and FocusUI [23] selectively retain informative visual tokens or patches, while TAG [24], GUI-AIMA [25], GUI-Actor [26], and InnerZoom [27] strengthen attention or computa-tion around likely target regions. However, visual focusing typically changes target scale and surrounding context at the same time. As a result, it remains unclear whether gains come from enlarging the target or removing context, how much enlargement is actually needed, and when further cropping becomes harmful. 

C. Test-Time Search and Verification for GUI Grounding 

When a single grounding prediction is unreliable, additional test-time computation can be used to search for, refine, or verify the target location. A common strategy is coarse-to-fine visual search. RegionFocus [8], DiMo-GUI [10], and GUI-Eyes [28] first localize a coarse region and then refine it through cropping, zooming, or local views. More adaptive methods decide when and where to spend this extra computa-tion. AdaZoom-GUI [19] selectively activates refinement for small targets, while UI-Zoomer [29] uses prediction consis-tency and spatial dispersion to guide zooming. DRS-GUI [16] and ZoomUI [30] further formulate grounding as an iterative search process that updates the candidate region based on previous predictions or target cues. Additional computation can also be used without explicit visual search. MVP [31] aggregates predictions from perturbed views to identify stable locations, while Re-Prefill [32] performs a second inference pass using selected visual and instruction features. These methods show that repeated inference can expose alternative predictions or reconsider an initial decision. However, prior work rarely distinguishes whether the gain comes from gener-ating a correct candidate or from selecting it once generated, making it unclear how much repeated computation remains useful when candidate selection itself is the limiting factor. B. Controlled Visual Interventions 

The visual experiments keep the instruction and target fixed while varying target scale and surrounding context. We use target-centered cropping to change target scale while progres-sively reducing surrounding context, and tiled perturbations to add irrelevant content while preserving the original target. 

Target-Centered Cropping. To measure the effect of visual scaling without confounding it with crop-selection accuracy, we construct oracle crops using the ground-truth target lo-cation. Given a screenshot of size W × H and the center 

c = ( cx, c y ) of the ground-truth bounding box, a crop with area ratio f has width Wf = √f W and height Hf = √f H .The crop is centered at c and shifted inward when necessary to remain within the screenshot while preserving its size. We evaluate f ∈ { 1, 0.5, 0.25 , 0.125 , 0.0625 , 0.03125 }, cor-responding to 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, and 3.125% of the original screenshot area. This controlled experiment removes crop-selection errors and measures how grounding changes as the target occupies a larger fraction of the input while surrounding context is progressively reduced. 

Irrelevant Context Addition. Target-centered cropping simul-taneously enlarges the target relative to the input and removes surrounding content. To isolate the effect of surrounding con-text, we construct tiled screenshots that keep the target screen-shot unchanged while adding unrelated interfaces around it. On ScreenSpot-v2 [33], each grid cell has the original screenshot size W × H, and we construct canvases with K ∈ 1, 2, 4 cells arranged as 1 × 1, 1 × 2, and 2 × 2 grids. The target screenshot is placed without resizing in a randomly selected cell, while the remaining cells contain screenshots sampled from the same dataset and resized to W × H. We use a fixed random seed, seed = 0 , so that all models receive the same target placement and surrounding content. Let (ox, o y ) denote the top-left offset of the target cell. Because the target screenshot is not resized, its bounding box B = (x1, y 1, x 2, y 2) is translated to B′ = (x1 + ox, y 1 + oy , x 2 + ox, y 2 + oy ).The instruction remains unchanged. Thus, the target itself and its scale are preserved while only the amount of irrelevant surrounding content increases, allowing us to measure its effect on grounding independently.

app describes visible text, iconography, color, and coarse location. The spatial view spa describes the target relative to neighboring interface elements. The functional view fun 

describes the effect of activating the target without relying on its appearance or location. The goal view goa describes the higher-level user intent associated with the target. The model is instructed to follow the images when the heuristic hints conflict with the visual evidence. These marked images are used only to generate descriptions. We generate four single-view descriptions, six pairwise combinations, four three-view combinations, and one four-view combination. Together with the original instruction, this yields 16 conditions per sample. Each condition integrates its selected perspectives into a single coherent instruction. Generated descriptions must remain faithful to the target, remain usable without the marked images, and avoid refer-ences to annotations, coordinates, percentages, or ground truth. Comparing individual views and their combinations measures which additional target descriptions improve grounding and whether their gains saturate as more descriptions are added.

B. Visual Scaling Is Governed by Target Occupancy 

Table I shows that visual difficulty is concentrated in SS-Pro and UI-Vision, where icon targets are substantially harder than text targets. For several models, the text–icon gap reaches 39–50 points, while the gap is much smaller on MMBench-GUI and ScreenSpot-v2. Within the Qwen3.5 family, this gap narrows from 4B to 27B but remains substantial, indicating that model scaling alone does not eliminate errors on small targets. Figure 3 (a) shows that target-centered cropping has a nonmonotonic effect. Accuracy generally improves as the crop becomes smaller, peaks when 6.25%–25% of the orig-inal screenshot area is retained, and then declines as more surrounding context is removed. Figure 3 (b) further shows that the largest gains occur on SS-Pro and UI-Vision, which contain more small targets, whereas ScreenSpot-v2 benefits little. The best crop ratio also varies across benchmarks and models, showing that visual scaling cannot be determined by a single crop ratio. Figures 4 (a) and (b) reveal a more consistent relationship with target occupancy. Targets with low full-screen OCC benefit most from cropping, whereas targets with full-screen OCC above 2% are generally harmed. After cropping, gains re-main broadly positive below approximately 2% OCC, weaken between 2% and 5%, and become negative beyond roughly 5%. Among full-screen errors that are rescued by cropping, the median OCC at the first successful crop is only 0.32%. These results show that visual scaling requires only modest target enlargement and quickly saturates once the target occupies a sufficient fraction of the input. Figure 4 (c) separately exam-ines surrounding context. Adding irrelevant interface content while preserving the target screenshot and its scale reduces accuracy for most models. However, performance on small targets does not consistently predict robustness to irrelevant context, and neither effect scales monotonically with model size. Target scale and surrounding context therefore affect grounding differently and should not be conflated. 

Finding 1. Visual scaling is governed by target occupancy rather than by a fixed crop ratio. Cropping primarily helps targets with low occupancy, requires only modest enlarge-ment to recover many errors, and becomes harmful once further cropping removes useful surrounding context. 

C. Instruction Scaling Depends on Target Identification 

Figure 5 (a) shows that adding coarse target attributes provides little consistent benefit. Position yields only a small average improvement, while color, size, and shape are neutral or harmful for several models. Even target descriptions derived directly from ground truth therefore do not necessarily improve grounding. Figure 5 (b) a much stronger dependence on target-identifying information. Removing the information needed to identify the referred element reduces all evaluated models to approximately 4%–6% accuracy and nearly eliminates the performance gap between Qwen3.5 models of different sizes. Restoring the original instruction recovers most of the lost performance, while additional location information provides smaller gains. Increasing model size therefore improves the use of available instruction information but does not compen-sate when target-identifying information is missing. Figure 5 (c) and Table II further show that different addi-tional target descriptions contribute very differently. Appear-ance descriptions improve average accuracy from 57.6% to 65.4%, spatial descriptions reach 62.6%, and combining the two reaches 69.1%. In contrast, function descriptions provide little improvement and goal descriptions reduce average accu-racy. Combining all four description types reaches 69.9%, only 0.8 points above appearance and spatial descriptions alone. Thus, most of the gain comes from additional descriptions that make the target easier to distinguish by its appearance and location, rather than from adding more target-related information in general. D. Sampling Improves Coverage but Not Selection 

Figure 6 (a) suggests that repeated sampling improves candidate coverage but not candidate selection. Compared with greedy decoding, Oracle@ 8 improves average grounding accuracy by 5.1–11.0 points across the four evaluated mod-els, showing that additional samples often contain a correct coordinate when the greedy prediction is wrong. In contrast, coordinate-medoid selection changes accuracy by only −1.4

to +0 .3 points, leaving most of this gain unrealized. Figure 6 (b) shows where this gap arises. Across the pooled model– item pairs, 23.1% contain both correct and incorrect samples, meaning that a correct candidate is generated but is not consistently selected. The prevalence of these cases varies substantially across models, from 32.6% for Qwen3.5-4B to 9.2% for MAI-UI-8B, indicating that the potential benefit of additional sampling is strongly model-dependent. Figure 6 (c) further explains why geometric selection cap-tures little of this potential gain. Among items rescued by Oracle@ 8, correct coordinates often appear only once or twice among eight samples and therefore remain rare among the generated candidates. A medoid favors the dominant spatial cluster, making these rare correct candidates unlikely to be selected. As correct candidates become more frequent, greedy decoding is also increasingly likely to be correct, leaving less additional gain for repeated sampling. 

Finding 3. Prediction sampling improves candidate gen-eration but not candidate selection. Additional samples frequently contain correct coordinates missed by greedy decoding, but these coordinates are often rare among the sampled candidates and are therefore not recovered by geometric selection. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we propose a systematic framework for studying test-time scaling in GUI grounding across visual input, instruction information, and output sampling. Our re-sults show that scaling is effective only when it addresses the active bottleneck. Visual scaling improves target visibility, instruction scaling resolves target ambiguity, and repeated sampling expands candidate coverage but still requires reliable selection.
