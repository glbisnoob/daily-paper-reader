# CLASHarness: Benchmarking Multi-Agent Conflict in Computer Use via a Controllable and Verifiable Generation Pipeline 

Yuxiang Lai 1,2 , Jike Zhong 3, Chengzhi Liu 1,6 , Ming Li 4, Haonan Wang 1,7 , Xiaofeng Yang 2,5 , Liang Zhao 2,Youbao Tang 1 and Ruixi Fan 1      

> 1Google, 2Emory University, 3University of Southern California, 4University of Florida, 5University of Chicago, 6University of California, Santa Barbara, 7Columbia University

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems to automate complex computer-use workflows, prompting numerous benchmarks to evaluate and advance agent capabilities. However, existing benchmarks mostly assume consistent, cooperative environments. In contrast, real-world collaboration rarely runs on consistent information: peer or sub-agents frequently report outdated states, issue contradictory instructions, or compete over shared permissions and files, forcing an agent to check others’ outputs and verify ground truth in the operating environment. Evaluating such conflict resolution remains challenging: manual task authoring cannot scale across complex systems, while generating tasks via unconstrained LLM generation produces narrative drift, answer leakage, and unsatisfiable grading rubrics that leave tasks unsolvable. We introduce CLASHarness (Conflict-Loaded Agent Scenario Harness), a controllable framework for generating verifiable multi-agent conflicts in computer use, accompanied by a benchmark of 600 multi-round tasks. By combining hierarchical scenario planning with an eight-step verified micro-pipeline, CLASHarness pre-commits decisive visual facts before rendering and compiles hybrid code and semantic graders directly from executed reference trajectories, guaranteeing that every task is solvable by construction. Evaluating 15 frontier models on CLASHarness reveals three core bottlenecks. First, when outputs of sub-agents conflict with the environment, weaker models blindly follow misleading information, whereas stronger models will inspect visual evidence to verify ground truth. Second, resolving conflicts is much harder than finding them: while agents can spot inconsistencies, actively fixing the system state drops task success significantly. Third, coordination protocols remain a universal bottleneck: even frontier models fail over 58% of closing actions, frequently forgetting to release shared locks or hand off tasks to sub-agents. CLASHarness provides a scalable, verifiable foundation for diagnosing coordination failures and building reliable multi-agent systems.

1. Introduction 

As vision-language models (VLMs) transition to tool-augmented computer-use scenarios, multi-agent systems are increasingly deployed to automate complex collaborative workflows (Du et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023), prompting numerous benchmarks to evaluate and advance agent capabilities (Jimenez et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024b; Xu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2024). However, existing computer-use benchmarks mostly assume consistent, cooperative single-agent environments (Xie et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024), while multi-agent evaluations remain largely confined to pure-text dialogue (Cemri et al., 2026; Pappu et al., 2026). For example, when a sub-agent relies on a stale cache to report a deployment as successful and urges routing production traffic, while live telemetry shows failing health checks, an agent resolving the conflict cannot blindly trust the peer report. Instead, it must check others’ outputs, inspect visual dashboards to verify ground truth in the operating environment, and execute a configuration rollback. Evaluating such conflict resolution faces a fundamental challenge in benchmark construction: manual task curation is labor-intensive and cannot scale across complex operating environments (Deng et al., 2023; Mialon et al., 2024), while generating tasks via unconstrained LLM prompting produces uncontrollable scenarios suffering from narrative drift, answer leakage, ungrounded visual assets, and unsatisfiable grading rubrics that leave tasks unsolvable (Lù et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2026b; Zheng et al., 2023). 3. The Generation Harness 

3.1. Problem Formalization 

We formalize a multi-agent conflict task as a conflict round defined by the tuple R = (W 0, 𝐼, T , 𝑣 ★, Φ),where W0 denotes the initial computer-use workspace containing operating system state, file trees, asynchronous multi-agent communication transcripts, and rendered visual evidence. The instruction  

> 𝐼

specifies the operational objective and explicitly frames competing claims from peer agents without revealing which assertion is grounded in fact. T represents the typed action space; executing 𝑐 ∈ T 

transitions environment state and appends structured records to an audit effect log Elog . The decisive value 𝑣 ★ is the ground-truth fact resolving the contradiction. Finally, Φ = {𝜙 1, . . . , 𝜙 𝑚 } is a hybrid evaluation suite combining exact code checks and targeted semantic criteria evaluated over the final workspace state and Elog .This formulation frames conflict resolution as active investigation and decision-making rather than passive text classification. At inference time, the agent is provided with (W 0, 𝐼, T ) and alerted to the contradiction, but must autonomously investigate multimodal evidence across W, identify 𝑣 ★, and execute operational state changes (e.g., protocol handovers, configuration patches, rollbacks). Checks  

> Φ

are compiled directly from executed reference trajectories (§3.4), guaranteeing that every task is solvable. Rounds are grouped into 10-round packages sharing an evolving workspace, generated from a structured scenario seed (§4). 

3.2. Hierarchical Phased Generation Pipeline 

Synthesizing multi-round, multi-agent conflict scenarios poses a fundamental systems challenge. Under single-pass prompting, generator models must simultaneously author complex operational contexts, enforce conflicting agent identities, manipulate multi-file workspaces, and invent evaluation checkers. Single-pass generation frequently causes broken schemas, inconsistent storylines across rounds, leaked answers, and impossible grading checks. CLASHarness resolves these challenges through hierarchical phased outline planning , decomposing synthesis into an eight-stage pipeline across three coordinated tiers with in-loop self-correction (Figure 1).

of 𝑅 = 10 sequential rounds. Accumulating the complete workspace and dialogue history across all rounds into a single prompt quickly exhausts context limits and causes inconsistencies. CLASHarness resolves this with a two-level planning hierarchy: High-Level Phase Planning (Stage 4): The 𝑅

At the start of each phase, Stage 4 ( Phase Milestone Expansion ) outlines high-level milestones for the upcoming 3–4 rounds with a single model call using a rolling window of recent history, maintaining scenario coherence without overloading the prompt. 

Fine-Grained Round Synthesis (Stage 5): Within each phase, the harness synthesizes each round sequentially through an eight-step verified micro-pipeline in Stage 5 ( Progressive Round Synthesis ): authoring task instructions that frame competing claims while strictly isolating the factual resolution, synthesizing peer agent dialogue history, applying workspace file updates, injecting visual assets, pre-committing the decisive ground-truth value 𝑣 ★ under automated leakage scanning, drafting step-by-step execution plans, executing reference command-line trajectories in a live sandbox, and compiling exact code checks Φ directly from recorded state changes without model calls. Workspace modifications from round 𝑟 persist and carry over to round 𝑟 + 1. Decomposing round synthesis into isolated sub-steps confines potential failures to narrow execution scopes, enabling bounded in-loop retries without discarding valid package state. 

Tier 3: Post-Generation Compilation & Multimodal Exclusivity (Stages 6–8). To guarantee that compiled benchmarks are verifiably solvable and genuinely multimodal, Tier 3 performs comprehensive end-to-end verification. A cross-stage consistency audit verifies natural-language task instructions against reference trajectories and compiled grading checks, eliminating mismatches between task goals and grading criteria in Stage 6 ( Consistency Audit ). Next, Stage 7 ( Multimodal Refinement )synthesizes declarative HTML/Canvas specifications for visual evidence assets, adapting each asset to its platform’s custom UI layout while embedding the decisive value 𝑣 ★ alongside realistic operational data. Stage 8 ( Render & Exclusivity Gate ) then renders high-resolution browser screenshots in a headless environment. To prevent agents from bypassing visual inspection via text shortcuts, automated text-leakage scans verify that the decisive value 𝑣 ★ appears exclusively within rendered visual assets for visually grounded tasks (§3.4), compiling the final verified scenario .json .

Deterministic Normalization and Bounded Self-Correction. Generating multi-round scenarios often introduces minor formatting inconsistencies or logical conflicts. Discarding and regenerating an entire 10-round package upon encountering an error would be slow and expensive. Instead, CLASHarness resolves generation defects through a two-tier self-correction mechanism (Figure 1): First, deterministic code normalization corrects formatting, syntax, and schema errors—such as missing fields or invalid file paths—directly in code with zero model invocations (green tags in Figure 1). Second, bounded semantic retries resolve logical mismatches: when inconsistencies arise between task instructions and grading checks, the harness confines retries strictly to the affected sub-step, providing diagnostic error feedback (red tags in Figure 1) rather than restarting the package. Because retries are restricted to isolated sub-steps, total API calls scale linearly with round count: Total = 8 + ⌈ 𝑅 /𝑃 𝑠 ⌉ + 𝐾 · 𝑅 + 𝐴 , where 𝐾 ≈ 7–8 calls per round and 𝐴 is the visual asset count, averaging 95 calls per 10-round package (Appendix §B.2). This makes benchmark synthesis scalable and cost-effective while ensuring all generated tasks are solvable. 

3.3. Declarative Controllability and Modular Plugins Structured Seed Specifications. Scenario structure is defined via declarative seed schemas governing conflict types, interaction protocols, tool families, and evidence modalities, allowing users to customize parameters and synthesize new domains on demand (Appendix §B.4).

Modular Plugins and State Effects. To support modular extensions without modifying evaluation engines, CLASHarness separates domain mechanics into 23 modular plugins (16 conflict mechanics, 7 challenge plugins; Table C1). Each plugin P and tool command 𝑐 ∈ T declares operational effects 

E (P) and E ( 𝑐 ), such as file edits or permission changes. Valid actions and grading criteria match declared effects: Tvalid = {𝑐 ∈ T | E ( 𝑐 ) ∩ E (P) ≠ ∅} . Users can synthesize new conflicts or custom APIs by authoring a seed schema and registering state effects, without procedural simulation. 

3.4. Verifiable Grading and Multimodal Grounding Executable Reference Grader Compilation. In unconstrained generation, natural-language rubrics often specify impossible requirements. CLASHarness compiles checkers directly from executed reference trajectories: exact-match checks verify specific identifiers, while semantic criteria evaluate scoped file modifications (Appendix §B.7), eliminating unreachable checks across the benchmark. 

Multimodal Pre-Commitment & Exclusivity Gates. Mirroring cross-boundary ticket attachments, point-in-time incident snapshots, and physical sensor captures where underlying DOM or source access is unavailable, visual assets are provided as standalone image files. Committing 𝑣 ★ prior to rendering aligns visual evidence with compiled checkers. Across 600 rounds (§4), 346 require visual evidence ( role="required" ), with automated leakage scans ensuring 𝑣 ★ appears exclusively in rendered images rather than text. 

4. The CLASHarness Benchmark: Scale, Diversity, and Validity 

Benchmark Scale and Structural Diversity. Using CLASHarness, we construct a primary evaluation benchmark comprising 20 structured scenario seeds across diverse operational domains, 60 package variants (3 variants per seed), and 600 executable computer-use conflict rounds ( 𝑅 = 10). Across these 600 rounds, the benchmark spans 3,760 evaluation checks (3,284 required) and 7,970 rendered visual evidence assets. Reference trajectories average 10.28 steps against a communicated step budget  

> 𝑏

= 20 (Appendix §C.1). Task difficulty is empirically measured by reference trajectory length (7–18 steps; 𝑏 /steps ∈ [ 1.11 , 2.86 ]). Synthesizing the 20 seeds with an alternate model family yields an aligned parallel benchmark with low trajectory correlation (Pearson 𝑟 = 0.186, avg. 12.48 steps; §6), confirming that the generator shapes execution complexity while seeds govern task semantics. Round diversity is strictly enforced: candidate 𝑣 ★ values cannot reuse past answers, and only 1.6% of semantic criteria repeat within a package (2,086 of 2,137 criteria are globally distinct; Appendix §B.9). 

Hybrid Rubric Composition and Evaluator Stability. Of the 3,284 scored checks, 34.9% (1 ,147) are deterministic code checks and 65.1% (2 ,137) are targeted semantic criteria evaluated by an LLM judge (Table C2). Every semantic check is scored via 3-sample self-consistency majority voting, achieving 97.5% unanimous agreement across 19 ,419 recorded judgments. Re-grading all high-effort rounds using independent frontier judges (Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Opus 5) shows high consistency across judges: relative model rankings remain nearly identical ( 𝜌 ≥ 0.98, Cohen’s 𝜅 ≥ 0.90; Appendix Table F4). In addition, primary diagnostic findings (visual grounding F3, evidence anchoring F4, terminal actions F5, temporal deadlines F6) are evaluated strictly via deterministic code checks, ensuring conclusions hold independently of model judging. 

Cross-Metric Consistency and Capability Decoupling. Model rankings under code and semantic rubrics closely agree ( 𝜌 = +0.90). Importantly, individual check evaluations reveal that visual evidence localization and semantic conflict resolution have almost zero correlation ( 𝜌 = +0.02). This confirms that visual grounding and conflict resolution are independent capabilities requiring separate evaluation rather than composite scores.

5. Behavioral Analysis

5.2. Failure Taxonomy: Content Accuracy vs. Protocol Execution 

To diagnose why agents fail under conflict, we map all 3 ,284 evaluation checks per model into three distinct competencies spanning six diagnostic failure modes (Appendix §D.6): Visual Grounding ,inspecting visual conflict evidence (F3); Content Accuracy , covering messaging (F1), file modifications (F2), and evidence citations (F4); and Structural Protocol Compliance , executing terminal actions (F5) and synchronizing time windows (F6). 

Resolving Text-Visual Contradictions vs. Multi-Agent Synchronization. First, visual grounding vs. blind trust separates strong and weak models : when sub-agent outputs conflict with the environment, weaker models blindly follow misleading text, whereas stronger models inspect visual evidence to verify ground truth and cite exact conflict evidence (outperforming weaker baselines by 31–65 pp, with evidence citation F4 showing a 15.2 pp spread even among top models; Appendix §D.2).

5.3. Failure Concentration and Error Dynamics 

While models achieve similar overall pass rates, they fail in fundamentally different ways: do errors trigger a chain reaction, or do models fail separate checks independently? To answer this, we evaluate 

co-failure lift —the likelihood that a second check fails given that one has already failed, relative to independent chance (Table 2; Appendix §D.3). Comparing tasks by ground-truth step length reveals two distinct patterns (Table 1): on short tasks (9 or fewer steps), errors trigger a chain reaction, with 11 of 15 models exhibiting co-failure lift significantly above 1 .0, indicating that an early mistake causes the entire task to fail. In contrast, on long tasks (12 or more steps), lift drops near 1 .0 (0 .99–1 .06), showing failures on long tasks stem from gradual mistakes rather than single fatal errors.

6. Ablations 

Cross-Generator Stability and Task Difficulty. To test whether findings depend on the scenario generator, we synthesized an alternate suite using Claude Sonnet 5 alongside default Gemini 3.6 Flash (Figure 2c). Although Sonnet yields longer reference trajectories (12.5 vs. 10.3 steps) and lower pass rates, model rankings stay consistent ( 𝜌 = 0.96) without self-preference (Appendix §F.1). 

Action Budget Limits and Planning Horizons. Varying step budgets from 20 to 40 steps (Figure 2a) reveals three planning patterns: First, step-efficient models (Claude Opus 5, Sonnet 5) solve tasks concisely (mean 10.3 steps), rarely hitting the 20-step cap ( < 3.3%) with minimal gain from extra steps ( < 2 pp). Second, loop-prone models (Gemini 3.5 Flash) hit the cap on 89 .2% of rounds due to repetitive tool calls, inflating cost to $8.69 per solved task. Third, horizon-sensitive models benefit from extended exploration: with deeper exploration, GPT-6 Astra reaches 28 .7%, while Gemini 3.8 Flash jumps from 12 .8% (20 steps) to 29 .2% (40 steps) and 30 .7% at 60 steps ( 𝑝 = .0098), cutting cost per solved task by 21% to $3.50 (Appendix §F.3). 

Visual Necessity: Sighted vs. Blind Evaluation. Withholding visual evidence causes agent perfor-mance to collapse across all models (Figure 2b). Even on tasks where image inspection is not explicitly enforced by the rubric, blind agents retain only 11.1%–13.1% of sighted pass rates ( 𝑝 < 10 −11 ), confirming visual assets provide decisive rather than decorative evidence (Appendix §F.4). 

7. Limitations 

We note three scope limitations: (1) Semantic Judging: Semantic checks rely on an LLM judge without human studies, mitigated via 3-sample voting and multi-judge validation ( 𝜌 ≥ 0.98, 𝜅 ≥ 0.90; §4). (2) Asynchronous Traces: Conflict resolution is evaluated through logs and workspace traces

8. Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced CLASHarness, a controllable generation framework and benchmark for synthesizing verifiable multi-agent conflict scenarios in computer use. By combining hierarchical outline planning with verified micro-execution, pre-committed visual ground truths, and execution-compiled hybrid checkers, CLASHarness guarantees task solvability by construction across 600 multi-round tasks and 20 operational domains. Our evaluation of 15 frontier models uncovers three fundamental bottlenecks: weaker models blindly trust misleading peer reports whereas stronger models actively inspect visual evidence to verify ground truth; actively fixing the system state is much harder than spotting inconsistencies; and closing coordination protocols—such as releasing shared locks and completing sub-agent handoffs—remain a universal failure mode across all model families. Beyond static evaluation, CLASHarness’s modular plugin architecture and automated verification pipeline provide both a rigorous diagnostic testbed and a scalable data engine for post-training next-generation VLMs to build reliable multi-agent systems.

B.1. Problem Formalization and Grounded Locality Axioms 

Our benchmark evaluates how agents resolve conflicts through active investigation in operating environments, rather than through passive text classification. The evaluating agent receives competing claims from peer agents without knowing in advance which claim matches the true system state. Formally, a conflict round is defined by the tuple R = (W 0, 𝐼, T , 𝑣 ★, Φ) (§3.1), governed by three structural principles: 1. Grounded Locality: The decisive ground-truth entity 𝑣 ★ is strictly localized within the workspace 

W0. In 346 of the 600 tasks, 𝑣 ★ resides exclusively within a rendered visual asset without textual duplicates in the workspace (§B.7). 2. Action-Based Resolution: Task completion requires executing concrete action commands 𝑐 ∈ T ,such as configuration edits, access control updates, or deployment rollbacks recorded in an append-only effect log Elog , rather than simply outputting text answers. 3. Satisfiable Verification: Satisfaction predicates Φ = {𝜙 1, . . . , 𝜙 𝑚 } are compiled directly from executed reference trajectories (§3.4), guaranteeing that all tasks are solvable by construction. 

B.2. Hierarchical Phased Generation Pipeline 

Directly generating long-horizon, multi-agent scenarios with unconstrained single-pass prompting causes common failure modes: format collapse, narrative drift, premature answer leaks, and unsatis-fiable grading predicates. CLASHarness resolves this by decomposing scenario synthesis into phased macro-planning and verified micro-execution across eight coordinated stages in three tiers (Figure 1): • Tier 1: Package Initialization (Stages 1–3, 8 calls): Stage 1 ( Global Outline , 3 calls) establishes the scenario background, agent roles, timeline events, and resource allocations. Stage 2 ( Subagents & Interaction Rules , 2 calls) compiles role boundaries ( M8 ) and dependency matrices ( M5 , M7 ). Stage 3 ( Background Workspace Setup , 3 calls) initializes codebases, system logs, session transcripts, and platform UI environments ( M3 , M12 /13 ). • Tier 2: Round Generative Loop (Stages 4–5): For each round 𝑟 = 1 . . . 𝑅 , Stage 4 ( Phase Expansion ,1 call/phase) dynamically projects milestone timelines over rolling attention windows ( 𝑃 𝑠 ≈ 3–4 rounds) based on accumulated execution history, avoiding context saturation. Stage 5 ( Progressive Round Micro-Pipeline , 𝐾 ∈ [ 7, 8] sub-calls) decomposes round synthesis into eight isolated sub-steps: (5.1) task instructions with boundary enforcement ( M1 /M8 ), (5.2) peer agent messages and dialogue history ( M4 ), (5.3) environment file updates ( M3 /M9 ), (5.4) visual asset injection (M14 ), (5.5) decisive fact commitment ( 𝑣 ★) under automated leakage scanning, (5.6) step-by-step execution planning ( M2 ), (5.7) reference CLI trajectory execution in a sandbox ( M9 , M11 ), and (5.8) deterministic predicate compilation ( M10 , 0 calls). Narrow sub-calls isolate failure modes and enable bounded in-loop retries without discarding valid package state. • Tier 3: Post-Generation Compilation & Audit (Stages 6–8): Stage 6 ( Consistency Audit , ≤ 𝑀 = 3retry iterations) performs a secondary cross-stage check reconciling instructions with evaluation predicates ( M6 , M9 , M10 ). B.4. Declarative Controllability and 3-Tier Constraint Enforcement 

Scenario structure and difficulty are declared through extensible seed schemas governing conflict types, interaction protocols, tool families, and evidence modalities. The harness enforces constraints via three complementary tiers: (1) Structural Code Rejection & In-Loop Retry for hard invariants, such as minimum trajectory length, entity persistence, and prompt leakage prevention; (2) Deterministic Schema Coercion in code for mechanical format repairs without model calls; and (3) Prompt Conditioning for semantic role framing and scenario context.

C. Benchmark Composition, Coverage, and Validity 

C.1. Benchmark Coverage and Structural Diversity 

The CLASHarness benchmark spans 20 scenario seeds, 60 package variants, and 600 executable conflict rounds. Reference trajectories average 10.28 steps against a 20-step action budget. The evaluation surface spans 3,760 total checks (3,284 required) alongside 7,970 rendered multimodal assets. Table C1 summarizes benchmark variation across design axes. 

C.2. Continuous Measured Difficulty via Ground-Truth Trajectory Length 

Difficulty in CLASHarness is measured empirically via ground-truth reference trajectory length, spanning 7–18 steps, rather than arbitrary metadata labels. Stratifying tasks by reference trajectory length into easy (≤ 9 steps), mid (10–11 steps), and hard (≥ 12 steps) shows a consistent performance drop for 14 of the 15 evaluated models (corresponding to 20-step budget-to-step ratios 𝑏 /steps of 

≥ 2.22, 1 .82–2 .00, and < 1.82); the single exception is DeepSeek V4 Flash, whose mid-band pass rate (11 .2%) edges above its easy-band rate (8 .5%). 

C.3. Hybrid Grading Validity, Judge Integrity, and Competency Decoupling 

Of the 3,284 scored evaluation checks, 34.9% (1 ,147) are deterministic programmatic conditions and 65.1% (2 ,137) are targeted semantic criteria evaluated by an LLM judge (Table C2). All reported proportions correspond to required checks, excluding the 476 unscored diagnostic checks within the asset access gate. 

Judge Stability and Sensitivity Analysis. To ensure evaluation consistency, each semantic check is scored using a 3-sample self-consistency majority ensemble across 19 ,419 recorded evaluation calls. Overall, 97 .5% of verdicts are unanimous, with split decisions occurring on only 2 .5%—representing 479 checks, ranging from 1 .4% on Gemini 3.7 Flash to 3 .4% on Gemini 3.1 Pro. Sensitivity analysis shows that flipping all 479 split verdicts in the least favorable direction shifts model pass rates by at

E.2. Reasoning Effort Inversion Dynamics 

Across the ten 20-step API models (open-weight models do not report reasoning tokens), reasoning tokens per round vary 16-fold (Table E1) and correlate negatively with pass rate at 𝜌 = −0.697, and at 𝜌 = −0.915 against the reasoning share of output tokens. Leave-one-out sensitivity tests confirm this negative correlation holds across all ten models ( 𝜌 ∈ [− 0.783 , −0.583 ]). While executed steps vary by only a factor of 1 .85 (10.6 to 19.7 steps per round), reasoning tokens per action span a 9-fold range, from 50 to 448 tokens per step. In contrast to cross-model comparisons, testing different effort levels within the same model shows a different picture. Raising reasoning effort from Low to High significantly improves five of the ten API models, leaves four unchanged, and harms exactly one: Gemini 3.5 Flash drops from 13 .5% at Low effort to 3 .0% at High effort, a drop of 10 .5 pp (95% CI [− 13 .5, −7.7]). This drop occurs because repetitive reasoning loops eat up steps, causing the model to hit the 20-step budget cap on 91 .9% of its failed rounds. These results indicate that spending many reasoning tokens often signals planning inefficiency in interactive computer-use tasks: models fail when internal deliberation loops crowd out necessary actions in the environment. 

E.3. Execution Step Budget Saturation 

Running out of steps ( cap -hit ) accounts for 0 .7%–11 .4% of failed rounds across six of the seven leading 20-step models (and only 1 .0% on GPT-6 Astra at its 40-step budget), but rises to 51 .0% on Gemini 3.7 Flash, and to 30 .3%, 28 .8%, and 91 .9% on Gemini 3.6 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Gemini 3.5 Flash respectively. Gemini 3.7 Flash shows an interesting pattern: while achieving competitive overall pass rates, it spends 71 .8% of its output tokens on reasoning and runs out of steps on half of its failed rounds. Running out of steps is therefore a major bottleneck for reasoning-heavy models.

Threshold Effects Across Step Budgets. The marginal return of expanding the budget for Gemini 3.8 Flash shows a clear threshold effect: increasing from 𝑏 = 20 to 𝑏 = 30 yields +6.2 pp (12 .8% → 19 .0%), whereas expanding from 𝑏 = 30 to 𝑏 = 40 jumps by +10 .2 pp (19 .0% → 29 .2%). In complex multi-agent conflict scenarios, agents must first inspect contradictory logs, diagnose out-of-sync local states, and locate visual evidence before enacting coordinating fixes. High-capacity agents that conduct extensive diagnostic exploration require an operational threshold of steps before their solutions can be committed and verified. Once this threshold is surpassed, pass rates accelerate rapidly. Expanding further from 𝑏 = 40 to 𝑏 = 60 adds +1.5 pp to reach 30 .7%, while cap exhaustion drops from 19 .2% to 3 .3%. The saturation of gains alongside minimal cap exhaustion indicates that 30 .7% represents a genuine performance ceiling for the model architecture rather than an artificial truncation floor.

Productive Execution vs. Repetitive Looping. A critical distinction between Gemini 3.8 Flash and Gemini 3.5 Flash lies in their economic efficiency curves. F.4. Visual Necessity and Multimodal Grounding Ablation 

Withholding visual images (Figure 2b, Table F2) causes pass rates to drop sharply across all models. Even on the 254 rounds without a mandatory image check, blind agents keep only 11 .1% to 13 .1% of their sighted pass rates (24 .8% → 2.8% for Claude Opus 5, 24 .0% → 3.1% for Claude Sonnet 5, and 19 .3% → 2.4% for Gemini 3.6 Flash). With Bonferroni correction (0 .05 /6), all three drops are statistically significant (largest 𝑝 = 2.6 × 10 −12 ), with 60, 54, and 44 rounds solved only when images are available versus just 4, 1, and 1 when blind. This confirms that visual evidence is essential for resolving conflicts, rather than decorative background. 

F.5. Judge Selection and Cross-Judge Stability Offline Replay Protocol. To test whether benchmark results depend on the specific evaluator model, we replayed all 9 ,000 recorded high-effort rounds from the 14 20-step baseline models offline against original scenario data and reconstructed checker inputs from stored action trajectories. Deterministic checks were re-evaluated as a fidelity verification: all 24 ,331 deterministic checks reproduced their recorded verdicts (100 .0% fidelity), confirming that differences between evaluation runs stem entirely from semantic judging. The replay generated 28 ,009 judge prompts, which were re-evaluated by three substitute judges using 3-sample majority voting: Gemini 3.6 Flash (the default judge re-run to estimate sampling variance), Claude Sonnet 5, and Claude Opus 5. 

Common Evaluation Subset. Model endpoints occasionally decline prompts: Claude Sonnet 5 declined 161 prompts (0 .62%) and Claude Opus 5 declined 3 ,089 prompts (11 .8%), while the default judge completed all evaluations. To maintain fair comparisons across judges without missing-data bias, all reported metrics evaluate the common subset of 22 ,938 checks (87 .9%) scored by all three judges, using recorded verdicts for un-scored prompts uniformly across columns. 

Evaluator Consistency. Each judge ensemble demonstrates high internal consistency across its three voting samples. Disagreement rates among the three samples were 2 .78% for Gemini 3.6 Flash, 1.31% for Claude Sonnet 5, and 0 .34% for Claude Opus 5, confirming stable majority verdicts across all evaluators.
