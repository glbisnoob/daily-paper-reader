Abstract 

GUI grounding requires locating small controls in cluttered screenshots and identifying which control a natural-language instruction refers to. Cropping and enlargement can expose local detail, but the resulting image may still leave ambiguity about the intended target. We pro-pose P RE VIEW , an auxiliary preprocessor that improves the inputs to an existing grounding model without further training that model. P RE -VIEW prepares two complementary types of input features: task-relevant views for local-ization and candidate-specific cues for target understanding. A trained lightweight auxiliary model predicts and refines a center, crops the screenshot into a local view, and enlarges that view for zoomed-in grounding. It also prepares a visual cue (i.e., a red-circle marker with a location hint) and a textual cue describing the candidate’s identity or function. The frozen grounding model uses the prepared inputs to predict the final click. Across five benchmarks, PRE VIEW improves the average grounding ac-curacy of Qwen3-VL-2B, OS-Atlas-4B, and Qwen3-VL-4B without further training these grounding models. Extensive analyses exam-ine the contributions of views and cues, center refinement, and visual input budgets. 

1 Introduction 

GUI grounding translates a natural-language in-struction into the screen location of an actionable interface element. It connects an agent’s intended action to its execution: even a correct plan fails if the agent clicks the wrong control. Dedicated grounding models have advanced this capability through GUI-specific data and training, including SeeClick, UGround, OS-Atlas, and GroundNext (Cheng et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Feizi et al., 2025). Professional interfaces remain challenging because they combine high-resolution screenshots, dense layouts, and small

interactive elements (Li et al., 2025; Nayak et al., 2025). These settings raise a question beyond the capabilities of the grounder itself: how should the screenshot and instruction be presented to make the grounding decision easier? 

We consider two intertwined requirements: lo-calization and understanding . Localization re-quires resolving an actionable control’s precise po-sition amid competing visual content. When an entire screenshot is processed under a limited vi-sual budget, small controls may receive insufficient detail. Understanding requires determining which element satisfies the instruction, including its func-tional role, its relationship to other elements, and the object to which it belongs. Consider the in-struction to click “Get involved” in Figure 1. The screenshot contains two stacked notification bars, each with an action button and a close control. A arXiv:2610.33645v1 [cs.CV] 24 Oct 2026 clearer image can help resolve these controls, but selecting the intended one also requires associating the button with the requested notification. Con-versely, identifying the correct control does not guarantee an accurate click on its small target area. Existing work addresses these difficulties along several complementary routes. Training on broader and higher-quality GUI data strengthens the grounder’s learned representations (Wu et al., 2024; Feizi et al., 2025). At inference time, zoom-in methods crop and rescale selected regions to expose local detail, while repeated predictions pro-vide opportunities to revise a localization decision. ScreenSeekeR uses a planner to guide cascaded visual search (Li et al., 2025). ZoomClick itera-tively narrows the view around predictions (Jiang et al., 2025). UI-Zoomer uses prediction uncer-tainty to decide whether and how to zoom (Tang et al., 2026). These methods demonstrate that grounding depends on the observation and infer-ence procedure as well as model parameters. Ad-ditional searches or predictions consume inference resources. Tighter cropping can also discard con-text needed to distinguish the target from nearby alternatives. Semantic augmentation provides another source of support. Screen parsing can expose interactable elements and their functional descriptions, as in OmniParser (Lu et al., 2024). Instruction refine-ment can make an intended target more explicit. AdaZoom-GUI combines such refinement with conditional zooming (Pei et al., 2026). These ap-proaches show that visual focusing and semantic clarification can be complementary. They also mo-tivate a more specific question: can a learned aux-iliary model prepare both task-relevant views and informative cues for an existing grounder? Such preprocessing must select useful evidence while limiting two sources of error: removing necessary visual context and supplying misleading semantic descriptions. We propose P RE VIEW , an auxiliary model that previews the interface before the downstream grounding decision, without further training the grounding model. It prepares two complementary types of input features: task-relevant views 1 and candidate-specific cues . Given a screenshot and in-struction, it predicts and refines a center, then crops the screenshot around that center to produce a lo-

GUI grounding. GUI-specific supervision im-proves grounding in SeeClick, UGround, OS-Atlas, GroundCUA, and Phi-Ground (Cheng et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Feizi et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Architectural approaches include high-resolution encoding in CogAgent, visual-token selection in ShowUI, and coordinate-free action prediction in GUI-Actor (Hong et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Aguvis and UI-TARS integrate grounding into broader visual agents (Xu et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025). Visual prompting and semantic augmentation.

3 Method 

Given a screenshot I and an instruction q, P RE -VIEW uses a trained auxiliary model Aθ to prepare inputs for a frozen grounding model G. As shown in Figure 2, it prepares a task-relevant view for localization and candidate-specific cues for target understanding. The grounding model predicts the final click without parameter updates. 

3.1 Training Center prediction. Given an unmarked screen-shot and instruction, Aθ predicts the target-box center in image-relative coordinates. This predic-tion determines where to crop rather than the final click. 

Candidate verification and description. Aθ re-ceives a local view, an instruction, and a marked candidate. It learns from positive and negative ex-amples to describe a matching candidate’s identity or function and explicitly reject a mismatch.

For a screenshot of size W × H, we construct a rectangle of size approximately W √ρ × H√ρ around the predicted center, where ρ is the crop area ratio. We shift the rectangle inside image boundaries when necessary and enlarge the resulting view. Refine-ment uses a 50% -area crop enlarged by √2 per dimension (P50 → Full). Visual and textual cues. The visual cue pairs a translucent red circle at c2 with a fixed location hint h explaining the marker. Although written in text, h belongs to this cue because it instructs the model how to use the visual marker. For the textual cue , Aθ generates a description d from the marked view and q. The description identifies the candi-date’s identity, function, or relationships. When cues are enabled and the candidate is accepted, the grounding input is

A REJECT response indicates that Aθ judges the candidate inconsistent with q. In this case, we with-hold the textual cue d.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets Training data. We construct training examples from 50,000 desktop-application screenshots in GroundCUA, using its element bounding boxes and official instruction-tuning annotations (Feizi et al., 2025). Center supervision is the geometric target-box center, normalized to [0 , 1000] 2. Posi-tive descriptions come from the official instruction-tuning release, matched by screenshot and target element. For each positive example, we randomly sample a candidate point within the target bounding box. Negative examples use annotated non-target elements with the response REJECT . The resulting mixture contains 50,000 center-prediction exam-ples, 49,311 positive description examples, and 49,284 rejection examples. 

Evaluation benchmarks. We adopt the five-benchmark suite used by Feizi et al. (2025): ScreenSpot-Pro (SSPro; Li et al., 2025), OSWorld-G (OSW-G; Xie et al., 2025), MMBench-GUI L2 (MMB-GUI; Wang et al., 2025), ScreenSpot-v2 (SSv2; Wu et al., 2024), and UI-Vision Element Grounding (UI-V; Nayak et al., 2025). OSW-G O

and OSW-G R denote separate original/refined in-struction conditions. Table 7 in Appendix B sum-marizes benchmark sizes. Appendix D reports category-level results for the two Qwen3-VL mod-els.

13 .9, and 1.5 percentage points for Qwen3-VL-2B, OS-Atlas-4B, and Qwen3-VL-4B, respectively. The first two models improve on four benchmark families: SSPro, OSW-G, MMB-GUI, and UI-V. 5.4 Visual Budget Ablation 

Selecting useful content and enlarging it make com-plementary contributions (Figure 4). At the same 

25% pixel budget, enlarging a 12 .5% -area crop beats using a native 25% -area crop on all three benchmarks with identical processed visual-token counts. The gain therefore depends on how the budget is allocated, not simply on supplying more tokens. For a fixed 12 .5% -area crop, doubling both dimensions adds 6.5–10 .8 points, showing the ad-ditional value of enlargement. At the same 50% vi-sual budget, smaller crops perform better on SSPro and UI-V, while the wider crop improves OSW-G R

by 0.71 points.

Input preparation can help even when the ground-ing model was trained on the data used to train the auxiliary model. P RE VIEW constructs 148,595 training examples from a subset of GroundCUA, only 21% as many as the 700,000 examples used for GroundNext’s supervised fine-tuning (Feizi et al., 2025). With View, GroundNext’s accuracy rises from 52 .9% to 59 .0% on SSPro and from 

67 .7% to 68 .3% on OSW-G R (Table 6). These gains suggest that GroundNext can still benefit from task-relevant views after being trained on GroundCUA, the same source used to train P RE -VIEW .

6 Conclusion 

We introduced P RE VIEW , an auxiliary preproces-sor that improves GUI grounding without updat-ing the downstream model. Center-guided views support localization, while visual and textual cues support target understanding. Across five bench-marks and three grounding models, it improves re-ported average accuracy by 1.5–13 .9 points. These gains require only one call to the frozen ground-ing model. Ablations demonstrate complementary benefits from views and cues, while error analysis identifies target exclusion and misleading descrip-tions as failure modes. Future work should adapt view selection and cue use based on estimated tar-get coverage and cue reliability. 

Limitations 

Cropping may discard targets or necessary context, while incorrect cues may mislead the grounding model. Training on desktop applications limits generalization to mobile interfaces, and offline eval-uation does not establish reliability in multi-step in-teraction. Auxiliary inference adds computational cost and latency. We report a single run per configu-ration, so results may be influenced by initialization noise. 

Ethical Considerations 

The datasets used in this study are publicly avail-able and were obtained through their official release channels. Incorrect grounding predictions can lead to unintended GUI operations, potentially modify-ing or deleting information or triggering unwanted actions. Outputs should therefore be carefully re-viewed before use, with human confirmation for consequential operations.

We list the task-specific prompts used by P RE -VIEW . Braced fields, highlighted in dark red, de-note example-specific placeholders. Image place-holders represent image attachments rather than literal prompt text. Both auxiliary tasks share the system message shown below and include one im-age in the user message. The grounder retains its model-specific chat template and coordinate-output instructions. 

Center prediction. The image is the unmarked screenshot during training and the current un-marked view during inference. The same prompt is used for both center-prediction stages. 

> [System]
> You are a helpful assistant.
> [User]
> {unmarked_image} Locate the UI element requested by the instruction. Instruction: {instruction} Return only <center>x, y</center>. Coordinates are normalized to 0–1000 within this image.

Candidate verification and description. The in-put image is the enlarged view with a translucent red circle at the candidate center. Candidate coor-dinates are normalized within this view. 

> [System]
> You are a helpful assistant.
> [User]
> {marked_view} Instruction: {instruction} Candidate center: ({x}, {y}) Coordinates are normalized to 0–1000 within this image. The translucent red circle marks the candidate center. If this candidate is the requested target, return <desc>description</desc>. Otherwise, return <desc>REJECT</desc>.

Grounding with paired cues. For an accepted candidate that passes consistency filtering, the grounding model sees the same marked view. The following augmented instruction is embedded in its native grounding prompt: 

> [System]
> ...
> [User]
> {marked_view} ... {instruction} The red circle is a location hint. If you are unsure where to click, click the center of the red circle. The marked element is described as: {description} ...

Ellipses denote unchanged model-specific sys-tem messages, tool definitions, and user-prompt wrappers. The system message is omitted when absent from the native template. The first two ap-pended sentences form the fixed location hint and belong to the Visual Cue. The final sentence intro-duces the generated Textual Cue. When verifica-tion rejects the candidate but consistency filtering passes, only the marker and location hint are re-tained. When consistency filtering fails, View-only fallback uses the unmarked view and original in-struction without either cue.
