Abstract
Mobile UI understanding and generation require both fine-grained component
perception and reliable structured synthesis. Inspired by the unified direction
introduced by UI-UG [46], we present UI-UG 2, a compact 4B-parameter mul-
timodal large language model (MLLM) that advances mobile UI Understanding
and Generation with broader task coverage, a front-end-aligned taxonomy, and
online render-aware reinforcement learning. UI-UG 2 constructs a multi-source
understanding corpus from RICO, VINS, Android in the Wild, and Android Con-
trol, annotated with an 80-category two-level UI taxonomy aligned with front-end
component semantics, and pairs it with over 5,000 requirement–screenshot–JSON
tuples for progressive structured UI generation through a domain-specific language
(DSL). For understanding, UI-UG 2 introduces decomposed rewards for category
prediction, OCR fidelity, color recognition, dense grounding, and GUI ground-
ing. Unlike the offline Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)-style generation
optimization in UI-UG, UI-UG 2 optimizes generation through end-to-end online
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): sampled DSL outputs are rendered
with a Playwright-based server-side pipeline and evaluated using LLM-as-a-Judge
and contrastive preference ranking. Experiments show that UI-UG 2 achieves
the strongest referring and dense-grounding results among the evaluated models
on our 80-category benchmark, and achieves the best composite generation score
among compared models at a compact scale (4B). More importantly, we observe a
consistent understanding-to-generation transfer: adding fine-grained UI perception
supervision improves generation quality under a fixed training budget, suggesting
that component-level semantics provide a shared representational foundation for
both tasks.

Introduction
Mobile user interfaces are the primary bridge between users and digital services. As applications
grow in complexity, the ability to accurately understand and generate UIs underpins a broad spectrum
of downstream applications, from screen parsing and GUI agent interaction to personalized interface
synthesis and LLM-driven front-end development. These two capabilities, however, have largely
evolved along separate tracks, with separate models, separate data pipelines, and separate evaluation
protocols.
General-purpose MLLMs such as GPT [32], Claude [2], Gemini [13], and Qwen-VL [3] can nomi-
nally perform both tasks, yet their accuracy remains insufficient for precision-critical mobile scenarios.

Mobile screens are fundamentally different from natural images: they are dense compositions of small,
semantically loaded components—buttons, icons, text fields, navigation bars—whose interaction
semantics require fine-grained categorical reasoning. Distinguishing a CloseButton from a Return-
Button, or a SearchBar from a generic TextInput, is essential for reliable automation and high-quality
generation, but lies beyond the granularity that general-purpose models reliably achieve. Specialized
approaches address one side of the problem—Ferret-UI [48] and Ferret-UI2 [23] for grounding and
referring, DCGen [41] and DeclarUI [52] for structured generation—while UI-UG [46] took an initial
step toward a unified model. However, important limitations remain: its labels were not explicitly
organized around front-end component semantics, its understanding coverage focused mainly on
referring and dense grounding, its generation optimization relied on offline Direct Preference Opti-
mization (DPO) over a fixed preference dataset, and its evidence for understanding-to-generation
transfer remained preliminary.
We present UI-UG 2, a compact 4B MLLM for mobile UI Understanding and Generation, with an
overview illustrated in Figure 1. UI-UG 2 keeps the central goal of unified UI understanding and
generation while strengthening the data, training, and analysis pipeline. On the data side, we construct
a large-scale understanding benchmark from four public sources (RICO, VINS, Android in the Wild,
and Android Control), covering over 30,000 screenshots with 80 fine-grained element categories
organized in a two-level hierarchy aligned to front-end conventions, enriched with element-level,
action-level, OCR, and color annotations. We pair this with a curated generation corpus of over 5,000
requirement–screenshot–JSON triples whose compact domain-specific language (DSL) supports
cross-platform rendering and progressive streaming.
On the optimization side, we adopt a two-stage pipeline of supervised fine-tuning followed by
task-specific Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For understanding, we design decom-
posed rewards targeting format compliance, category prediction, OCR fidelity, color recognition,
dense grounding, and GUI grounding. For generation, UI-UG 2 performs online render-aware RL:
each sampled DSL is rendered by a Playwright-based server-side pipeline, then evaluated through
LLM-as-a-Judge and Contrastive Preference Ranking (CPR). This closes the loop between struc-
tured generation, actual rendering, and reward feedback, making optimization better aligned with
deployment-time UI quality.
Experiments demonstrate that UI-UG 2 achieves the strongest referring and dense-grounding results
among the compared models on our benchmark, strong GUI grounding on ScreenSpot mobile
benchmarks, and the best overall generation quality among compared models at a compact 4B scale.
Through data-mixing ablations, reward transfer analysis, and attention probing, we further observe
an asymmetric synergy: understanding supervision improves generation quality at an appropriate
data-mixing ratio. This finding—that component-level perceptual representations form a transferable
foundation for generative tasks—offers a concrete design principle for future mobile UI systems.

Our contributions are:
• UI-UG 2. We propose UI-UG 2, a compact unified MLLM that jointly supports mobile
UI understanding and generation, advancing the UI-UG direction [46] with broader task
coverage, stronger efficiency, and more rigorous optimization. UI-UG 2 achieves the best
results among the compared models on our newly constructed referring and dense-grounding
benchmark, and obtains the best composite generation score among all compared baselines
despite using only a compact 4B backbone.
• Front-end-aligned data and tasks. We contribute an 80-category hierarchical UI under-
standing dataset with taxonomy aligned to front-end conventions, supporting referring, dense
grounding, and GUI grounding, together with a high-quality UI generation corpus based on
a compact, renderable JSON DSL.
• Task-specific online GRPO. We introduce decomposed reward functions for category, OCR,
color, dense grounding, GUI grounding, format validity, renderability, LLM-judged UI qual-
ity, and CPR-based preference, enabling online render-aware optimization for generation.
• Understanding-to-generation synergy. We provide systematic evidence that fine-grained
UI perception consistently benefits generation quality, supported by data-mixing ablations,
reward transfer analysis, and attention probing.

Related Work
UI Understanding. UI understanding is central to screen parsing, interface analysis and intelligent
automation. Early advances were enabled by datasets such as VINS [5], ERICA [10], WebSRC [7],
and RICO [11]. Methodologically, the field has evolved from relying on structured signals such
as Document Object Models (DOMs) or view hierarchies [6] to parsing interfaces directly from
pixels [43, 20, 27, 28]. More recently, MLLM-based models such as Ferret-UI [48] and Ferret-
UI2 [23] have unified multiple UI understanding tasks, while agent-oriented grounding models such
as SeeClick [8], ShowUI [24], UI-TARS [33], GUI-Owl [47] and UI-Venus [14] have improved
fine-grained localization on benchmarks such as ScreenSpot Series [8, 21]. Compared with prior
work, we introduce a richer front-end-aligned taxonomy and support a more comprehensive set of
fine-grained UI understanding tasks.
UI Generation. Automated UI generation aims to translate visual designs or user requirements
into executable interface code. Early approaches such as pix2code [4], Sketch2Code [18], and
REMAUI [31] focused on image-to-code prediction or explicit component reconstruction. More
recent MLLM-based methods, including DCGen [41] and DeclarUI [52], improve structured UI
generation through stronger multimodal reasoning. Benchmarks such as Web2Code [50] and Live-
CodeBench [17] have also helped standardize evaluation. In contrast, we study MLLM-based UI
generation from both user requirements and reference images, with additional support for interactive
prototyping.
Unified UI Understanding and Generation. UI-UG [46] first explored jointly training mobile
UI understanding and structured UI generation in a single MLLM, using a renderable DSL and
task-aware post-training. This line of work suggests that UI perception and UI synthesis can share a
common representation, but several questions remain open, including how to define front-end-aligned
supervision, how to optimize generation under rendered visual feedback, and how to analyze transfer
between understanding and generation more systematically.
Reinforcement Learning and Multi-task MLLMs. Post-training of MLLMs has evolved from
RLHF [9] to preference optimization such as DPO [34] and efficient policy optimization such as
GRPO [15], with recent multimodal extensions including Visual-RFT [26], R1-VL [51], and VLM-
R1 [37]. We apply GRPO to UI understanding and generation with task-specific rewards for structured
perception and renderable synthesis. Related multi-task studies show that heterogeneous objectives
can cause imbalance and asymmetric transfer [25, 39, 35], motivating our sequential optimization
and transfer analysis.

Data and Structured Representation
3.1
Understanding Corpus and Annotation
UI-UG 2 uses structured supervision for both UI understanding and generation. For understanding,
we curate over 30,000 deduplicated mobile screenshots from RICO [11], VINS [5], Android in the
Wild [36], and Android Control [22]. The data combine element-level supervision from RICO/VINS
with action-level interaction semantics from AITW/AC, which we convert into instruction-conditioned
GUI grounding samples.
We annotate screenshots with an 80-category two-level taxonomy (9 parent classes + 71 child
classes) aligned with common front-end component semantics, drawing on naming and interaction
patterns found in libraries such as Ant Design [1], Material UI [30], and Vant [49]. A human-in-the-
loop bootstrapping pipeline expands an annotated seed set with a post-trained detection model [19],
OCR, and color labels, followed by human refinement; agreement reaches Cohen’s κ > 0.82 on a
500-image held-out set. Each element receives a category and a bounding box; text-like elements
additionally receive OCR text and text-color annotations. The taxonomy distinguishes functional
roles (e.g., ReturnButton versus CloseButton), component states, and truncated elements near
screen boundaries. The CLIP-deduplicated corpus draws approximately 7.5K screenshots from each
source. The complete taxonomy and distribution analyses appear in Appendices A and B.
3.2
Generation Corpus and Rendering Pipeline
For generation, we retain the renderable JSON DSL used by UI-UG [46] instead of raw HTML/CSS,
as it offers a compact, structured, and cross-platform interface for progressive UI rendering, following
the broader industrial trend of DSL-to-UI systems such as Google/A2UI [12] and Vercel/json-
renderer [40].
We improve the corpus with stronger MLLM distillation, richer requirements,
and stricter rendering-based filtering. The final generation data contain over 5,000 high-quality
requirement–image–JSON tuples; the DSL reduces token usage by 16.7% on average compared with
equivalent HTML. Figure 2 shows the corpus construction pipeline. Each accepted tuple couples a
natural-language UI requirement, a reference screenshot, and a JSON specification that passes schema
and rendering checks. The shared renderer also executes sampled DSL outputs during generation
GRPO.

3.3
Renderable JSON Representation
Our JSON-based DSL describes UI content and styling features. The basic node attributes include:
• type: Node type, either "Component" or "Tag".
• name: If type is "Component", the component name from the available components list; if
"Tag", an HTML tag name.

• className: Tailwind CSS class names using relative layouts. Width/height can be absolute
(e.g., w-[100px]) or relative (e.g., w-full).
• params: Content features of the node. Must include bindType ("Static" or "Data") to
indicate whether the value is hardcoded or dynamically bound.
• children: A list of child nodes.
The representation separates component identity, layout/styling, content binding, and child structure.
It supports progressive rendering as the model produces the structured specification. Figure 10 in
Appendix C gives a complete rendered example. A minimal button can be expressed as:

Method
Given the data and representation in Section 3, we train UI-UG 2 with unified supervised fine-tuning
followed by task-specific GRPO. Compared with UI-UG [46], UI-UG 2 uses a smaller 4B backbone
and replaces offline generation preference learning with online render-aware GRPO.
4.1
Supervised Fine-tuning
SFT Data Construction. We construct a unified SFT dataset for referring, grounding, and
generation, with all targets expressed in JSON for reliable parsing. Referring predicts category, OCR
text, and color from a given box; grounding covers dense full-screen localization and instruction-
conditioned captioned grounding; generation maps a requirement and optional reference screenshot
to a structured JSON DSL. Dense-grounding boxes are sorted by category frequency and top-left
coordinates to expose repeated layout regularities.
Model Fine-tuning. We initialize UI-UG 2-4B-SFT from Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, freeze the ViT
encoder, and train the LLM and VL adapter. The training mixture and hyperparameters are reported
in Section 4.3.
4.2
Reinforcement Learning via GRPO

generation.

Sequential GRPO Training. We use sequential task optimization, as supported by the schedule
ablation in Section 5.3. The selected curriculum optimizes generation, then referring, then grounding,
with one GRPO epoch per stage. Training settings are reported below.
4.3
Training Configuration
We train the policy on 4 A100 80GB GPUs. SFT uses 120K instruction samples (approximately 0.6B
tokens) with a referring:dense/GUI-grounding:generation ratio of 3:3:4. GRPO uses 6,000 referring
samples, 6,000 grounding samples, and 2,000 generation samples, with eight rollouts per prompt.
The policy-training time is approximately two days for SFT and two days for GRPO; it does not
include a separate accounting of judge-service compute.

Experiments
We compare UI-UG 2-4B against both general-purpose MLLMs (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5,
Gemini 3.1 Pro, Kimi K2.5 [38], GLM-4.5V [16], Mistral Large 3 [29], and the Qwen3-VL series)
and UI-specific models (Ferret-UI2, OmniParser V2, ShowUI, UI-TARS, GUI-Owl, and UI-Venus)
on UI understanding and UI generation. Compared with UI-UG [46], the evaluation here is broader
in both task coverage and evidence: we add GUI grounding on ScreenSpot mobile benchmarks, use
confidence-free dense-grounding metrics tailored to MLLM outputs, and include broader ablations
on online RL, reward transfer, and understanding-generation interaction.
5.1
UI Understanding
UI understanding consists of Referring, Dense Grounding, and GUI Grounding. Referring and
Dense Grounding are evaluated on our in-house dataset, while GUI Grounding is evaluated on public
7


mobile benchmarks. The former assesses the proposed annotation space within the curated corpus;
ScreenSpot provides complementary external single-target localization evidence.
Evaluation. We evaluate Referring by predicting category, OCR text, and color attribute from
a ground-truth box, using Category Accuracy (Cat), OCR-Acc (Eq. 2), and Color-Acc (Eq. 3).
Dense Grounding is evaluated by F1, Precision, Recall, and mIoU; because MLLMs do not provide
confidence scores, we use set-matching F1 rather than AP-style metrics. GUI Grounding is evaluated
by click accuracy on the mobile splits of ScreenSpot V1 [8] and ScreenSpot-V2 [45], yielding 502 and
449 samples after mobile-only filtering. All MLLMs use standardized prompts with temperature 0
and model-specific coordinate adaptations when needed. For models whose interfaces do not support
certain tasks, we mark the corresponding entries as unsupported in Table 2; UI-UG-7B [46] is mapped
to our taxonomy for comparison.
Dense set matching. After parsing and discarding malformed or out-of-taxonomy predictions, we
match predicted and reference boxes with a Hungarian assignment whose cost is 1 −IoU for equal
categories and 1 otherwise. A true positive requires equal categories and IoU ≥0.5. Precision,
recall, and F1 are computed per image and then averaged; mIoU is computed over category-correct
true-positive matches. Consequently, the reported mean F1 need not equal the harmonic mean of
mean precision and mean recall. Appendix E provides the step-by-step protocol.
Results. Table 2 shows that UI-UG 2-4B achieves the best overall performance on Referring and
Dense Grounding.
On Referring, general-purpose MLLMs remain inconsistent: the strongest closed-source baseline,
Gemini 3.1 Pro, reaches 0.535 category accuracy, and the best open-source general model, Kimi K2.5,
reaches 0.514. Relative to UI-UG-7B, UI-UG 2 pushes the unified setting to a substantially stronger
operating point: UI-UG 2-4B-SFT already reaches 0.904/0.930/0.727 on Cat/OCR/Color, and GRPO
further improves this to 0.906/0.938/0.739. The same-backbone SFT-to-GRPO comparison isolates
the additional post-training gain. The comparison to UI-UG-7B also changes the backbone, label
space, and data, so it does not isolate any single design choice.
On Dense Grounding, the margin is larger: the best general-purpose model, Gemini 3.1 Pro, achieves
0.471 F1, while UI-UG 2-4B-SFT reaches 0.742 F1 and 0.865 mIoU, and GRPO improves this further
to 0.777 F1 and 0.894 mIoU (+0.035 F1 and +0.029 mIoU over SFT). Relative to UI-UG-7B, the
new model again advances the unified setting by a wide margin, with the difference most visible in
recall because the earlier 20-class label space cannot fully cover our 80-category protocol. Among
UI-specific models, ShowUI and UI-TARS expose point/action-based single-target interfaces and
do not provide the exhaustive typed boxes or referring attributes required by this protocol, whereas
GUI-Owl and UI-Venus-Ground retain grounding ability.
On GUI Grounding, UI-UG 2-4B attains 0.902/0.929 on ScreenSpot-V1/V2 mobile, outperforming
most open-source general MLLMs and competitive 7B specialists such as UI-TARS-7B (0.886/0.911).
Several larger general-purpose models and task-specific baselines remain higher, including UI-Venus-
Ground-7B (0.910/0.951) and UI-Venus-Ground-72B (0.922/0.971), likely because they are optimized
only for single-target localization. Still, UI-UG 2-4B clearly surpasses its base model Qwen3-VL-4B,
validating the effectiveness of the unified training recipe.

Ablation Study
RL Training Strategy. We ablate RL objectives and training schedules. Figure 3 reports improvement
over the SFT baseline in referring accuracy (∆Ref Acc), grounding F1 (∆Grd F1), and generation
score (∆Gen) across joint training and 1/2/3-stage sequential RL configurations.
Finding 1: RL improves over SFT. Task-aligned RL consistently improves each target capability
over the SFT baseline: single-stage GenerationRL raises generation score by +4.43%, GroundingRL
improves grounding F1 by +4.02%, and ReferringRL increases referring accuracy by +1.47%.
Notably, understanding-oriented RL also benefits generation, suggesting that stronger element-level
understanding transfers to UI generation. The gains further scale with the number of training stages,
as 2-stage and 3-stage sequential configurations progressively improve all three metrics.
Finding 2: Sequential outperforms joint optimization. Joint RL yields Gen +2.73%, Grd F1
+2.70%, and Ref Acc +0.86%, below the selected sequential configurations at the same reported
training budget. The tasks differ in response length and reward distribution; this pattern is consistent
with greater interference during simultaneous optimization. The pattern is consistent with prior work
showing that heterogeneous rewards are difficult to combine into a single stable objective [44].

Discussion and Limitations
Benchmark scope and comparisons. The largest understanding gains are measured on the proposed
80-category corpus and should be interpreted as in-domain results. ScreenSpot mobile evaluates
external single-target localization, where several larger or specialized models remain stronger. Na-
tive point/action-based specialists do not expose the typed exhaustive predictions required by our
dense/referring protocol; unsupported entries indicate an interface mismatch, not a measured fail-
ure on their native task. The UI-UG-7B comparison changes the backbone, data, and taxonomy
simultaneously, whereas the Qwen3-VL-4B and UI-UG 2-4B-SFT rows provide same-backbone
controls.
Generation evaluation and reward alignment. Generation rewards and reported scores share four
visual-quality dimensions, so optimization against the rubric may overstate improvements outside that
rubric. The human-reference study is limited to 100 cases and is post-hoc rather than a continuous
audit during GRPO. Screenshot-based judgments of usability assess visual affordances; they do not
replace interactive functional testing, accessibility evaluation, or end-user studies. Online rendering
and large-model judging also add service compute beyond the reported policy-training budget.
Transfer and deployment. The fixed-budget mixture and reward ablations support a benefit from
selected understanding signals, but some isolated rewards reduce other generation dimensions.
Attention alignment is descriptive evidence rather than causal identification. Rare UI types outside
the taxonomy, distribution shifts, and environments beyond the studied mobile setting remain open
challenges. Finally, the appendix’s latency extrapolations use active parameter count as a rough
scaling assumption; they are not direct deployment benchmarks of every model.
7
Conclusion
We presented UI-UG 2, a unified 4B MLLM for mobile UI understanding and generation. With
a front-end-aligned taxonomy, broader grounding supervision, a compact renderable DSL, and
online render-aware GRPO, UI-UG 2 achieves strong results across referring, dense grounding, GUI
grounding, and UI generation. Our ablations further show asymmetric transfer from fine-grained
understanding to generation, suggesting that component-level UI perception is a useful foundation
for structured UI synthesis.

Complete UI Element Taxonomy
This section provides a full taxonomy reference used throughout our UI understanding and generation
pipeline. We include the complete parent-child structure together with front-end correspondence for
reproducibility.
Our taxonomy follows the structure in “Mobile UI Component Taxonomy: Category Definitions and
Library Mappings,” with 80 categories in total, organized as 9 parent classes + 71 fine-grained
subcategories. The parent classes are annotation super-categories designed for stable dense labeling
rather than one-to-one modules in any single front-end library. In this appendix, Ant Design Mobile
is used as a practical reference for front-end correspondence. Table 7 further situates our taxonomy
against representative public UI datasets.
Compared with coarse UI labels, this taxonomy explicitly models three properties that are critical for
UI understanding: (1) state-level variants (e.g., checked/unchecked, fold/unfold), (2) functional
variants (e.g., different button and card roles), and (3) boundary truncation variants for partially
visible elements near screen edges. These design choices improve semantic coverage and annotation
usability for dense grounding and referring tasks. Table 6 provides the complete 9-parent hierarchy
and mapping rationale, with subcategory counts summing strictly to 71.

This section summarizes how the UI understanding dataset is composed and why its distribution is

B.1
Data Sources and Scale
Following the data setup in the main text (Data Construction section), our curated UI understanding
dataset is built from four sources: RICO, VINS, Android in the Wild, and Android Control. After
CLIP-based deduplication, the merged corpus contains over 30,000 unique screenshots, with an
almost balanced contribution of roughly ∼7.5k images per source. All samples are annotated under
our 80-category taxonomy (9 parent classes + 71 child classes), providing unified supervision for UI
understanding across heterogeneous mobile UI domains.

B.3
Distribution Balance Analysis
Our final dataset is constructed by mixing screenshots from all four sources (RICO, VINS, AITW,

reduces the risk that the model overfits to one dominant app scenario.

the complexity bias of individual datasets and provides more stable supervision for both easy cases
(format and basic localization consistency) and hard cases (fine-grained category discrimination and
dense grounding under crowded layouts).
In summary, the four-dataset mixture serves as a practical balancing strategy: it compensates for
scene-type imbalance and complexity imbalance in any single source, yielding a more diverse and
better-conditioned training distribution for unified UI understanding and generation.

E
Dense Grounding Evaluation Protocol
Because dense grounding outputs are variable-length structured predictions, we use a dedicated
set-matching evaluation protocol rather than confidence-based detection metrics.
Dense grounding evaluation requires matching a variable-length set of predicted bounding boxes to a
variable-length set of ground-truth boxes, which is fundamentally a set-matching problem. Unlike
standard object detection benchmarks that rely on confidence-score-based AP metrics, MLLMs do
not produce per-box confidence scores. We therefore adopt a set-matching protocol:
Step 1: Parsing and filtering.
We parse the model’s structured JSON output to extract predicted bounding boxes and their associated
category labels. Predictions with malformed coordinates or categories outside the 80-class taxonomy
are discarded.

truth boxes, where each entry is:

Here ˆcj and ci denote the predicted and ground-truth category labels. We then apply the Hungarian
algorithm to find the optimal one-to-one assignment that minimizes total cost.
Step 3: IoU thresholding.
A matched pair (ˆbj, bi) is counted as a true positive only if both (a) the categories match (ˆcj = ci)
and (b) IoU(ˆbj, bi) ≥τ, where we use τ = 0.5 as the default threshold. Unmatched predictions are
false positives; unmatched ground-truth boxes are false negatives.
Step 4: Metric computation.
We compute:
• Precision = TP/(TP + FP)
• Recall = TP/(TP + FN)
• F1: computed per sample from its precision and recall, then averaged over the evaluation
set.
• mIoU: computed over category-correct true-positive matched pairs, reflecting localization
quality after semantic matching.
This protocol ensures fair comparison across models with different output formats.

Although all evaluated MLLMs are prompted with the full 80-category taxonomy, strict type matching
remains challenging in practice. Two factors are prominent: (1) limited instruction-following
consistency for constrained category output, and (2) limited fine-grained visual discrimination

relaxed matching mode via post-processing, where visually and semantically similar predicted types
are tolerated.

strict-mode errors are near-miss confusions among closely related categories rather than complete
perception failures. This analysis helps disentangle category granularity mismatch from core UI
understanding capability.

Figure 15 presents qualitative comparisons of grounding results across representative models on two

grounding results.
