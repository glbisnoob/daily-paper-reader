Abstract 

Graphical User Interface (GUI) grounding aims to map natural language instructions to precise operational coordinates, serv-ing as a critical task for autonomous agent interaction. Current approaches formalize GUI grounding as predicting action-coordinate pairs from instructions, with end-to-end training supervised solely by final coordinates. Training datasets typ-ically provide only a small number of descriptions per tar-get coordinate, whereas real-world scenarios involve a much larger set of varied descriptions for the same target, which limits robustness to linguistic variation. While recent GUI grounding paradigms have begun employing reinforcement learning to enhance localization accuracy, models with lim-ited exploratory capability before reinforcement yield con-strained improvements even with identical training data during the reinforcement phase. To overcome these limitations, we propose a bidirectional reward paradigm that enables model self-reinforcement. During SFT, bidirectional training equips the model to generate action descriptions from target coordi-nates. During RL, the model samples semantically consistent instruction variations on-policy and grounds each variation back to its target. A cycle-consistency gate couples seman-tic quality to grounding success, turning the model’s own instruction policy into a source of continually refreshed re-inforcement trajectories. Our 4B-parameter model achieves competitive accuracy across five grounding benchmarks in our evaluation, indicating the effectiveness of the proposed training paradigm. https://github.com/Spell-shi/BiGrounder 

## Introduction 

Current approaches in GUI grounding predominantly fo-cus on learning direct mappings from singular operational descriptions to screen coordinates. However, this paradigm overlooks a critical characteristic of real-world applications: the complex many-to-one mapping relationship between user instructions and target coordinates. Specifically, users may employ diverse expressions, varying levels of detail, and dif-ferent linguistic styles to refer to the same interface element. Such variability in natural language can significantly impair coordinate prediction, especially when instructions differ in length or style. To mitigate this challenge, existing research primarily adopts offline data augmentation strategies (Liu et al. 2025a; Gou et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025a; Liu et al. 2025c), using in-struction paraphrasing and expansion to improve model gen-eralization. However, because the augmented instructions are generated in advance and remain fixed throughout training, the model can explore only a predefined sampling space that may not capture the breadth and evolving nature of instruc-tional variations encountered in real-world scenarios. From a training paradigm perspective, recent work has in-creasingly explored reinforcement learning (Lu et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025b) for improving GUI grounding per-formance, typically utilizing coordinate hits within target bounding boxes as the primary reward signal. Neverthe-less, current reinforcement learning methods (Yuan et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025b) predominantly operate on fixed of-fline datasets, resulting in limited exploration capabilities. To address the aforementioned limitations, we aim for agents to not only fit the "instruction →coordinate" out-come mapping but also learn robust semantic-spatial align-ment representations based on local visual evidence from coordinate-corresponding regions, thereby better adapting to diverse interfaces and instruction expressions in real-world scenarios. To this end, we propose BiGrounder: a reinforcement learning framework based on bidirectional se-mantic verification. Beyond traditional forward localization, the model generates instructions from annotated target coor-dinates and then grounds its own on-policy generations back to the same elements. This turns reverse generation into an endogenous exploration mechanism: as the policy evolves, it continually supplies instruction variations that are seman-tically anchored to the target yet reflect the model’s current behavior. The resulting reverse and forward trajectories re-inforce semantic–spatial alignment through cycle-consistent rewards. Experimental results demonstrate competitive re-sults across multiple industry-standard benchmarks. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• Constructing GUI understanding data from ground-ing annotations. We reverse existing instruction-to-coordinate grounding samples into coordinate-conditioned instruction-generation data and incorporate them into bidirectional SFT, providing explicit supervi-sion for learning the semantic and spatial properties of GUI elements.

## Related Works 

High-Quality Dataset Construction and Instruction Augmentation 

Several works improve data quality and instruction coverage. UI-E2I-Synth (Liu et al. 2025a), AutoGUI (Li et al. 2025a), OS-ATLAS (Wu et al. 2024), UGround (Gou et al. 2025), and JEDI (Xie et al. 2025) offline expand grounding datasets by generating diverse instructions and functionality descriptions with large language models, while GroundCUA (Feizi et al. 2025) and ScaleCUA (Liu et al. 2025c) highlight the value of high-quality human-annotated and GUI-understanding data. However, such offline augmentation confines instructions to predefined sample sets and cannot progressively evolve with the model during training, making it hard to cover the wide range of real-world instruction expressions. 

Reinforcement Learning in GUI Agents 

Recent research explores various strategies to enhance GUI grounding with RL. One line refines the reward signal: UI-R1 (Lu et al. 2025) uses a composite accuracy/action/format reward and shows explicit <think> tokens are redundant, GUI-G1 (Zhou et al. 2025b) curbs reward hacking by shap-ing reward granularity with hits, IoU, and element size, and GUI-G 2 (Tang et al. 2025) models elements as continuous Gaussian distributions to turn sparse binary hits into dense optimization. A second line targets the training framework and exploration: GUI-R1 (Luo et al. 2025) adopts an RFT framework with a unified action space, SE-GUI (Yuan et al. 2025) filters overly difficult samples via attention foci from the previous round, infiGUI-G1 (Liu et al. 2025b) performs adaptive exploration policy optimization over multiple sam-pled answers, UI-Ins (Chen et al. 2025) alleviates policy collapse through instruction refinement and multi-view SFT, and GUI-RCPO (Du et al. 2025) self-evolves on unlabeled data via test-time sampling. Overall, RL is an effective and efficient approach for advancing GUI grounding. However, existing methods reward the model mainly by grounding accuracy and lack semantic rewards for generating correct instructions or descriptions of UI elements. 

## Method 

Overview. We propose BiGrounder, a two-stage frame-work based on bidirectional semantic verification for en-hancing visual-language alignment in GUI agents. Un-like traditional paradigms that only learn unidirectional "instruction →coordinate" mappings, BiGrounder addition-ally learns a reverse generation policy conditioned on ground-truth target coordinates. During SFT, the model jointly learns forward localization and reverse description, acquiring both the ability to locate elements from instructions and to generate semantic descriptions from element regions. During RL, this reverse capability becomes an on-policy source of instruction variation. The cycle comprises two steps: (1) instruction generation : conditioned on the ground-truth target coordinate o∗, the policy generates descriptions or interaction instructions; and (2) instruction grounding :the same policy grounds each generated instruction, and the resulting coordinate is evaluated against o∗.

## Experiments 

Experimental Setup 

Implementation details. Model Architectures. Our pri-mary model, BiGrounder-4B, is built on the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct backbone. To evaluate generalization across model scales and backbone families, we additionally vali-date BiGrounder on Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. Following our ablation studies, we set the BERTScore threshold t to 0.85 and all center and size toler-ance thresholds to 6 pixels. 

• SFT Stage. We adopt the open-source grounding data from ScaleCUA (Liu et al. 2025c) and GroundCUA (Feizi et al. 2025), and construct the corresponding reverse coordinate →instruction data to add into training. For samples with only element descriptions, we employ Gem-ini 2.5-Flash to rewrite the descriptions into user instruc-tions. We will release the rewritten instructions together with the exact prompts used. With a total of 4,482k sam-ples, the training is conducted for 2 epochs with a global batch size of 640 and a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, aim-ing to endow the model with fundamental capabilities for both description generation and element grounding. 

• RL stage. We use approximately 63k filtered samples for the RL stage. Unlike offline augmentation with its fixed sample set, our framework samples instruction variations on-policy from the current model at a temperature of 0.9. We employ GRPO for 1 epoch. For each sample, we generate 4 instructions in the backward phase, each followed by 4 grounding rollouts. We set the training batch size to 64 and the learning rate to 5 × 10 −7.

GUI Grounding Benchmark and Performance 

To systematically evaluate GUI grounding under diverse user instructions, we conduct experiments on five bench-marks with different instruction characteristics and visual complexities: ScreenSpot-Pro (Li et al. 2025b), ScreenSpot-V2 (Wu et al. 2024), MMBench-GUI L2 (Wang et al. 2025b), UI-Vision (Nayak et al. 2025), and OSWorld-G-R (Xie et al. 2025), which cover desktop, mobile, and web interfaces from complementary perspectives. As shown in Table 1, BiGrounder-4B achieves competitive results across all five benchmarks, reaching 66.6% on the high-resolution ScreenSpot-Pro and 87.5% on MMBench-GUI L2. BiGrounder is particularly robust on benchmarks whose instructions carry complex or implicit user intent, consistent with its more stable grounding under instruction variation. 

GUI Agent Performance 

Beyond static grounding, we test whether it transfers to on-line agent tasks, where the executor must localize targets from instructions dynamically generated by a planner. All models share the same GPT-4o planner and interaction bud-get, and differences reflect the executor alone. In terms of accuracy, BiGrounder-4B reaches the highest success rate on both benchmarks, indicating that its grounding stays re-liable when queries are produced by a planner. In terms of robustness, its run-to-run std is comparable or lower ( 0.5 vs. 

0.8 on OSWorld), indicating stable behavior across the var-ied phrasings a planner produces. The gains are modest but consistent across both metrics. 

Robustness to Instruction Variation 

To quantify robustness to instruction reformulation, we build OSWorld-G-R-Variants (564 elements × 4 instruc-tions = 2 ,256 grounding queries), pairing each element’s original instruction with three variants rewritten by GPT-4o and manually verified to be semantically equivalent and unambiguous, then querying every model with all four. As shown in Table 3, BiGrounder attains the highest original-instruction accuracy and the highest Any- and All-Correct rates, while reducing the Any–All gap from 17.4 to 14.8 points compared with Qwen3-VL-4B. This 2.6-point reduc-tion indicates lower sensitivity to instruction wording and more consistent grounding across semantically equivalent expressions. ## B Dataset Construction Details 

Fig. B.1 summarizes the composition of our training cor-pus, which comprises 4,482k instruction–grounding pairs in total. The corpus is split evenly into two symmetric halves that mirror the bidirectional design of BiGrounder: a forward 

half (2,241k, 50%) that maps textual instructions to spatial targets, and a reverse half (2,241k, 50%) that generates in-structions from ground-truth coordinates. Because every re-verse sample is constructed from its corresponding forward grounding sample, the two halves share an identical inter-nal breakdown, which keeps the forward and reverse policies exposed to matched data distributions during training. Within each half, the data are drawn from two sources. The larger source, ScaleCUA (Liu et al. 2025c) (1,800k per half), spans the three grounding modalities used throughout our prompts: Action Grounding (830k, 37.0%), Grounding bbox (790k, 35.3%), and Grounding point (180k, 8.0%). The GroundCUA portion consists of Video-Audio Production 

samples and their reverse counterparts: 441k forward action-grounding pairs and 441k reverse instruction-generation pairs (19.7% of each half). The core pipeline can be trained using open-source data alone. However, since ScaleCUA’s action-grounding subset lacks bounding boxes, the cover-age of localization-based rewards during RL training is lim-ited. We therefore use Gemini-2.5-Flash only as an optional augmentation : it rewrites instructions for the samples that al-ready carry bounding-box annotations, i.e., the 790k Ground-ing bbox portion, using the dual-view refinement pipeline detailed in the “Grounding Instruction Refine” subsection below. The remaining modalities retain their original Scale-CUA/GroundCUA formatting, and the generated instructions will be released. The instruction-generation and grounding prompt tem-plates are collected in Appendix J. 

B.1 Grounding Instruction Refine 

To prepare data for the reinforcement learning stage, we design an automated instruction refinement pipeline utiliz-ing Gemini-2.5-Flash. Since the action-grounding subset of ScaleCUA does not provide bounding-box annotations, the localization-based reward has insufficient action-grounding data to draw on during RL. We therefore repurpose the 

Grounding bbox portion—which already carries bounding-box annotations—by rewriting its instructions into action-oriented commands, thereby filling this gap. We employ a dual-view prompting strategy by feeding the VLM both the global screenshot and a high-resolution crop of the target element. This design enables the model to simultaneously capture global spatial relations derived from the full view and fine-grained visual attributes extracted from the crop. By synthesizing these cues, we transform static region de-scriptions into precise action-oriented commands suitable for action grounding. For both bbox and point grounding tasks, we strictly adhere to the data formatting protocols established in ScaleCUA (Liu et al. 2025c). The refinement prompt is provided in Appendix J.3. 

## C OSWorld-G-Refine-Variants: Construction and Evaluation 

To measure how sensitive a grounding model is to the ex-act wording of an instruction, we augment OSWorld-G-Refine into OSWorld-G-Refine-Variants . This section de-tails how the paraphrase variants are generated and how the paraphrase-consistency metrics reported in the main paper are computed, so that the benchmark can be reproduced ex-actly. 

C.1 Variant Generation 

Starting from the 564 annotated elements of OSWorld-G-Refine, we keep each element’s original instruction and addi-tionally generate three target-preserving paraphrases, yield-ing 564 ×4 = 2 ,256 grounding queries ( 564 original + 1 ,692 

variants). Paraphrases are produced by prompting GPT-4o with the template provided in Appendix J.4. The template constrains every variant to (i) refer to the exact same UI element and location as the original, (ii) use different word-ing, sentence structure, or perspective, and (iii) preserve the original level of spatial/visual specificity without adding or removing critical cues. To encourage genuine diversity rather than mere synonym substitution, the prompt explicitly lists four rewriting strategies—varying spatial references, empha-sizing different visual attributes (color, shape, icon, text, po-sition), changing the interaction verb, and reordering descrip-tive clauses. The model is queried at a sampling temperature of 0.7 and must return exactly three paraphrases as a JSON array; malformed responses are re-requested for up to five at-tempts, and any element whose rewriting still fails falls back to its original instruction and is logged. ## D Reward Hacking and Semantic Noise 

A central design question in BiGrounder is how to reward the reverse policy πθ (ˆ x | s, o ∗), which generates instructions from a ground-truth target coordinate o∗. The natural choice is a semantic similarity reward such as BERTScore between the generated instruction ˆxi and the reference instruction x.However, semantic similarity alone is vulnerable to reward hacking : the reverse policy can learn to emit instructions that are lexically and semantically fluent, and therefore score high under BERTScore, yet do not actually point to the tar-get element on the screen. Such instructions inject semantic noise into the reinforcement loop, because they are treated as valid supervision for the forward grounding policy even though grounding them would lead to the wrong location. If left unchecked, this noise not only wastes the on-policy instruction budget but can actively degrade grounding, since the model is reinforced toward describing the wrong element convincingly rather than the right element accurately. 

Why BERTScore alone is insufficient. BERTScore mea-sures token-level embedding similarity between ˆxi and x

(Eqs. 2–4 in the main paper). We compute BERTScore us-ing RoBERTa-large representations from layer 17. Two fail-ure modes make it an unreliable standalone reward for GUI grounding. First, GUI screens contain many visually and functionally similar elements (e.g., repeated “star”, “book-mark”, “follow” affordances), so an instruction that de-scribes a plausible-but-wrong element can be nearly indis-tinguishable from the reference in embedding space. Sec-ond, BERTScore rewards surface fluency and paraphrasing, which the policy can exploit to maximize reward without im-proving visual disambiguation. The case study in Table D.2 makes this concrete: the distractor “Click the star to follow the repository.” attains a high BERTScore of 0.86 against the ground truth “Click star to save this page.”, yet it refers to a different action on a different element. A reward that trusts BERTScore in isolation would happily reinforce this instruction. 

Cycle-consistency as a grounding-verified gate. Bi-Grounder resolves this by never trusting the semantic score on its own. As formalized in the reverse reward (Eq. 5 of the main paper), the BERTScore of a generated instruction 12 is retained only if the instruction survives two gates simulta-neously: 

Rrev =

BERTScore(ˆ xi, x ), bi ≥ t ∧ ci = 1 ,

0, otherwise ,

where bi = FBERT (ˆ xi, x ) is the semantic score, t = 0 .85 is the semantic threshold, and ci = I[∃k : success( τi,k , o ∗)] is the cycle gate . The cycle gate is the key anti-hacking mecha-nism: for each candidate instruction ˆxi that passes the seman-tic threshold, the same policy is asked to ground it back with 

K rollouts {τi,k }Kk=1 , and ci = 1 only if at least one rollout returns to the true target (forward grounding reward ≥ 0.9). Thus a fluent instruction that cannot be grounded back to 

o∗ receives Rrev = 0 and is prevented from reinforcing the reverse policy, no matter how high its BERTScore. In the running example, “Click the star to follow the repository.” fails the cycle gate ( ci = 0 ) and its reward collapses to 0.00 ,while the correct paraphrase “Click the star to bookmark this webpage.” grounds back successfully ( ci = 1 ) and keeps its reward of 0.92 (Table D.2). 

Ground-truth fallback keeps the forward loop clean. 

Rejecting a semantically noisy instruction does not waste the sample. When a candidate fails the semantic threshold (bi < t ), BiGrounder substitutes the ground-truth instruc-tion x into that forward loop ( qi ← x, the GT fallback in Algorithm 1). Consequently, every one of the G instruction branches still contributes valid forward grounding trajecto-ries, but only semantically qualified and cycle-consistent generations are allowed to update the reverse policy. This decoupling is what lets BiGrounder harvest instruction di-versity aggressively while keeping the forward supervision signal noise-free. 

Quantifying noise removal. To verify that cycle-consistency actually suppresses semantic noise rather than merely filtering paraphrases, we manually annotate 300 re-verse instructions (273 correct, 27 incorrect) and measure how well each gating strategy separates them. As shown in Table D.1, thresholding on BERTScore alone filters only 

18 .5% of the incorrect instructions ( 5/27 ), and even an LLM judge removes only 74 .1% (20 /27 ). In contrast, the cycle-consistency gate filters 25 of 27 incorrect instructions, reach-ing 92 .6% Noise Filtering Rate (NFR) while simultaneously raising Precision to 99 .2% on the retained set. This confirms that verifying groundability, rather than semantic plausibil-ity, is the decisive signal for rejecting reward-hacking in-structions, and that the two gates are complementary: the semantic threshold discards obviously off-topic generations cheaply, while the cycle gate catches the fluent-but-wrong cases that BERTScore cannot.

## F Comparison with Related GUI Learning Methods 

Table F.1 compares BiGrounder with WinClick, UI-Ins, and 

Look Where You’re Told . Our contribution is an on-policy bidirectional RL framework for the many-to-one instruction– element mapping problem, connecting instruction explo-ration with grounding feedback. 

Relation to prior methods. WinClick (Hui et al. 2025) learns forward grounding and reverse description prediction through supervised losses. UI-Ins (Chen et al. 2025) uses instruction rewriting as intermediate reasoning with SFT and GRPO; its online reasoning differs from our indepen-dent, target-conditioned reverse task. Look Where You’re Told (Kim et al. 2026) reconstructs instructions from at-tended visual information and enforces attention consistency, whereas BiGrounder verifies whether generated instructions ground back to the annotated target. 

Coupled on-policy reinforcement. ScaleCUA (Liu et al. 2025c) incorporates GUI-understanding data, including ele-ment appearance, functionality, and layout descriptions, into SFT alongside grounding supervision, motivating the use of interface semantics to support localization. BiGrounder ex-tends this principle from SFT to RL: coordinate-conditioned reverse generation continues to train the model to describe GUI elements in language while the forward policy learns grounding. Joint reverse updates help preserve this descrip-tive capability during grounding optimization, sustaining the source of on-policy instructions rather than leaving it without direct reinforcement. The current policy samples instructions and grounds them back to the target; a positive reverse reward requires both semantic qualification and grounding success. Table E.2 demonstrates the grounding benefit: adding reverse updates to online generation raises Original accuracy from 68.8 to 69.5 and All accuracy from 60.1 to 60.5, while reduc-ing Gap from 15.1 to 14.8 points. On-policy exploration and continued GUI understanding thus reinforce one another, us-ing existing annotations and retaining standard forward-only inference.

I.4 Connection to GRPO and Bidirectional Updates 

In language-model GRPO, responses are sampled from a recent policy snapshot, rewarded, and used in a clipped, importance-ratio update before the sampling policy is refreshed (Guo et al. 2025). Fixed question prompts are compatible with on-policy response sampling. Accordingly, a fixed instruction library does 

not by itself make the forward grounding trajectories off-policy. BiGrounder adds a second policy-controlled trajectory: the instruction itself is the output of the reverse policy. Scope of the analysis. The propositions analyze expected-reward policy gradients with trajectory-independent baselines and independent rollouts. GRPO additionally uses sample-dependent group normalization, clipping, KL regularization, and token/sequence averaging. Its normalized gradient samples are dependent, so these results do not establish unbiasedness or variance ordering for full GRPO updates with different group sizes (16 versus 4). They explain sampling and distribution track-ing; Appendix E evaluates the coupled algorithm empirically.

21 J.5 Agentic Evaluation: Planner Prompt 

In the interactive agentic setting on Android, BiGrounder serves as the downstream grounding/action model inside a two-stage planner– executor loop. At each step, a planner VLM analyzes the current screenshot, the user’s overall task, and the interaction history, and emits the next intended operation as structured natural language—never as executable code, JSON actions, screen coordinates, or UI element indexes. The downstream grounding/action model—BiGrounder, driven by the grounding prompt in Appendix J.2—consumes this intended operation and its target description and converts them into a concrete executable action. This decoupling isolates high-level decision making (the planner) from pixel-level localization and low-level execution (the actor), letting us evaluate BiGrounder’s grounding ability independently of the planner’s reasoning. The planner’s action_type_hint , target_description , and value_hint mirror the (operation, element, value) decomposition popularized by SeeAct (Zheng et al. 2024), cleanly separating what to do , where to do it , and with what argument . The planner is driven by two prompts: a fixed system prompt that defines its role, action-type hints, planning rules, and output format; and a user prompt filled in at each step with the current task and operation history (placeholders {instruction} and {actions} 

are substituted at run time). Both are shown below.

Do not repeat the same failed operation; if the previous operation did not work, or the agent appears stuck in a loop, switch to a different approach. 

Completion: Do not plan TERMINATE_SUCCESS unless the screenshot or history clearly proves the task is complete. If the user asks a question and the answer is known, plan ANSWER first; the following step should usually be TERMINATE_SUCCESS . If the task is impossible because of a missing app, missing permission, unavailable information, blocked UI, or an unsupported operation, plan TERMINATE_FAILURE .

Navigation: If the current screen is irrelevant, plan to open the relevant app, navigate back, or return home; if the task requires entering a specific app, prefer OPEN_APP . If the target item is not visible, plan to scroll, search, or navigate to reveal it. 

Text input: If text needs to be entered, plan to focus the correct field and input the exact text; if the field is already focused, plan direct text input. If text was just entered, check whether confirmation is needed (e.g., pressing Enter or tapping Search, OK, Save, Send, or Done). If the correct text is already present, do not plan to type it again. 

Scrolling: If the target content is likely below the visible area, plan to scroll down; if likely above, plan to scroll up. If the previous scroll did not help, try the opposite direction or another route. 

Safety: Avoid planning actions that delete data, purchase items, send messages, make calls, create accounts, log in, submit final forms, or expose private data unless the user’s task explicitly requires it. If such an action is required, proceed only when the current UI clearly matches the user’s intent. If risky or ambiguous, plan TERMINATE_FAILURE
